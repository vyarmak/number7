*Trading System Knowledge Base · theme T10 · equities/ETF-weighted*
*Updated: includes López de Prado (AFML bars/frac-diff/labeling), McKinney (pandas pipeline).*
*Updated (pass 3): added Coqueret & Guida (factor-data engineering), Clenow, Chan data cautions.*

**What this covers:** The full data lifecycle that feeds a trading system: data types and sources (market, fundamental, reference, alternative); how prices are adjusted for corporate actions (including **dividend-adjusted total-return** series and **historical index membership** for survivorship-free cross-sectional universes); the data-quality pitfalls that quietly invalidate research (survivorship, look-ahead, point-in-time correctness, primary-vs-consolidated and futures back-adjustment); the construction of *information-driven bars* (tick/volume/dollar/imbalance/runs bars and the volume clock); returns/stationarity transforms including **fractional differentiation** and **cross-sectional feature uniformization/ranking per date** with characteristic-persistence (Stambaugh) matching; the construction of predictive features — including the Kakushadze alpha-operator grammar as a reusable feature DSL, microstructural and entropy features, structural-break features, and the **factor-zoo redundancy/competition test** as feature selection; limit-order-book (LOB) features; labeling schemes for supervised ML — fixed-horizon, **categorical/ordinal** factor labels, and **triple-barrier + meta-labeling**; and a correct, anti-look-ahead **pandas data pipeline** (resample→OHLC, `shift()` lagging, rolling/expanding/ewm causal windows, `groupby.transform` cross-sectional z-scoring, `merge_asof` as-of joins).

**Why it matters:** "Garbage in, garbage out" — the *nature* of your inputs dictates what models are even possible (slow GDP data cannot power a fast strategy), and the most common reason a backtested edge evaporates live is a data defect, not a modeling one (Narang, p.133). A single look-ahead error produces a Type I error: believing a worthless strategy works (Narang, p.140). Feature quality dominates model choice; uninformative data actively *reduces* predictiveness (Tortoriello *QuantAssetMgmt* ch.6).

**Primary sources:** Jansen, *ML for Algo Trading*; **López de Prado, *Advances in Financial Machine Learning* (AFML) & *ML for Asset Managers*; Coqueret & Guida, *Machine Learning for Factor Investing* (cross-sectional factor-data engineering); McKinney, *Python for Data Analysis*; Hasbrouck, *Empirical Market Microstructure*; Bouchaud et al., *Trades, Quotes & Prices* (TQP); Tortoriello, *Quantitative Strategies for Achieving Alpha* / *Quant Asset Management* (PIT Compustat);** Kakushadze, *101 Formulaic Alphas* & *Risk Models*; Narang, *Inside the Black Box*; Harris, *Trading & Exchanges*; Kaufman, *Trading Systems & Methods*; Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algorithmic Trading* & *Successful Algorithmic Trading*; Guo et al., *Chinese Quant* (microstructure); Bennett, *Trading Volatility*; Hilpisch; Kaabar; Chan, *Quantitative Trading* & *Algorithmic Trading: Winning Strategies and Their Rationale*; Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*; Leshik & Cralle.

## 1. Data types & sources

**Two foundational families** (Narang, pp.135–137):
- **Price/market data** — prices, volume, timestamps, full order book, index levels. Refreshes fast (intraday) → enables faster strategies.
- **Fundamental data** — a company's financial *health*, *performance*, *worth*, and *sentiment*. Refreshes slowly (weeks/quarters) → slower strategies.

A third practical family is **reference/master data** (the security master) and a fourth is **alternative data**.

### Market / price-volume data
Core fields: open/high/low/close (OHLC), volume, VWAP, average daily dollar volume (ADDV) (151Strategies, pp.125–127). Equities/ETFs are the easiest, most common retail domain (Halls-Moore *SuccessfulAlgo*, pp.35–37).

**Feeds & APIs:** Nasdaq TotalView-ITCH binary feed (parse with Python `struct`/`namedtuple`, reconstruct the book from add/cancel/execute messages); FIX is the de-facto pre/at-trade messaging standard; AlgoSeek consolidated-SIP minute bars (NBBO-based, up to 54 fields including bid/ask and uptick/downtick volumes, dark-pool volume) (Jansen, pp.95–127). Retail/free sources: `pandas-datareader` (Tiingo, IEX, Alpha Vantage, Quandl, Fama-French, Stooq), `yfinance`, Quandl, Zipline bundles; FRED for macro; Refinitiv Eikon for professional structured + news data (Jansen p.95; Hilpisch pp.52–64; Kaabar pp.170–175). *Caveat:* the 2015-vintage Yahoo/`pandas_datareader` endpoints are now broken — treat as illustrative, substitute `yfinance` or a vendor API (Lachowicz, pp.186–187).

**Source tiers** (Narang, pp.137–138):
- **Primary** (exchanges, regulators, governments, corporations, news) — maximum control + speed, but huge integration cost.
- **Secondary** vendors (e.g. quantified financial statements).
- **Tertiary** vendors — unified, security-master-mapped, partially cleaned; convenient but add a latency layer.

**Capital gates data choices** (Chan, pp.13–15): low capital ⇒ futures/FX, intraday, small universe, *survivorship-biased* daily data; high capital ⇒ equities, large universe, *point-in-time bias-free* data plus fundamental and news databases.

### Tick vs bar — regularizing noisy ticks
Raw ticks are irregular and have non-normal returns. Aggregate up a ladder of increasing robustness (Jansen, pp.107–113):
1. **Tick bars** — raw events.
2. **Time bars** — fixed period; the simplest denoising.
3. **Volume bars** — aggregate by traded volume; handles order fragmentation.
4. **Dollar bars** — aggregate by price×volume; robust to price-level changes and splits.

Collect OHLCV + VWAP per bar. **Tick bars** (e.g. equal 100-tick blocks) handle low-overnight-volume regimes better and gave much smoother trend recognition than fixed-time bars (Kaufman, pp.1471–1473). **Bar size matters more than traders think** — trend breakouts peaked around 120-min bars; daily/720-min best after costs; tiny bars are terrible (noise + HFT zone + costs); start new strategies on larger bars (~1440-min) (Davey, pp.34–42). Mind the **uneven last bar** (energy closes 14:30 NY → a stub hourly bar) — it can break volume/volatility comparability (Kaufman, pp.1471–1473). Intraday volatility is **U-shaped** like volume; the opening-bar volatility predicts the day's (Kaufman, pp.1475–1477).

**Frequency should match the strategy** (Kaufman, pp.93–96): weekly/monthly data surfaces trends and seasonality; daily/intraday surfaces noise. Macrotrend → weekly/daily; mean-reversion/fast → hourly/15-min.

### Information-driven bars & the volume clock (López de Prado, AFML Ch.2)
Time bars **oversample low-activity periods and undersample high-activity periods**, and inherit the poor statistical properties of calendar sampling (serial correlation, heteroscedasticity, non-normal returns). López de Prado's prescription is to sample *as a function of market activity* so that each bar carries a comparable amount of information — synchronizing sampling with the **"volume clock"** rather than the wall clock. The ladder:

- **Tick bars** — sample one bar per fixed number of transactions. Returns are closer to IID-normal than time bars; but order fragmentation (one large order chopped into many small executions) inflates tick counts arbitrarily.
- **Volume bars** — sample one bar per fixed quantity of units (shares/contracts) traded. Fixes the fragmentation issue and yields still-better statistical properties; the natural choice when studying *value* or fitting market-microstructure models.
- **Dollar bars** — sample one bar per fixed *dollar* amount exchanged (price × size). Robust to large price swings, splits, and corporate actions over long samples (the number of shares for a fixed dollar amount shrinks as price rises); the number of dollar bars per day stays far more stable than tick/volume bars when a stock's price or float changes materially. **Preferred default for equities.**

**Imbalance bars (information-driven sampling).** Sample a new bar whenever accumulated *signed* order-flow imbalance exceeds an expectation, so bars are emitted when informed trading arrives:
- **Tick Imbalance Bars (TIB):** sign each tick with the tick rule `b_t = b_{t−1}` if `Δp_t = 0` else `sign(Δp_t)`; accumulate `θ_T = Σ b_t`; close a bar when `|θ_T| ≥ E_0[T]·|2P[b=1]−1|` (the expected imbalance, estimated by an EWMA of prior bars). TIBs are "buckets of equal expected information."
- **Volume/Dollar Imbalance Bars (VIB/DIB):** same idea but accumulate signed *volume* `Σ b_t·v_t` (or signed dollars), so a few large informed orders trigger a bar.
- **Runs bars (TRB/VRB/DRB):** monitor the length of *runs* of same-signed (or buy-side) ticks/volume and sample when a run's accumulated volume exceeds expectations — designed to detect sequences of one-sided sweeps and order-splitting.

Bars built this way produce features with much better statistical properties for ML (closer to stationary, IID, homoscedastic) and naturally regularize the magnitude of bar returns `|r_t|` — which matters for entropy encoding (§4) and triple-barrier labeling (§6). **Sampling features off information-driven bars (CUSUM-event sampling, §4) is the recommended front of any AFML pipeline.**

### Fundamental data
SEC EDGAR 10-K/10-Q via XBRL (GAAP taxonomy); financial-statement-and-notes datasets (SUB/TAG/NUM). Build PIT time series (e.g. trailing-12m EPS → P/E) carefully **adjusting for stock splits to align with adjusted prices** (Jansen, pp.127–135). Fundamentals refresh slowly (Narang, p.137).

**Point-in-time Compustat discipline (Tortoriello).** When testing fundamental factors, use a **point-in-time** database (e.g. PIT Compustat) so that each financial item is observed *as it would have been known on the test date*, before restatements and with the correct reporting lag — naively using the latest Compustat values backfills restated and revised figures into the past and manufactures look-ahead. If a true PIT feed is unavailable, lag each annual/quarterly item by a conservative reporting delay (e.g. fundamentals available ~3 months after fiscal-period end for annual data) before forming any ranking. This is the fundamental-data instance of the §2 point-in-time rule.

### Corporate actions & adjusted prices
**Always use adjusted close** (split/dividend-adjusted); the adjustment factor flows through to adjusted open/close (151Strategies, pp.125–127; Lachowicz pp.186–187). Adjustment auto-handles splits/dividends in derived features — e.g. normalizing OHLC by the open (H/O, L/O, C/O) is split-robust (Halls-Moore *AdvAlgo*, pp.307–319). **Track corporate actions independently** so you can rebuild the series (Narang, p.143). *Futures pitfall:* continuous/back-adjusted contracts accumulate roll differences — old prices aren't real, long-term horizontal support/resistance is wrong, and early prices can go negative (early Apple under aggressive split-adjust does the same) (Kaufman, p.242; see §2).

**Total-return (dividend-adjusted) equity data (Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*).** A momentum/trend equity system must rank and trade on **total-return series** — prices adjusted for *both* splits and dividends — because a stock paying a steady dividend will otherwise look weaker than its true holding-period return, biasing cross-sectional momentum ranks against high-yield names. Using raw (price-only) closes silently penalizes dividend payers and distorts any relative-strength ranking. Equity total-return adjustment is the dividend-bearing analogue of the futures back-adjustment problem: the same caveat applies that historical *levels* on a back-adjusted series are not tradeable prices.

**Primary vs consolidated, and back-adjustment cautions (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*).** Two data-vendor choices materially change a backtest: (1) **primary-exchange vs consolidated** prices. A *consolidated* close (e.g. the SIP/last-sale across all venues) can differ from the **primary-exchange** official close, and the high/low of a consolidated series may include prints from venues you can't actually trade — so backtest fills computed off consolidated extremes are optimistic. Decide which feed your live execution will hit and back-test on the matching one. (2) **Futures back-adjustment method**: returns and indicator values depend on *how* the continuous contract is stitched, and a back-adjusted series can carry negative or non-tradeable historical prices (reinforces the Kaufman/Davey futures cautions in §2). Prefer computing P&L on per-contract returns and rolling explicitly rather than trusting back-adjusted *levels*.

### Reference / security master
A **security master** maps ticker/SEDOL/ISIN/CUSIP to one internal ID (Narang, pp.137–138). A minimal MySQL securities-master schema: a `symbol` table (ticker, instrument, name, sector, currency) and a `daily_price` table (data_vendor_id, symbol_id, price_date, OHLC + adj_close as `decimal(19,4)` for precision, volume as `bigint`) (Halls-Moore *SuccessfulAlgo*, pp.52–54). Local DB I/O vastly beats internet for repeated backtests.

### Alternative data
Sources (Jansen, pp.142–157): **individuals** (social/search/reviews — Twitter, StockTwits); **business processes** (credit-card transactions with ~10yr near-real-time history, supply-chain, L2/L3 order book); **sensors/satellites/geolocation** (3–4yr history). **Evaluation criteria:** signal quality (alpha content, orthogonality — correlation to known risk premia under ~5% is valuable — and strategy capacity), data quality (history length, reliability), legal/reputational risk (**MNPI** = insider-trading exposure; **PII** = GDPR), exclusivity (harder-to-process data resists signal decay), latency, format (Jansen, pp.146–152). Web-scraping toolkit: Requests + BeautifulSoup (static HTML), Selenium (JS-loaded), Scrapy + Splash (scalable crawling) (Jansen, pp.157–166). **COT data** (CFTC, weekly, ~1 week stale): net hedger vs fund positioning; `Net COT = Net funds − Net hedgers` is stationary and highly correlated to the underlying (CAD net COT vs CADUSD r=0.66); an LSTM on COT beat price models (~60.9% directional) because COT is less noisy (Kaabar, pp.291–303; Bernstein pp.133–135). Prefer features with a **causal/structural story** — instance-specific signals with a known channel beat static historical averages (AutonomousBidding, pp.76–80). **Alt data is framed as the single biggest opportunity** (NLP/sentiment, satellite, ESG-but-sparse); for frequency-mismatched series **nowcast** via Gibbs sampler / MIDAS / Kalman filter (Tortoriello *QuantAssetMgmt* ch.7, ch.10).

**Sentiment is domain-specific.** Comparing three sentiment pipelines on ~82k mega-cap headlines (2011–2018) — generic TextBlob (trained on movie reviews, poor on finance), a supervised classifier + LSTM on labeled news, and a **finance-specific lexicon (VADER + a stock-market lexicon)** — the domain lexicon won (highest, most intuitive return correlation across tickers) and drove a profitable backtrader strategy across stocks and sub-periods. Takeaway: *domain-specific vocabulary is the key to financial sentiment* (MLDSBlueprints ch.10).

## 2. Data quality & PITFALLS

This is the highest-leverage section: most "edges" die here.

- **Survivorship bias.** Free/cheap databases (Yahoo, HQuotes/CSI) silently drop delisted names → backtests overstate returns. CRSP is bias-free but expensive (Chan, p.37). Build your own PIT database by saving your universe's prices going forward (Chan, p.40). **Historical index-membership is the equities-specific form (Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*).** A cross-sectional S&P 500 / index strategy must select each rebalance from the **index constituents *as of that date*** using a survivorship-bias-free *historical membership* table — not today's membership list. Backtesting on the current constituent set quietly excludes every company that was deleted (bankruptcies, acquisitions, relegations) and only ever ranks the survivors, inflating returns; the delisted names must remain investable in the backtest right up to their delisting. Pair historical membership with total-return prices (above) so both the universe *and* the ranking signal are bias-free.
- **Look-ahead bias / point-in-time (as-of) correctness — the most insidious defect** (Narang, p.140; Jansen pp.875, 1006; Tortoriello PIT note §1). A 10-Q's numbers are *as-of* quarter-end but *released* weeks later; macro data is revised; global market close-times mismatch. Fix by recording the **actual availability date** and lagging data accordingly; live trading has no look-ahead so neither should research. Third-party timestamps need adjustment to reflect *when info was actually available* (the "SEC filing released after-hours" assumption is flagged as a likely error). **Always shift HFT/microstructure features to prevent lookahead** (Jansen, pp.597–600). In code, lagging is `df.shift(1)` *before* any join to the label (see §7).
- **Restatements & revisions.** Fundamentals get restated; macro series get revised. Use vendor PIT snapshots (PIT Compustat), not the latest values (Tortoriello).
- **Splits/dividends adjustment.** See §1 — re-derive features on adjusted series and track actions independently (Narang, p.143).
- **Bad ticks / incorrect values.** Decimal/pence errors and bad prints → use **spike filters**, but real spikes happen, so alert a human or cross-check two vendors rather than auto-deleting (Narang, pp.139–144). Distinguish a true zero from a blank; carry last price or interpolate using neighbors + related instruments. Volume itself is erratic (±50% day-to-day) — smooth first, judge low volume by clusters not single days (Kaufman, p.720).
- **Volume isn't comparable across venues.** Dealer-intermediated markets double/triple-count (each dealer leg adds volume); Nasdaq counts all regulated volume, NYSE only what crosses its system. Normalize before using volume as a feature (Harris, p.48).
- **Primary vs consolidated prices (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*).** A **consolidated** OHLC (last-sale across all venues / SIP) is not the same as the **primary-exchange** official OHLC: consolidated highs/lows can include prints from venues you cannot route to, so backtest entries/exits keyed off those extremes over-fill. Match the price feed to where live orders actually execute, and prefer the primary-exchange close for marking. (Equity analogue of the back-adjusted-futures level problem; see §1.)
- **Alignment / timezones / asynchronicity.** Global close-time mismatch is a classic look-ahead vector (Narang, p.140). Under asynchronous trading, correlations vanish as the sampling interval shrinks — the **Epps effect** (Guo et al., pp.107–116). Naive **realized variance** is *inconsistent* under microstructure noise: `RV(n)/n → 2vε`, bias grows linearly in sample size (Guo et al., pp.99–101); use noise-robust estimators (two-time-scales/TSRV, realized kernels, pre-averaging, QMLE) and Fourier/Hayashi-Yoshida for covariation. As-of joins across asynchronous series should use `pd.merge_asof` (§7), never a naive equality merge.
- **Sample-window sensitivity.** Truncating the S&P 500 to post-2013 (excluding 2008 vol) makes ARMA fits pass Ljung-Box that fail on full history — **windowing changes apparent stationarity** (Halls-Moore *AdvAlgo*, p.143). Volatility/std-dev estimates need lots of data across regimes; post-2010-only would miss 2008 and understate risk (Kaufman, p.1571). Use as much history as possible (≈10 yrs for daily/swing; ~5 yrs ok intraday), or **≥30–100 trades per rule/parameter** (Davey *Davey2* ch.11).
- **Continuous-futures distortion.** Back-adjusted series distort percentages (wheat $1.72 cash vs $12.45 adjusted in 1965) — use price *differences*, not returns, on them (Kaufman, p.696). Unadjusted contracts create phantom roll gaps/profits; **back-adjusted contracts can go negative and break any indicator that multiplies or divides price** (ratios, % change) because the adjustment shifts historical ratios at every roll, so back-test signals won't match live (Davey *Davey2* ch.11). Stitching methods: Panama/back-adjust (trend bias, can go negative), proportional (preserves % returns, breaks levels), and **rollover/perpetual** linear-weighted blend over N days (best for backtesting) (Halls-Moore *SuccessfulAlgo*, pp.74–77).
- **Forex data is broker-specific.** Decentralized FX → each broker streams its own data; back-test feeds are usually the *bid* stream, so limit/stop fills that the *ask* never reached are **phantom fills that only flatter winners**. Use market orders only for FX to keep back-test and live fills aligned (Davey *Davey2* ch.11).
- **Day-session vs 24-hour data.** Gaps and some indicators require pit/RTH data only, never 24-hour (Bernstein, p.99). Indicator values are platform-dependent (Stochastic especially) — fix one calculation engine for reproducible triggers (Bernstein, p.47).

## 3. Returns & transforms

### Returns: log vs simple
Convert non-stationary prices to (approximately) stationary returns by taking **log returns** (`diff(log(Close))`) — comparable across assets and the basis for covariance matrices (Halls-Moore *AdvAlgo*, p.107). Log-difference = returns; use the backward-shift operator `B` (`Bxₜ=xₜ₋₁`) and difference operator `∇xₜ=(1−B)xₜ` (Halls-Moore *AdvAlgo*, p.88). **Exception:** on back-adjusted/continuous futures use differences, not returns (Kaufman, p.696).

### Stationarity
Stationarity is the precondition for most models (Halls-Moore *AdvAlgo*, p.81). Distinguish stationary-in-mean vs in-variance; **second-order stationary** = autocovariance depends only on lag k. Diagnose with the **ACF/correlogram** (`acf()`): linear decay ⇒ trend; fixed-period peaks ⇒ seasonality; flat/near-zero ⇒ white-noise residuals = good fit (Halls-Moore *AdvAlgo*, pp.83–84). Test formally with **ADF** (null = unit root/nonstationary; want p<0.05) and **KPSS** (null = stationary; detects stationarity in trending series) (Kaabar, pp.70–76). Transform toolkit: log, deflate, detrend (regress on time index or subtract a centered MA), first/seasonal differencing (Jansen pp.404–408; Kaufman pp.844, 948).

### Fractional differentiation (López de Prado, AFML Ch.5) — stationary-yet-memory features
**The dilemma:** prices have memory but are non-stationary; returns (integer `d=1` differencing) are stationary but memory-*less*. Standard practice over-differentiates and erases the very memory that gives predictive power. Supervised learners need stationary features, but **stationarity is necessary, not sufficient**. Goal: the *minimum* amount of differentiation that achieves stationarity while preserving maximum memory (AFML Ch.5.1–5.2).

**Method.** Generalize the backshift difference `(1−B)^d` to *real* `d` via the binomial series. The fractionally-differentiated value is a weighted dot product of the price history with weights `ω_k` generated iteratively (AFML Ch.5.4, Snippet 5.1):

> `ω_0 = 1`, `ω_k = −ω_{k−1} · (d − k + 1) / k`

- For integer `d` the weight series truncates: `d=1 ⇒ ω = {1, −1, 0, 0, …}` = ordinary returns.
- For non-integer `d ∈ (0,1)`, every weight after `ω_0` is negative and in `(−1, 0)`; they alternate/decay so the series stays stationary while retaining long memory (AFML Ch.5.4.1–5.4.3).

**Fixed-Width Window FracDiff (FFD)** — the book's key contribution: drop weights once `|ω_k| < τ` and apply the *same fixed weight vector* to every estimate (Snippet 5.3). This removes the negative drift that an expanding window introduces, yielding a stationary, driftless "level + noise" series (it is non-Gaussian: skew and excess kurtosis are the signature of *retained memory*). **Prefer FFD over the expanding-window version** (Snippet 5.2).

**Find the minimum `d*`** that passes the ADF test (Snippet 5.4). Empirical result on E-mini S&P 500 log-prices: ADF crosses the 95% critical value (`−2.8623`) near **`d ≈ 0.35`**, where correlation to the original series is still **0.995** — versus returns (`d=1`), whose correlation to the original is only **0.03**. Across the 87 most liquid futures, **stationarity is reached with `d < 0.6` in every case** (often `d ≪ 1`; orange juice / live cattle needed essentially none). Integer `d=1` is gross over-differentiation (AFML Ch.5.6, Table 5.1).

**Recommended feature recipe** (AFML Ch.5.7): (1) cumulative-sum the series so some integration order is guaranteed; (2) compute `FFD(d)` over `d ∈ [0,1]`; (3) pick the minimum `d` where the ADF p-value `< 5%`; (4) use `FFD(d)` as the predictive feature. Beats Box-Jenkins (memory-less returns) and Engle-Granger cointegration (few variables, unstable cointegrating vectors). Tooling shortcut: `pip install fracdiff` for the basic fractional difference (Kaabar, pp.249–253; Jansen p.404); for FFD with the fixed-width-window trick, implement Snippet 5.3 directly.

### Winsorizing & outliers
**Winsorize** before variance-sensitive steps like PCA; drop assets/days with <95% data coverage, impute the rest with the daily cross-sectional mean (Jansen, pp.626–627). Flag outliers via **z-score**, dropping |z|>3 (Tchebysheff) (Leshik, pp.65–66). Spike-filter bad prints but keep a human in the loop (Narang, p.140). **Caution:** in classification, outliers are *often the valuable minority class* — don't discard reflexively; prefer ROC/PR/AUC over accuracy and calibrate probabilities (isotonic/Platt) (Tortoriello *QuantAssetMgmt* ch.6). **Factor-panel outlier handling (Coqueret & Guida, ch.4.3):** either hard thresholds `[μ − mσ, μ + mσ]` with `m ∈ {3, 5, 10}`, or quantile-based **winsorization** (clamp values below the `q`-quantile and above the `1−q`-quantile to those bounds) — compute these bounds **per date, cross-sectionally**, consistent with the uniformization rule above.

### Missing-data look-ahead traps in factor panels (Coqueret & Guida, ch.4.2)
Imputing missing characteristics is a prime source of **forward-looking leakage** — the imputation method, not just the value, can import the future. Concrete rules:
- **Persistent series (e.g. market cap): carry *backward in time*** — `x_t ← x_{t−1}` (a backward-looking fill). This is `ffill`, never `bfill` (§7); a backfill would import a value not yet known.
- **Never linearly interpolate accounting / quarterly data.** Interpolation between two reported quarters bleeds the *later* (not-yet-released) figure into the gap — a textbook look-ahead leak. Lag each fundamental by its real reporting delay instead (ties to the §1/§2 point-in-time rule).
- **Missing *returns*: set to 0** (≈ the cross-sectional median) rather than carrying the last return forward — a stale carried return is spurious signal.
- **Cross-sectional median imputation** is non-forward-looking (it uses only same-date peers) but creates a **distribution atom** (a spike of identical values) the model may latch onto — use deliberately.
- **Quarterly dividends are a trap:** a missing dividend may genuinely mean "no dividend paid" rather than "data glitch" — don't blindly impute; the absence is itself information (compare the "flag *why* a value is missing" point in §7).
- **Record every design choice.** Data-prep scripts are reused constantly across studies, so freeze and document each imputation/scaling decision (chain-of-custody, echoing Tortoriello §4).

### Normalization / standardization
Standardize = subtract mean, divide by σ (Leshik, p.63). For unbounded indicators across markets, normalize via %MV (volume × Δprice / price[n]) or TRMV (÷ ATR), or convert to a raw stochastic (Kaufman, pp.716, 744). Use **ddof=1** for sample variance (N−1) vs ddof=0 for population; for asset×time matrices, `axis=0` is down columns, `axis=1` across rows (Lachowicz, pp.229–230). In walk-forward, **re-fit standardization periodically** because feature distributions drift; the toolkit also includes Box-Cox / Yeo-Johnson power transforms and ranking/binning (binning especially helps tree models) (Tortoriello *QuantAssetMgmt* ch.6).

### Cross-sectional ranking & neutralization
For equities/ETFs the workhorse transforms are **cross-sectional**: rank returns/features across the universe each day (Jansen, pp.498–499), and **neutralize** features to industry/sector or risk factors (see the `indneutralize` operator in §4 and Kakushadze's risk-model factors). The pandas idiom for per-date cross-sectional z-scoring is `df.groupby('date')['feat'].transform(lambda s: (s - s.mean())/s.std())` (§7). Categorical encoding: `pd.get_dummies(..., drop_first=True)` to dodge the dummy trap; for tree models prefer native integer-factorize handling (LightGBM/CatBoost) over one-hot (Jansen, pp.325–326, 534). Richer encodings when needed: effect coding, hashing-trick, target/leave-one-out encoding (Tortoriello *QuantAssetMgmt* ch.6).

### Cross-sectional feature uniformization — scale *per date*, never across time (Coqueret & Guida, *ML for Factor Investing*, ch.4.4.2)
**The single most important rule in factor-data engineering: scaling/normalization must be done separately for each date and each feature, cross-sectionally — scaling across dates is proscribed.** The factor panel is a stack of `(date, firm)` instances (one instance per couple; with no gaps `I = T×N`, ch.1.1–1.2). The aim is that every rebalancing date carries **comparable, cross-section-informative predictors** so the model sees a stable feature distribution at each step regardless of the era. Three admissible per-date transforms (ch.4.4.2):
- **Standardization** — per-date demean ÷ per-date σ (cross-sectional z-score; the §4 `rank`/z idiom).
- **Min-max** to `[0,1]` or `[−1,1]` per date.
- **Uniformization (the book's preferred choice)** — `x̃_i = F_x(x_i)`, i.e. map each feature to its *empirical CDF* so the cross-section becomes a **rank → uniform on `[0,1]`** (Kelly et al. subtract 0.5 → `[−0.5, 0.5]`). This is exactly the cross-sectional `rank(pct=True)` of the Kakushadze DSL (§4), made the default for *every* characteristic.

**Why time-scaling is forbidden:** many characteristics trend over the sample (market cap drifts up over decades), so normalizing a feature over its *whole-history* distribution would dilute the cross-sectional signal that actually drives factor returns and smuggle a level trend into the feature. Uniformizing per date removes scale/units and makes features comparable **across time** precisely *because* each date is ranked within itself. In pandas this is `df.groupby('date')['feat'].rank(pct=True)` (§7) — never a global `StandardScaler` fit across all rows.

### Characteristic persistence & the Stambaugh-bias caveat (Coqueret & Guida, ch.4.5.5, 4.6)
Match the **autocorrelation of the label `y` to that of the features `x`.** Monthly forward returns are only weakly autocorrelated, whereas firm characteristics (value, size, quality) are **highly persistent** — when a slowly-moving feature is regressed on a noisy near-IID label, the model arbitrages the mismatch noisily and the estimates inherit a **predictive-regression / Stambaugh (1999) bias**. Two fixes:
- **Lengthen the label horizon** (annual or biennial forward returns) so the label inherits autocorrelation and matches the persistent features.
- **Difference the features** `Δx_{t,n}^k = x_{t,n}^k − x_{t−1,n}^k` to strip out their persistence (often also more economically meaningful — a *change* in quality/valuation rather than its level).

**Smooth-label + smooth-feature is the most robust pairing.** (This is the factor-panel counterpart of the §3 stationarity discipline and the §1 fractional-differentiation memory/stationarity trade-off — here applied to the persistence *mismatch* between feature and label rather than to a single series.)

### Denoising covariance/correlation matrices (López de Prado, *ML for Asset Managers*)
Empirical covariance/correlation matrices are everywhere (regressions, risk, optimization, clustering, dimension reduction) but are **noisy and ill-conditioned**: few independent observations estimate many parameters, so a small determinant magnifies estimation error on inversion. *Assume all matrices are denoised before use* (MLAssetManagers p.24).

- **Marčenko–Pastur theorem (p.24).** For an IID random matrix `X` of size `T×N` (zero mean, variance `σ²`), the eigenvalues of `C = T⁻¹XᵀX` converge to the MP PDF on support `[λ₋, λ₊]` with `λ₊ = σ²(1 + √(N/T))²` and `λ₋ = σ²(1 − √(N/T))²`. Eigenvalues inside `[λ₋, λ₊]` are consistent with **noise**; those above `λ₊` carry **signal**. Fit the MP PDF to the empirical eigenvalue density via a Gaussian **kernel density estimator** (the lone ML piece) and minimize SSE to recover the implied `σ²`, which sets the cutoff `λ₊` adjusted for real factors (p.26).
- **Signal-to-noise (p.28).** On a stylized noisy matrix the fit recovers `σ² ≈ 0.6768` → only ~32% of variance is signal, the rest arbitrage-suppressed noise. MP/RMT denoising is preferred over (a) the fixed-variance threshold method and (b) Ledoit–Wolf shrinkage, both of which dilute signal.
- **Tuning (p.45).** Optimal KDE bandwidth and discretization binning are set by cross-validation; for entropy estimation, optimal bin counts follow the **Hacine-Gharbi** formulas (marginal and bivariate cases, the latter using the estimated correlation).

## 4. Feature engineering for trading

**Feature engineering beats raw data** — prioritize transforms grounded in risk-factor research and demand a *narrative* for why an alpha works; deflate the Sharpe for the number of trials to fight false discovery (Jansen, pp.1007, 1167). **Add redundant engineered aggregates** (differences, time-to-event, deltas vs a reference) even when derivable — they help the learner (AutonomousBidding, pp.124–125). **Wheat-from-chaff (Tortoriello *QuantAssetMgmt* ch.6):** feature quality > model choice; keep raw data as the single source of truth with a *chain of custody* — every transform destroys information, so re-add what was destroyed as an explicit feature (e.g. add size back after currency-normalizing). Selection: filters / wrappers / embedded (LASSO can zero features; ridge keeps correlated sets; ElasticNet for correlated groups); use a **shuffled/null feature as the inclusion threshold** and control multiple testing (Bonferroni / Benjamini-Hochberg). Dimension reduction: PCA/ICA/SVD, autoencoders, MRMR.

### Feature selection for factor panels — drop the redundant, keep the competitive (Coqueret & Guida, ch.4.4.1)
With dozens of overlapping characteristics (the "factor zoo"), prune *before* modeling using four concrete filters:
- **Pairwise-correlation cut:** drop features with `|corr| > ~0.7` (echoes Kaabar's "don't feed several RSIs of similar period" multicollinearity warning below).
- **Significance filter:** drop OLS-insignificant predictors (`p > 0.05`).
- **Cluster-and-keep-one:** cluster correlated features and retain a single representative per cluster.
- **Variable-importance:** keep high-importance features from a tree/random forest (catches *nonlinear* relevance a linear corr/OLS filter would miss).

**The factor-zoo redundancy / competition test (Coqueret & Guida, ch.11.1–11.3) — features and models earn their place only by telling a *different story*.** Stacking model errors into `E (I×M)`, the optimal combination weights `w* = (E'E)⁻¹1 / (1'(E'E)⁻¹1)` are **identical math to a minimum-variance portfolio**: near-collinear errors make `E'E` singular and produce wild long/short arbitrage between redundant models. The variance of an equal-weight blend is `ρσ² + σ²(1−ρ)/M` — the **`ρσ²` floor never vanishes**, so high inter-model (or inter-feature) correlation kills diversification no matter how many you add. The same logic is a *feature-selection* test: a candidate feature/factor that is ~collinear with the existing set adds a redundant column and a singular cross-product, not signal; it only earns inclusion if it competes (low correlation to incumbents). Practical evidence: their 5 base models (penalized regression, tree, RF, XGBoost, NN) were **>95% correlated**, so both linear and stacked ensembles added almost nothing. Restore diversity by training on **different data subsets** (random/deterministic splits across assets or dates) or by **macro-conditioning** — fitting a model of the *errors* on macro indicators to learn *when* to trust a forecast (their result: low term-spread + negative inflation ⇒ errors ~2× larger ⇒ stand aside). Breiman's non-negativity-constrained combination auto-selects one model per correlated cluster and yields sparse, stable weights — but **simple averaging is, as usual, hard to beat.** (Ensemble *mechanics* → file 04; this entry is the feature/model-redundancy lens.)

### Factor-feature extensions (Coqueret & Guida, ch.4.7)
Beyond the raw characteristics, the book's sanctioned feature constructions:
- **Lagging** `x̆ = x_{t−1}` to inject memory — most useful for *oscillating* features (less so for already-persistent ones; cf. persistence-matching above).
- **Ratios / products** of raw features when economically motivated (high overfitting risk — demand a narrative, per Jansen).
- **Macro conditioning** `x̆_{t,n}^k = z_t × x_{t,n}^k` (eq.4.3): multiply each characteristic by macro indicators (Gu et al. use 8 macro series → a **9× feature space**) so the model can learn macro-state-dependent factor premia. The same idea drives **dynamic thresholds**, e.g. volatility-scaled label bands `r_{t,±} = r_± · e^{±δ(VIX_t − V̄IX)}` (eq.4.4) — a VIX-conditioned version of the triple-barrier bands in §6.
- **Sample filtering (ch.4.5.4):** counter-intuitively, **keeping only the ~20% top + 20% bottom label values** (dropping the middling bulk) can *improve* out-of-sample tree performance — splits move toward distribution centers, giving more balanced clusters and more monotonic features. Don't over-filter (10% tails discards too much signal).

**Technical features** (full catalog → file 03): lagged returns, momentum (recent vs long-horizon), ATR/NATR (volatility), RSI/PPO/MACD (momentum), Bollinger ratios (mean reversion), Fama-French factor loadings, calendar dummies, cross-sectional return ranks (Jansen, pp.498–499). Moving averages are the core building block; **let ML learn candlestick patterns** rather than hand-coding them (Jansen, p.128). Explicit reusable recipes (EMA, ROC, MOM, RSI with overbought≈70 / oversold≈30, stochastic %K/%D) are given in MLDSBlueprints ch.6, with three price-model feature families: **correlated assets** (other stocks, FX, indices, rates), technical indicators, and fundamentals (ch.5). **Watch multicollinearity** — don't feed several RSIs of similar period; pick weakly-correlated indicators (Kaabar, p.304). The book's reference **Alpha Factor Library** = TA-Lib (150+ indicators) + 101 Formulaic Alphas (Jansen, pp.1019+).

**Lagged windows & labels for supervised ML.** A lagged-return feature builder produces adjusted-close % returns + N daily lags + Volume + `Direction = sign(Today)`; pull extra ~365 days history so early lags are valid; nudge zero returns to 1e-4 (Halls-Moore *AdvAlgo*, pp.251, 285). Intraday: shift to build a lookback (features) and lookforward (label) frame; directional label = `sign(Lookforward1)` (Halls-Moore *AdvAlgo*, pp.415–424). Lagging features and reframing the problem as supervised regression is the core trick to inject time-series structure (MLDSBlueprints ch.5). **Integrate data sources** — nonlinear models exploit interaction effects, so prices + macro + sentiment + alt-data yield more regime-robust predictions *if* there's enough data (Jansen, p.1006). FINMEM's framing: route each data type to a memory layer whose decay rate matches its half-life (news shallow, fundamentals deep) (LLMagents).

### The Kakushadze alpha-operator dictionary (a reusable feature DSL)

The *101 Formulaic Alphas* express signals as compositions of a small operator vocabulary over standard input fields. Treat this as a domain-specific language for cross-sectional equity features. **Input fields:** `open`, `high`, `low`, `close`, `volume`, `vwap`, `returns` (daily close-to-close), `cap` (market cap). Most operators act *time-series-wise* per stock; `rank` and `indneutralize` act *cross-sectionally* across the universe each day.

| Operator | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `rank(x)` | Cross-sectional rank of `x` across all stocks on a given day, normalized to [0,1]. |
| `delay(x, d)` | Value of `x` from `d` days ago. |
| `delta(x, d)` | `x_today − delay(x, d)` (the d-day change). |
| `correlation(x, y, d)` | Time-series Pearson correlation of `x` and `y` over the trailing `d` days. |
| `covariance(x, y, d)` | Time-series covariance of `x` and `y` over the trailing `d` days. |
| `scale(x, a=1)` | Rescale `x` so `Σ|x| = a` (default `a=1`); used for normalizing weights. |
| `decay_linear(x, d)` | Linearly weighted moving average of `x` over `d` days (weights `d, d−1, …, 1`, normalized to sum 1; more recent = heavier). |
| `ts_min(x, d)` / `ts_max(x, d)` | Trailing min / max of `x` over the last `d` days. |
| `ts_argmin(x, d)` / `ts_argmax(x, d)` | The day index (within the window) on which `x` hit its `d`-day min / max. |
| `ts_rank(x, d)` | Rank of today's `x` within its own trailing-`d`-day window. |
| `ts_sum(x, d)` / `sum(x, d)` | Trailing sum of `x` over `d` days. |
| `product(x, d)` | Trailing product of `x` over `d` days. |
| `stddev(x, d)` | Trailing standard deviation of `x` over `d` days. |
| `signedpower(x, a)` | `sign(x) · |x|^a` — preserves sign while raising magnitude to power `a`. |
| `min(x, y)` / `max(x, y)` | Element-wise min / max. |
| `abs`, `log`, `sign` | Element-wise absolute value, natural log, sign. |
| `indneutralize(x, g)` | Cross-sectionally **neutralize** `x` against grouping `g` (e.g. industry/sector/subindustry) — demean within each group so the feature carries no net group exposure. |

These compose into alphas with short holding periods (0.6–6.4 days) (Jansen, p.128). Example pattern (illustrative of the grammar): `rank(decay_linear(delta(close, 1), 7))` = cross-sectional rank of a recency-weighted 1-day price change. The `indneutralize` operator is the programmatic counterpart of the §3 neutralization step and ties to Kakushadze's *Risk Models* industry factors. Pair the DSL with cross-sectional `rank` to make features comparable across the universe and dollar-neutral by construction. In pandas, `rank` ↔ `groupby('date').rank(pct=True)`, `ts_*` ↔ causal `rolling(d)` reductions, and `delay`/`delta` ↔ `shift(d)` (§7).

### Structural-break features (López de Prado, AFML Ch.17)
Regime transitions (mean-reversion → momentum) catch participants offside and offer strong risk/reward, so **a break is itself a feature**. Four test families: CUSUM, explosiveness, right-tail unit-root, sub/super-martingale.

- **CUSUM tests (Ch.17.3).** *Brown-Durbin-Evans* on recursive-least-squares residuals: `Sₜ ~ N[0, t−k−1]` under constant `β` (sensitive to an arbitrary start point). *Chu-Stinchcombe-White* on *levels* (Ch.17.3.2) drops features, assumes no expected drift, standardizes the log-price departure (`Sₙ,ₜ ~ N[0,1]`, one-sided critical value via `b₀.₀₅ = 4.6` from Monte Carlo); scan backward-shifting reference windows and take the **supremum** to avoid an arbitrary reference level. The **symmetric CUSUM filter** is also the recommended event sampler: emit a sampling event whenever cumulative returns breach `±h`, then build labels at those events.
- **SADF — Supremum Augmented Dickey-Fuller (Ch.17.4.2):** the workhorse explosiveness / bubble feature. Fit an ADF regression at each end-point `t` over backward-expanding start points and take `SADF_t = sup{ADF_{t0,t}}` over `t0`; it spikes when prices behave like a bubble and falls when one bursts. Unlike Chow-type tests it assumes *no known number of regime switches* — robust to periodically-collapsing bubbles that fool standard unit-root tests. **Use log prices** (price levels condition the mean of returns, not their volatility — avoiding structural heteroscedasticity over multi-decade samples). Complexity `O(T²)`. Detects sell-offs too (a "bubble" can be a collapse).
- **Sub/super-martingale tests (SMT, Ch.17.4.3):** test for explosive trend (`H₀: β=0`) under polynomial / exponential / power specifications; take the sup over expanding windows of `|t-stat of β|` (absolute value catches growth *and* collapse). Penalize long windows with exponent `φ ∈ [0,1]` (`φ=0.5` balances the simple-regression case; `φ→0` favors long-run bubbles, `φ→1` is noisier/short-run) — sweep a range of `φ` to build features targeting different holding horizons.

### Entropy features (López de Prado, AFML Ch.18) — informational content of prices
Entropy measures the informational content / predictability of a price *string*. **Hypothesis to learn:** momentum bets pay more when prices carry *little* information (low entropy, compressible, "bubble-prone"); mean-reversion pays more when prices carry *much* information (high entropy). Higher entropy ⇒ lower redundancy ⇒ more efficient market (Ch.18.8.1).

- **Estimators (Ch.18.3–18.4).** *Plug-in / maximum-likelihood*: build a dictionary of length-`w` words, use empirical word frequencies for the entropy rate (need `n ≫ w`). *Lempel-Ziv*: complexity ≈ size of the LZ dictionary relative to message length (complex strings need large dictionaries). *Kontoyiannis*: use the average length of the longest matching substring within a (sliding or expanding) window and take its reciprocal as the entropy rate; balance bias/variance with window `n` where `N ≈ n + (log₂ n)²` (e.g. `N=256 ⇒ n≈198`). Caveats: defined in the limit (repeat short messages); the symmetric matching window drops the last bit on odd-length messages; analyze the *reversed* message when the tail matters.
- **Encoding schemes (Ch.18.5).** *binary* by return sign (natural for price-bar / horizontal-barrier sampling where `|rₜ|` is ~constant); *quantile* (equal observations per code — tends to *raise* entropy readings); *sigma* (fixed step `σ` per code — lower average entropy but spikes on rare codes). **Prefer encoding fractionally-differentiated series** (keeps memory). Sample with **volume/dollar bars** to regularize `|rₜ|` heteroscedasticity. Gaussian benchmark: standard-Normal entropy ≈ **1.42**, giving an entropy-implied volatility check (Ch.18.6). Entropy = log of the *effective number / diversity* of a probability list — a special case (`q→1`) of the generalized mean, linking it to portfolio-concentration measures (Ch.18.7, 18.8.3).

### Microstructural & information-toxicity features (Hasbrouck; AFML)
- **Trade-direction `q_t` (the core microstructure feature).** Sign each trade by comparing its price to the prevailing quote midpoint: above mid → buy (`+1`), below → sell (`−1`) (Lee-Ready style) (Hasbrouck p.93). Caveats: midpoint trades, stale/mis-sequenced quotes, off-quote prints — but signed `q_t` still strongly explains price dynamics. The **tick rule** is the quote-free fallback: `b_t = sign(Δp_t)`, carrying forward the prior sign on zero ticks (used to build imbalance bars in §1).
- **Signed-volume / nonlinear-impact features.** Both Kyle and Easley–O'Hara imply larger orders carry more information, so expand the trade-variable set beyond `q_t` to **`q_t·V_t`, `q_t·V_t²`, `q_t·√V_t`, `q_t·log(V_t)`** to capture a concave impact function; collect as `Q_t` and stack with `Δp_t` in a VAR (Hasbrouck p.99).
- **Kyle's λ (price impact / illiquidity).** Slope of price change on signed order flow: regress `Δp` on signed volume `q·V`; the coefficient `λ` is the depth/illiquidity feature (smaller λ ⇒ deeper, more liquid). **Amihud illiquidity** is the cheap daily proxy: `ILLIQ = |return| / dollar-volume`, averaged over a window. Both are standard cross-sectional liquidity features.
- **VPIN (Volume-synchronized Probability of Informed Trading).** Built on the volume clock: split flow into equal-*volume* buckets, estimate buy vs sell volume per bucket (bulk-volume classification), and set `VPIN = Σ|V_buy − V_sell| / Σ(V_buy + V_sell)` over a rolling number of buckets — a real-time order-flow-toxicity / adverse-selection gauge that spikes ahead of liquidity-driven dislocations (AFML; volume-clock motivation §1).
- **Variance ratios as efficiency features (Hasbrouck p.61).** `V_{M,N} = [Var(p_t − p_{t−M})/M] / [Var(p_t − p_{t−N})/N]`; `=1` under a random walk, `>1` when microstructure noise inflates short-horizon variance, decaying toward 1 as horizon lengthens.
- **Roll-model / efficient-price filtering (Hasbrouck pp.16–19, 47, 59–60, 93).** From Roll, `Δp_t` is covariance-stationary with autocovariances zero beyond lag 1, so by Wold it has an **MA(1)** representation `Δp_t = e_t + θ·e_{t−1}` (`γ_0=(1+θ²)σ_e²`, `γ_1=θσ_e²`) and an equivalent infinite **AR** form `Δp_t = θ·Δp_{t−1} − θ²·Δp_{t−2} + … + e_t` (converges if `|θ|<1`); using the lag operator, MA `= (1+θL)e_t`, AR `= (1+θL)^{−1}Δp_t`. Of the two MA solutions matching `{γ_0,γ_1}`, keep the **invertible** one (`|θ|<1`; the roots satisfy `θ_inv = 1/θ_noninv`). The long-horizon forecast `f_t = lim_{k→∞} E[p_{t+k} | history] = E[m_t | history]` is the optimal linear filter of the efficient price `m_t` and is a **martingale** (Watson / Beveridge–Nelson); in the generalized Roll model `f_t = p_t + θ_inv·e_t`, and when trades *and* quotes are observed `f_t = m_t = p_t − c·q_t` exactly. The two-sided **smoother** `E[m_t | …, p_{t+1}, p_t, p_{t−1}, …]` is a double-sided exponentially-weighted average of prices (weights sum to 1).

### Volatility-estimator features (Bennett, *Trading Volatility*)
Annualize daily vol by `×√252 ≈ 16` (weekly `×√52≈7`, monthly `×√12≈3.5`): a stock moving **1%/day → ~16% annualised vol; 2%/day → ~32%** (p.43–44). ATM option premium ≈ `0.4·σ·√T` (zero rates/divs); OTM derived via ~50% delta (p.43–44). **Historical-vol estimators** (all on log returns, assuming zero drift so variance is additive) and their efficiency vs close-to-close:

| Estimator | Inputs | Rel. efficiency | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Close-to-close | C | 1× | only reliable auction prices; **best for large samples** |
| Parkinson | H,L | 5.2× | ignores jumps → underestimates |
| Garman-Klass | OHLC | 7.4× | ignores overnight jumps |
| Rogers-Satchell | OHLC | 8× | handles drift |
| Garman-Klass Yang-Zhang ext. | OHLC | 8× | handles overnight jumps |
| Yang-Zhang | OHLC | up to 14× | handles drift+jumps; **best for small samples** |

Range estimators that ignore jumps *underestimate* vol; use **EWMA** to reduce the "vol-spike-falling-out-of-window" artifact (p.179–180). These are direct regime/volatility features and a cross-check on entropy-implied vol (§4 entropy).

**Blended EWMA + long-run volatility estimator (Carver, *Advanced Futures Trading Strategies*) — transfers verbatim to equities/ETFs.** Forecasting *volatility* is easy (vol clusters) where forecasting returns is hard, but raw recent-return vol is jumpy and high-turnover, so **exponentially weight** the returns (p.97–99). An **EWMA std with span ≈32 days** (`λ ≈ 0.06061`, half-life ≈11 days) ≈ a 22-day (1-month) simple window; research puts `λ ≈ 0.06` near-optimal for vol forecasting (Carver elsewhere calls it span ~35 — treat as a ~30–35-day EW window). The production estimator blends it with a slow long-run average:

> **`σ_blend = 0.30 × σ_long-run + 0.70 × σ_EWMA(span≈32)`** (p.99)

where `σ_long-run` is the average of the volatility estimate over *all* history. The slow term (a) corrects vol's long-run **mean-reversion** (so you don't carry an outsized position into the calm-before-the-storm) and (b) further dampens turnover. **One single vol estimate serves *all* trend/holding speeds** — the optimal look-back (~30–35d) is *independent* of holding period (p.206). This is the practical default volatility feature for sizing and for the volatility-scaled triple-barrier bands of §6.

## 5. LOB (limit-order-book) features

The **order-book snapshot is the core microstructure feature set:** ranked bids/offers with size, BBO/NBBO, last trade price+time, day high/low/volume (the Level II montage). Depth, displayed size, and spread width are the liquidity signals brokers actually read (Harris, pp.12, 73).

**LOB construction** uses two feed types (Guo et al., pp.137–138): *order-based* (richer, can attribute orders to clients, resource-heavy) vs *price-level* (snapshots on ADD/CXL/CXR events; e.g. CME). A non-optimal order-book engine materially slows execution. TAQ is the standard intraday trade+quote database for NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ (Guo et al., p.116).

**Engineered LOB / order-flow features:**
- **Order-book imbalance** — ask-vs-bid size imbalance; a primary HFT feature (Jansen, pp.597–600). Liquidity is *conditional*, varying with imbalance and context — don't assume a static spread (Harris, p.399). For **large-tick** stocks, *queue imbalance predicts direction* — small ask / large bid → price tends up; takers rush thinning queues (Bouchaud *TQP* p.67).
- **Microprice / fair value** — size-weighted mid that leans toward the heavier side of the book (the practical "fair value" between bid and ask).
- **Queue features** — per-level queue sizes maintained by the book engine (Guo et al., p.138).
- **Trade-side imbalance / money flow** = volume on upticks − volume on downticks (analogous to Lee-Ready signing) (Harris, p.423); plus tick up/down volume *shares* (Jansen, pp.597–600).
- **Tick classification** — uptick / downtick / zero-tick, sub-classed zero-uptick / zero-downtick by last different price (Harris, p.81).
- **Order-flow direction** is the dealer's primary value signal: buying pressure ⇒ value likely > price (Harris, p.288); informed-trader proxies = large/impatient/active-manager flow (Harris, pp.288–289).
- **Displayed ≠ available liquidity** — books understate true depth (hidden/iceberg, upstairs orders); estimate "size behind the quote" when predicting impact (Harris, pp.438–439). Mean volume profiles rise then decay slowly (significant deep/latent liquidity + round-number clustering), yet *snapshots* are sparse (Bouchaud *TQP* p.68).
- **Spread/liquidity predictors (cross-sectional features):** narrower spreads with high volume, large firm size, more coverage; wider with high volatility, young/growth firms, low volume, around earnings (Harris, pp.313–317).
- **Microstructure-noise model:** observed `Y = X + ε` (X = log efficient price, ε = bid-ask bounce/discreteness/async noise) — the reason naive realized variance is biased (Guo et al., pp.99–101; see §2).

**Empirical LOB stylized facts (Bouchaud et al., *Trades, Quotes & Prices*)** — calibration targets when engineering and validating LOB features:
- **Intraday shape.** LO & MO volumes follow a **J/U-shape** (busiest ~4× quietest, peaks at open/close); most stable stats restrict to ~10:30–15:00 (p.61). For large-tick stocks, volume-at-best *rises* through the day (close ≈20× open) and the **spread narrows** intraday as overnight-news adverse selection decays (p.61).
- **Spread distribution.** Large-tick: sharply peaked at `s=ϑ` (one tick), rare openings to 2–3ϑ. Small-tick: broad, up to tens of ticks, with an **exponential upper tail** (p.62). Spread sampled *just before transactions* is narrower than at random times — takers time MOs to tight spreads (p.63). Cross-section: **`⟨s⟩ ≈ 0.001 × m`** for small-tick names, floored at `ϑ=$0.01` below ~$10 (p.70).
- **Order placement / cancellation.** Same-side relative price `d` of arriving LOs peaks at the best quote but decays *slowly* into the book (older studies: power-law); small-tick books are sparse, with a secondary placement peak ~one *spread* away (the spread, not the tick, sets the gap scale) (p.65). **Cancellation rate ≈ proportional to arrival rate** (rapid place-then-cancel churn) (p.65).
- **Heavy-tailed sizes.** LO sizes, MO sizes, and best-queue volumes `Vb, Va` all have **power-law upper tails, exponent ≈ −5/2** — sizes span a very broad range, not clustered around a mean (p.66).
- **Tick-size is the master variable.** Large-tick: `⟨Vb⟩,⟨Va⟩` up to ~10% of daily volume, MOs small (<10% of queue). Small-tick: `Vb,Va ≈ 1%` of daily volume, arriving MOs consume **>half** the (small) queue (p.71). Averaged over long windows, all stats are buy/sell **symmetric** (p.59).

Additional intraday per-stock "algometrics" on tick data (Leshik, pp.81–84): **%Range = (MAX−MIN)/((MAX+MIN)/2)×100** as a cross-stock-comparable volatility proxy; crossing density (zero-line crossings = mean-reversion measure); shares/transaction; LC Roughness Index. **Efficiency Ratio** (net move ÷ Σ|individual moves|) measures *noise vs path*, not volatility — low ER ⇒ mean-reversion regime, high ER ⇒ trend regime (Kaufman, pp.99–101).

## 6. Labeling

- **Fixed-horizon labeling.** The default: label by the sign or bucket of the forward return over a fixed window — `Direction = sign(Today)` or `sign(Lookforward1)` (Halls-Moore *AdvAlgo*, pp.251, 415–424). Build the lookback (features) and lookforward (label) frames by shifting; an up/down-factor label can be formed with bitwise-& over "down" columns and bitwise-| over "up" columns (Halls-Moore *AdvAlgo*, pp.415–424). Weakness: a fixed horizon ignores the *path* and applies one threshold regardless of volatility.
- **Regression targets.** E.g. next-period return (Kaabar EURUSD weekly, pp.304–307) or rolling 10-day volatility (Kaabar Bitcoin, pp.308–316).
- **Factor-panel label choices: numeric, binary, or ordinal (Coqueret & Guida, ch.1.2, 4.5.2).** For a `(date, firm)` factor panel the label is the **forward total return** (1/3/6/12-month — note the §1 *total*-return requirement). Three encodings, matched to the model family:
  - **Numeric** forward return → regression.
  - **Binary categorical** (above/below the cross-sectional median) → classification; balanced by construction.
  - **Ordinal buy/hold/sell** (eq.4.2): `+1` if `r̂ > r_+`, `−1` if `r̂ < r_−`, else `0`, with the thresholds `r_±` chosen to **balance the three classes**; nominal (unordered) classes need **one-hot encoding** of the label.
  Longer label horizons (annual/biennial) double as the persistence-matching fix (§3) — a smooth label paired with smooth features. Thresholds can be made **volatility-conditional** (`r_{t,±} = r_± · e^{±δ(VIX_t − V̄IX)}`, §4) so the class boundaries widen in turbulent regimes — the cross-sectional analogue of the volatility-aware barriers below.
- **Triple-barrier labeling (López de Prado, AFML Ch.3).** Label each event by *which of three barriers the price touches first* over a holding horizon: an upper **profit-take** barrier, a lower **stop-loss** barrier (both set as horizontal levels at multiples of a *dynamic* volatility estimate, e.g. an EWMA of returns × a target band), and a vertical **time** barrier (max holding period). The label is `+1` if the upper barrier is hit first, `−1` if the lower is hit first, and `0` (or the sign of the realized return) if the vertical barrier is reached first. This is path-dependent and volatility-aware, exactly matching how a stop/limit position actually exits. Sample the events to label with the **CUSUM filter** (§4) rather than on every bar.
- **Meta-labeling (López de Prado, AFML Ch.3.6).** A two-model architecture: a **primary model** decides the *side* of each bet (long/short — it can be any existing strategy, even a simple one with high recall), then triple-barrier outcomes are turned into a **binary meta-label** (1 = the primary bet would have made money, 0 = it would have lost). A **secondary (meta) model** learns to predict that label and thereby **sizes the bet** (act / pass, and how large) — improving *precision* and the F1 score without touching the side decision. Meta-labeling cleanly separates "which way" from "how much," is the natural home for **bet sizing**, and lets you bolt ML risk control onto a discretionary or rules-based primary signal.
- **Sample weights & uniqueness (AFML Ch.4).** Triple-barrier labels overlap in time (concurrent events share outcomes), so observations are **not IID**; weight samples by their *uniqueness* (the inverse of label-overlap / average uniqueness) and optionally by absolute return attribution, and embargo/purge train-test splits so labels whose horizons straddle the split don't leak. (Cross-validation mechanics → file 04.)

Always lag/shift features relative to the label horizon to keep labels strictly in the future (Jansen, pp.597–600). Use a **time-series embargo/buffer ≥ the label horizon**, and **stratify** train/validation/test partitions by sector/quintile to preserve benchmark-relative exposures (Tortoriello *QuantAssetMgmt* ch.6).

## 7. The pandas data pipeline (McKinney, *Python for Data Analysis*)

McKinney's pandas vocabulary is the reference implementation of a *correct, anti-look-ahead* feature pipeline. Concrete patterns:

- **Resample → OHLC bars with explicit edge-stamping.** `df.resample('5min', label='right', closed='right').agg({'price':'ohlc', 'size':'sum'})` (or `.resample('1D').ohlc()`) builds bars; **set `label`/`closed` deliberately** so a bar is stamped at the *end* of its interval — a bar labeled with its left edge but containing data through the right edge is a classic look-ahead leak. `resample` is the time-aware groupby; downsampling aggregates, upsampling needs `asfreq`/`ffill`. (Bar construction in §1 is this operation.)
- **`shift()` is the anti-look-ahead primitive.** `df['feat_lag1'] = df['feat'].shift(1)` moves data *forward in time* so today's feature only sees values known as of the prior bar; build the supervised target with a *negative* shift on the price/return only — `y = close.pct_change().shift(-h)` — and never shift the label back into the feature window. Every Kakushadze `delay(x,d)`/`delta(x,d)` (§4) is `shift(d)`. **Lag before you join** (§2).
- **Rolling / expanding / ewm as strictly causal windows.** `df['feat'].rolling(window=20, min_periods=20).mean()` / `.std()` use only trailing data by construction (the window ends at the current row); use `expanding()` for anchored cumulative stats and `ewm(span=...)`/`ewm(halflife=...)` for exponentially-weighted (the EWMA behind volatility estimators §4 and information-bar thresholds §1). **Avoid `center=True`** in any feature — a centered window peeks into the future. Pair with `min_periods` so partial leading windows produce `NaN` rather than biased early values.
- **`groupby(...).transform(...)` for per-date cross-sectional operations.** Cross-sectional z-scoring is `df.groupby('date')['feat'].transform(lambda s: (s - s.mean()) / s.std())`; cross-sectional ranking is `df.groupby('date')['feat'].rank(pct=True)` (the `rank` operator of §4). `transform` returns a result aligned to the original index (unlike `apply`/`agg`), which is exactly what you need to write the standardized feature back column-wise without reindexing.
- **`merge_asof` for as-of joins.** Join asynchronous series (trades↔quotes, prices↔slow fundamentals, signal↔macro) with `pd.merge_asof(left, right, on='timestamp', by='symbol', direction='backward', tolerance=pd.Timedelta('1D'))` — it matches each left row to the *most recent prior* right row, which is precisely point-in-time correctness in one call (`direction='backward'` guarantees no future leakage; `tolerance` caps staleness). This is the canonical fix for the §2 alignment/PIT pitfalls (Tortoriello PIT note). Requires both frames sorted on the key.
- **Missing-data handling.** Distinguish a true zero from missing; `isna`/`notna`, `dropna(thresh=...)` to drop rows/cols below a coverage bar, and `fillna` / `ffill` (carry last) / interpolate — but **`ffill`, never `bfill`** in time series, since backfill imports future values; and **never interpolate accounting/quarterly fundamentals** (it bleeds the later, unreleased quarter into the gap — Coqueret & Guida ch.4.2, §3). Reindex to a complete trading-day calendar before filling so gaps are explicit. (Coverage-drop + cross-sectional-mean imputation echoes Jansen §3; *flag why a value is missing* — non-random absence may itself predict, e.g. a missing quarterly dividend may mean "none paid" — and impute *inside* the CV loop, MICE/KNN/RF, avoiding naive backfill, per Tortoriello *QuantAssetMgmt* ch.6 and the factor-panel rules in §3.)
- **Datetime & indexing plumbing.** A `DatetimeIndex` with `tz_localize`/`tz_convert` resolves the §2 timezone/asynchronicity hazards; `pd.to_datetime`, `pd.date_range`, `.dt` accessors, `pd.Timedelta`, and `pd.cut`/`pd.qcut` for quantile-binning features round out the toolkit; `align` and `reindex` enforce a shared index across assets before any cross-asset computation.

## 8. Practical data stack (full detail → file 11)

- **pandas is the IO spine:** `pd.read_csv(fn, index_col=0, parse_dates=True)`; export via `.to_excel`/`.to_json`; resample/rebar with `.resample(...).agg({'VALUE':'last','VOLUME':'sum'})` (Hilpisch, pp.47–64; McKinney §7).
- **Storage formats** (write-once / read-many): **HDF5** (fastest for numeric, via PyTables — near-hardware read/write; bulk-immutable vs `format='table'` appendable) and **Parquet** (best for mixed numeric+text, columnar) >> CSV; **TsTables** for chunked time-series `read_range` slices; **SQLite/SQLAlchemy** when SQL queryability matters (bigger/slower on disk) (Jansen pp.135–136; Hilpisch pp.66–76). Narang's storage options: flat files (lean, sequential), relational (flexible, slower), data cubes (instruments × attributes × date — fast but rigid) (Narang, pp.144–145).
- **Vectorization is the perf foundation:** NumPy `ndarray` delegates loops to C (~8× faster); prefer array ops / `np.where` over Python loops (Hilpisch, pp.3–4, 82–88). sklearn consumes numpy arrays, so feature matrices live as arrays (Kaabar, p.188).
- **Synthetic data for testing:** geometric Brownian motion via `np.cumsum`/`np.exp`; seed everything (`npr.seed`) for reproducibility (Hilpisch pp.78–79; Lachowicz pp.177–180).

## To validate / watch-outs
- **Point-in-time correctness is non-negotiable:** confirm every fundamental/alt-data field is stamped with its *actual availability date*, not its as-of/report date, prefer a **PIT Compustat** feed, and shift all features (`shift(1)` before joins, `merge_asof(direction='backward')` for as-of) to prevent lookahead (Narang p.140; Jansen pp.875, 597–600; Tortoriello PIT note).
- **Survivorship:** verify the historical universe includes delisted names before trusting any backtest (Chan p.37); free vendors are biased by default. For a cross-sectional/index strategy, select from **historical index membership as of each rebalance date** (survivorship-bias-free constituents), not today's list, and keep delisted names investable up to delisting (Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*).
- **Total-return data:** rank and trade equity momentum/relative-strength on **dividend-adjusted total-return** series, not price-only closes, or high-yield names are silently penalized in the ranking (Clenow).
- **Primary vs consolidated:** back-test on the price feed your live execution actually hits — consolidated highs/lows can include unroutable venue prints and over-fill; prefer the primary-exchange close for marking (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*).
- **Corporate-action integrity:** re-derive every price feature on adjusted series, track splits/dividends independently, and never compute % returns (or any ratio/×÷-price indicator) on back-adjusted futures; prefer per-contract returns + explicit rolls over trusting back-adjusted *levels* (Kaufman p.696; Davey *Davey2* ch.11; Chan *AlgoTrading*; Narang p.143).
- **Sample window:** stationarity, volatility, and correlation estimates are window-dependent — include stress regimes (e.g. 2008) or risk understating tail behavior; use ≥10 yrs daily / ≥30–100 trades per parameter (Halls-Moore *AdvAlgo* p.143; Kaufman p.1571; Davey *Davey2* ch.11).
- **Microstructure noise:** at high frequency, prefer noise-robust variance/covariance estimators (TSRV, realized kernels, Fourier/Hayashi-Yoshida); naive realized variance and shrinking sampling intervals (Epps effect) are biased (Guo et al. pp.99–116).
- **Bar construction:** prefer **information-driven bars** (volume/dollar, imbalance, runs) over time bars so each bar carries comparable information and `|r_t|` is regularized for entropy encoding and triple-barrier labeling; sample events with the CUSUM filter (López de Prado AFML Ch.2–3, 17–18).
- **Fractional differentiation:** pick the **minimum `d*`** that passes ADF (FFD, fixed-width window) rather than defaulting to returns (`d=1`) — over-differencing destroys the memory that drives prediction (López de Prado AFML Ch.5; `d*≈0.35` keeps 0.995 correlation on E-mini).
- **Labeling leakage:** triple-barrier labels overlap → weight by uniqueness and **embargo/purge** train-test splits by ≥ the label horizon; stratify partitions by sector/quintile (López de Prado AFML Ch.3–4; Tortoriello *QuantAssetMgmt* ch.6).
- **Covariance denoising:** denoise empirical covariance/correlation (Marčenko–Pastur cutoff `λ₊`, KDE-fit `σ²`) before any inversion/optimization — raw matrices are ill-conditioned and signal is often <⅓ of variance (López de Prado, *ML for Asset Managers* pp.24–28).
- **Cross-sectional scaling only:** uniformize/standardize/min-max every factor feature **per date (cross-sectionally)** — scaling across the whole time axis is proscribed and dilutes the cross-sectional signal (e.g. a trending market-cap level leaks in); prefer per-date rank→uniform (`groupby('date').rank(pct=True)`), never a global scaler fit across all rows (Coqueret & Guida ch.4.4.2).
- **Persistence matching:** check that the label's autocorrelation matches the features' — pairing weakly-autocorrelated monthly returns with highly persistent characteristics invites Stambaugh (1999) predictive-regression bias; fix by lengthening the label horizon or differencing the features (smooth-label + smooth-feature) (Coqueret & Guida ch.4.5.5–4.6).
- **Factor-panel missing-data leakage:** carry persistent series *backward in time* (`ffill`), set missing returns to 0, **never linearly interpolate accounting/quarterly fundamentals** (imports the unreleased quarter), and treat a missing quarterly dividend as possibly "none paid"; cross-sectional-median fills are safe but create a distribution atom (Coqueret & Guida ch.4.2).
- **Factor-zoo / model redundancy:** a candidate feature or model earns inclusion only if it tells a *different story* — near-collinear features/models give a singular `E'E` and an irreducible `ρσ²` variance floor, adding no diversification; cut `|corr|>0.7`, cluster-and-keep-one, and restore diversity via data-subset training or macro conditioning (Coqueret & Guida ch.4.4.1, 11.1–11.3).
- **Operator-DSL parameters:** the alpha operators' lookback windows `d` are tunable hyperparameters — guard against overfitting by limiting trials and deflating Sharpe accordingly (Jansen pp.1007, 1167); confirm `indneutralize` groupings match your risk model's industry taxonomy.
- **Causal windows only:** never use `center=True` rolling windows or `bfill` in feature construction; verify resample bars are stamped at the *closing* edge (McKinney §7).
