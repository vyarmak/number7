# 08 — Risk Management & Position Sizing
*Trading System Knowledge Base · theme T08 · equities/ETF-weighted*

*Updated: includes Carver, López de Prado, Clenow, Sinclair, Taleb, Noguer, Tsay, Davey.*

*Updated (pass 3): added Carver Advanced Futures sizing, Clenow ATR parity, Chan Kelly/stops, Natenberg option-Greeks risk.*

**What this covers:** How to decide *how much* to trade (position sizing), how to cap the damage on any single trade and over time (stops, exits, risk-of-ruin), how to measure portfolio and strategy risk (factor models, Sharpe/Sortino/Calmar, VaR, Expected Shortfall, max drawdown), and how to set hard operational limits. Formulas and typical parameter values are kept inline.

**Why it matters:** Surviving is the whole game. Long-term compounded growth requires avoiding ruin, because any nonzero ruin probability drives long-run wealth to zero (Chan QT, p.96). Most blowups (LTCM, Amaranth) come from over-leveraging a *previously good* model, not from inherently bad models (Chan QT, p.110–112). Risk is largely immutable — you can reshape it (many small vs. few large losses) but it sums to roughly the same; the job is to size and structure exposure so you never panic-sell the lows (Kaufman, p.1989–90; p.78–79). A complementary distinction runs throughout: **risk is a currency to be *spent*, not merely minimized** — risk control is the primary dial, and uncompensated/unintentional risk is wasted (Narang p.67, 76; QuantAssetMgmt ch.19). **Risk management ≠ money management:** RM sets *where* (stops/targets, the distance between entry and exit, ongoing monitoring of point-value/volatility drift); MM sets *how much* (position sizing / number of contracts) (Tomasini p.159–160, p.3–4).

## 1. Position sizing

### 1.1 Fixed-fractional (the baseline)
Risk a constant fraction of equity per trade; size the position so the stop distance equals that fixed dollar/percent risk. This lets you trade *any* setup regardless of stop width (Grimes, glossary p.176; BrokerTest p.254, 267–269). Three-step share sizing (Aziz, p.23):
1. Max $ risk for the trade (≤2%, often 1%).
2. Per-share risk = entry − stop.
3. Shares = (1) ÷ (2). *Example:* $400 risk, $1.50/share stop → 266 → round to 250 shares.

Because sizing is constant, every trade has the same dollar impact regardless of stop distance — hence **"there is no low-risk trade, only low-probability trades"**: a very tight stop is *high*-risk because its high hit-probability makes it a near-certain loss over a large sample (Grimes, p.101–102). Express P&L in **R multiples** (R = pre-defined risk; +1R = profit equal to initial risk) (Grimes, p.185). Typical caps: never risk >1–2% per trade (Aziz 2% hard cap, p.22; Hougaard 2% with stop at 2×ATR, p.149–150); Kaufman's common-sense rule is ≤5% (some say ≤3%) (p.1998). Tomasini's closing rule of thumb is stricter still: keep per-trade risk **<1% (ideally 0.5%)** of capital from the entry (p.197). Scale size up only from *accumulated profits*, not by raw share count (Kaufman p.78–79; Bellafiore: think positions as % of bankroll and grow the account, p.347–349).

**Sizing dominates outcomes.** Identical 55%-edge coin-flip trades ended at \$5,207 under 5% fixed-fraction sizing vs \$620 under \$5-flat (Sinclair); a parallel 55%-edge example ends at \$5,207 (5% fixed-fraction) (Sinclair). Avoid "feel"-based and martingale sizing — martingale injects skew, not edge (Sinclair). The same point recurs as a scalper's mantra: there are **two levers only — stop-loss and position size** — and the trader is the "Money Manager" of the account; any single trade's outcome is a random draw, so think in summed-winners-vs-summed-losers terms (LightScalp ch.7). **Asymmetric sizing rule:** scale *up* when winning; cut size hard in drawdown (down to <10% of normal) or stop and take a break — survival over recovery math, since losing 50% needs +100% to recover (LightScalp ch.7).

**Davey's fixed-fractional discipline.** N = int(*x* · Equity / LargestLoss), with `LargestLoss` taken from walk-forward history; the fraction *x* is set **not** by a rule-of-thumb 0.02 but by **Monte Carlo** — sweep *x*, plot return / return-DD / median DD / risk-of-ruin, and pick the *x* that maximizes return/DD *subject to* his DD and ruin caps. Worked example: an optimal-f (Vince) of *x*=0.32 implied 67% median DD and 21% ruin → he capped at 45% DD / 10% ruin, yielding *x*=0.175 (Davey ch.16, 20). Core sizing truths: there is no universally optimum sizing method; a scheme that fits one equity curve won't generalize; risk and reward are a team (extra reward needs extra risk; start-of-trade risk never shows on the equity curve); sizing can be *over*-optimized just like a strategy. **Losing systems can't be sized into winners; winning systems are easily sized into losers** (over-aggression); and no sizing at all is also a mistake. Develop on 1 contract then size (Davey's way), or develop *with* sizing (Unger's way — tends to produce smaller losses that size more easily) — try both (Davey ch.16). MM only works on a system with **positive expectancy**; whether profit factor is 1.5 or 1.001 matters less than that it is >1 and steady (Tomasini p.160). Expectancy `= (%win × avg win) − (%loss × avg loss)` must be positive; use it to rank/select systems (Tomasini p.29).

### 1.2 Fixed-ratio & pyramiding
Adding to positions ("compounding") (Kaufman, p.2061–69):
- **Upright pyramid** (scale-*down* adds) = mild added leverage, robust.
- **Inverted pyramid** (equal adds) = max leverage but top-heavy and fragile.
- Add **only on new-high profits** (confirmation), cap the number of legs (e.g. ≤5). Trending markets favor immediate full entry; noisy markets favor spacing the adds.

Grimes is blunter: use **proper pyramids** (start with the largest size you will ever hold at that level, so it endures end-of-trend volatility); **reverse pyramids** (1→2→4→8) are "marketing fiction" with unmanageable drawdowns (glossary p.183, p.185). **Averaging down** (adding to losers) generally *underperforms* entering full at the signal, except during sustained bear markets (Kaufman p.2061–69) — and most practical rule-sets ban it outright (see §6).

**Fixed-ratio MM (R. Jones).** Key parameter **delta** = profit-per-unit needed to add a lot; `N = 0.5·[((2N₀−1)² + 8P/delta)^0.5 + 1]`, so **lots ∝ √(account size)** — aggressive early, conservative later. On LUXOR this gave a better return/DD than fixed-fractional (delta $500 → 6.4 vs the 5% fixed-fractional's 2.8) (Tomasini p.168–172). Contrast with **fixed-fractional (R. Vince)**, where **lots ∝ account size** (linear): tying LUXOR's 0.3% stop ($600/lot at GBP=$2) to a 2% risk forces a ≥$30k (use $60k) account per lot — illustrating why small accounts can't size and why funds win (Tomasini p.164–166). **Max-drawdown MM (L. Williams):** hold ≥150% of worst-case 1-lot drawdown + margin per lot, add a lot each time equity grows by 150% of that DD — turned $120k → $137M on paper but is unrealistic (15,000 lots) and a $139M drawdown followed the peak (Tomasini p.163). Across all schemes the lesson is the same: **MM is an amplifier of gains AND losses** (Tomasini p.164–166).

### 1.3 Volatility / ATR-based sizing
Equalize risk across instruments by sizing inversely to volatility.
- **Futures (Donchian / volatility parity):** `contracts = capital / (ATR × BigPointValue)`, where `$ATR = 20-day ATR × big-point-value × FX` (Kaufman p.662, p.2028–35). Equalizes volatility risk across markets.
- **Stocks:** divide allocation by 20-day ATR **or** by inverse annualized volatility, then scale the whole portfolio to a target vol (e.g. 10%) (Kaufman p.2028–35).
- Reduce size as volatility spikes to hold risk constant (heating-oil Dec/Jan example, p.842; detrended-series spikes, p.848). This is the core of **"managing risk without stops"** — for trend systems, cut size rather than place arbitrary dollar stops, which stabilizes portfolio volatility while keeping you in the trend (p.2041–46).

**Clenow's ATR / volatility-parity sizing (the canonical trend-following formula).** `Contracts = (Equity × RiskFactor) / (ATR × PointValue)`, rounded **down** (Following ch.3):
- **ATR** = exponential MA of true range over **100 days**; `ATR × PointValue` = the expected $ move of one contract on a normal day.
- **RiskFactor = 0.002 (20 bp):** each position targets ~0.2% average daily P&L impact on the whole portfolio → equalizes *risk* contribution, not notional. Less-volatile instruments get far larger size.
- *Worked example:* $1M portfolio, 20bp = $2,000 target/position; gold ATR $10 × point value 100 = $1,000/contract → buy 2.
- Size is **fixed at entry** and not re-scaled (risk drifts up as longs win, down as shorts win — a known, explicitly acknowledged flaw).
- **Stops tie to sizing:** at 20bp/ATR-unit and a 3-unit stop, theoretical max loss per trade ≈ **60 bp** of portfolio (3 × 0.2%); whole-portfolio giveback to stops is thus bounded (~$30k on $5M) before slippage/gaps.
- **RiskFactor is the master dial:** 0.10% / 0.15% / 0.20% / 0.25% scale return AND drawdown together (0.1% ≈ BTOP-50-like low vol). Doubling the universe ~doubles risk → halve the risk factor to hold vol constant.
- **Leverage ≠ risk:** a typical $5M core portfolio ran ~11:1 notional (1,100%), ~960% of it in low-vol STIRs/rates, but cost-to-stop is equal across sectors — don't confuse big notionals with risk (Following ch.5).
- **Caveats:** volatility is non-stationary (emphasized repeatedly) — ATR is only an estimate; assume something worse than the worst historical drawdown will hit, possibly right after launch. Alternatives noted: standard-deviation-of-returns sizing, or margin-to-equity (e.g. 0.5%/position).
- **Portfolio-level weakness (Following ch.8, flagged as the strategy's biggest):** sizing is per-position and ignores cross-correlations; in single-theme regimes (long bonds / short equities / short commodities / short FX-vs-USD) you hold *one bet*, not many. Build a **log-return correlation/covariance matrix** (use multi-day returns, e.g. `ln(Pₜ/Pₜ₋₁₀)`, to fix global closing-time mismatches); graduate to **VaR** for incremental/pre-trade risk once large.

**Clenow's equities ATR risk-parity (the *Stocks on the Move* version — same idea, equities-tuned).** *"We allocate risk, not money."* Equal-dollar weighting lets the most volatile stocks dominate P&L; instead size each name so every stock has roughly equal ability to move the portfolio (Stocks on the Move ch.8):
- **Sizing formula:** `Shares = (AccountValue × RiskFactor) / ATR20`, with **RiskFactor = 0.001 (10 bp)** and a **20-day ATR** — *half* the 20 bp Clenow uses for the *futures* book (§1.3 above), reflecting equities' tighter target. *Worked example:* $100,000 × 0.001 = $100 target daily impact/stock; Monster Beverage ATR20 = 3.26 → 100/3.26 ≈ 30 shares.
- **Parameter sensitivity (ch.14):** 10 bp yields ~20–25 stocks and is the recommended sweet spot. Fewer/larger positions (50 bp → 5–6 stocks) *score higher* in simulation but carry unacceptable single-stock event risk and luck dependence; broader (5 bp → 40–50 stocks) both underperforms and breaks risk parity once positions shrink to single-share lots. Keep <10 stocks off the table for event risk; >40 starts hugging the index; **8–15 bp all fine**.
- **Position rebalancing (sizing) — every 2nd Wednesday.** Volatility is non-stationary, so a fixed share count drifts off target risk (Monster's ATR doubling would push a 10 bp position to >20 bp). Recompute target size with current ATR and account value; only trade if the deviation is meaningful (suggested **>5% difference** filter) to limit turnover; trigger an off-cycle resize on extraordinary moves. (Carver's portfolio-level *buffering*, §1.5a, is the futures analogue of this no-trade band.)
- **Diversification caveat:** equities are highly correlated, so diversification saturates fast — 20 stocks ≫ 5, but ~40 ≈ 30; a sector cap is optional (buying the top names organically produced sensible sector mixes).
- **Regime filter as the real risk control:** the index-trend filter (only buy when the index is above its long MA) is what *halves drawdown* vs the index by scaling the book to cash in bear markets — i.e. the portfolio-level risk dial sits in the regime gate, not the per-stock sizing (ch.8, and §7 below). This is the equities counterpart to "managing risk without stops."

**Critical equities caveat (price–volatility relationship):** volatility rises with price only in *absolute* terms; in *percentage* terms it is **inverse** — low-priced stocks are relatively MORE volatile (BAC more volatile at $10 than $50) (Kaufman p.1678–79). To equalize portfolio risk, **avoid stocks below ~$5–10** (high, erratic vol blows up position size); mean-reversion traders may deliberately target them. Implied (VIX) vs historic vol gave roughly the same test results, so simpler price-based sizing is fine (p.1677; p.2028–35).

### 1.4 Volatility parity / targeting at the portfolio level
Target a fixed annualized volatility of the *return series* (not price): deleverage above target (≈ taking profits), leverage below it. Typical target ~12% (futures-fund norm; Kaufman p.1698, p.2056–61), practical floor 6–8%, and ≥16% is dangerous. **Target-risk method:** set 1σ of your return series to e.g. 12% (→ 16% chance of −12%, 2.5% chance of −24%); if actual vol is higher, scale positions by `0.12 / actual_vol`. Weighting schemes ∝ `1/σᵢ` or `1/σᵢ²` recur across momentum, mean-reversion, futures and ETF strategies to avoid overweighting volatile names (151Strategies p.56–57, p.10). Vol forecasting via GARCH/EGARCH (EGARCH captures the asymmetric leverage effect) feeds both risk management and sizing (Jansen p.420–425).

**Why volatility targeting works (the cross-source rationale).** Higher near-term volatility does *not* raise near-term expected return, so holding risk constant by varying leverage tends to *improve* returns as well as stabilize risk (Ilmanen ch.19.3, 28.2.2). Pairwise vol-scaling additionally neutralizes long-short directionality. **Risk parity / monetized diversification:** a 60/40 nominal portfolio puts >95% of its risk in equities; equal-*volatility* stock/bond weighting raises the Sharpe modestly and cuts equity correlation (0.96→0.76), then leverage scales the whole thing to a target vol (Ilmanen ch.28.1.1, 28.2.1); gains grow as the stock-bond correlation falls. The **diversification return** makes this compound: `GM ≈ AM − Variance/2`, so cutting variance via diversification + rebalancing to constant weights boosts compound return without leverage; rebalancing sells winners, whereas let-it-run cap-weighted strategies suffer "covariance drag" ≈ 0 DR (Ilmanen ch.28.2.2). DR rises with more assets, higher variance, lower correlation.

### 1.5 Carver's volatility-targeting framework (forecast → instrument risk → vol target)
Carver's *Systematic Trading* separates three jobs that the rest of this file usually blurs together (ch.5):
1. **Trading rules / stop losses** — based *only* on market volatility, never account size.
2. **Volatility target** — how much cash to risk (a function of wealth + pain threshold).
3. **Position sizing** — combine the forecast, instrument risk, and the vol target.

**Forecasts — the rule interface (ch.7–8).**
- Forecasts are **continuous & scaled**, proportional to **expected risk-adjusted return (Sharpe)**, with **expected absolute value = 10** (+10 average buy, −10 average sell) and **capped at ±20** (capping at twice the mean limits any single rule's dominance; ~5% of Gaussian forecasts exceed |20|; extremes often mean-revert or hide low vol). No-short instruments → floor at 0.
- **Forecast scalar** = 10 ÷ (average absolute raw forecast), measured from a back-test *without looking at performance* (App.D).
- **Combined forecast** = weighted average of rule-variation forecasts using **forecast weights** (positive, sum to 100%; from handcrafting/bootstrapping), then × **forecast diversification multiplier (FDM)**, then re-capped at ±20.
- **FDM** restores the abs-value-10 scale lost to diversification: `FDM = 1/√(W·H·Wᵀ)` (W = forecast weights, H = correlation matrix, floor negatives at 0). Rule-of-thumb (Table 18): more assets / lower correlation → higher multiplier (e.g. 4 assets @0.5 corr → 1.27; @0.0 → 2.0). **Cap FDM at 2.5** (ch.8, App.D). This is the same diversification-multiplier logic López de Prado, Ilmanen and others invoke for sizing — here applied to *forecast* combination.

**Volatility targeting via Kelly (ch.9).**
- **Percentage volatility target** = desired annualised stdev of % returns. Set it with the **Kelly criterion**: *optimal* %vol target = expected Sharpe ratio. Use **Half-Kelly** (half the optimal) for safety (full Kelly → ~10% chance of halving capital within 10 yrs at SR 0.5) — i.e. the vol target is **½ · expected Sharpe**. Recommended targets (Table 25, skew>0): realistic SR 0.5 → **25%**, SR 0.75 → 37%, SR 1.0 → **50%** (cap). **Halve again for negative-skew** systems. Carver runs his own ~SR-1.0 futures system at only **25–37%** (vs the ~100% full-Kelly figure).
- **Cash vol target:** annualised cash vol target = `trading_capital × %vol_target`; **daily cash vol target = annual ÷ 16** (16 = √256 business days).
- **Roll up P&L:** keep the %target fixed but recompute on *current* capital (lose $2k of $100k → target becomes 30% of $98k); this compounds gains and de-risks after losses. Carver re-checks hourly (automated); recommends ≥ daily if the target is >15%.
- Never use capital you can't lose; exclude very-low-vol instruments (pegged FX) that need insane leverage and blow up; ensure no single position can wipe you out on the largest conceivable move.

**Position sizing — the formula chain (ch.10).**
1. **Block value** = cash P&L from a 1% price move of one instrument block (1 share, 1 contract, £/point).
2. **Price volatility** = expected daily stdev of % price changes (25-day MA / 36-day EWMA).
3. **Instrument currency volatility** = block value × price volatility.
4. **Instrument value volatility** = instrument currency volatility × FX rate (instrument ccy / account ccy).
5. **Volatility scalar = daily cash vol target ÷ instrument value volatility** — the position that yields target risk at a +10 forecast (don't round).
6. **Subsystem position = (forecast × volatility scalar) ÷ 10.**
   - *Worked example:* £1,000,000 annual target → £62,500 daily; WTI crude (price vol 1.33%, block value $750 → ICV $997.50 → × 0.67 FX = £668.33); vol scalar = 62,500 / 668.33 = 93.52; forecast −6 → subsystem position = (−6 × 93.52)/10 = **short 56.11 contracts**.
- **Maximum position** (for min-size checks) = `2 × vol scalar × instrument weight × IDM` (asset allocators use 1×, since the forecast is a constant +10). Aim for max ≥ 4 blocks per instrument.

This is the most fully-specified sizing pipeline in the KB; it subsumes the §1.3 ATR approach (Clenow's `RiskFactor` and Carver's `volatility scalar` are the same idea — daily cash-risk target ÷ instrument daily $-vol) and explicitly ties the *amount of risk* to the **half-Kelly vol target** of §1.6.

### 1.5a Carver's *Advanced Futures* — the compact sizing equation (same family, one line)
Carver's later *Advanced Futures Trading Strategies* collapses the §1.5 chain into a single equation and is the cleanest statement of the whole volatility-targeting family. **Do not treat this as a separate method — it is the §1.3 (Clenow) / §1.5 (Carver *Systematic Trading*) idea restated.**
- **The core position-sizing equation** (Strategy 2, p.72): number of contracts
  **`N = (Capital × τ) ÷ (Multiplier × Price × FX × σ%)`**
  where **τ = annual % risk target** and **σ% = annualised % return volatility of the instrument**. N rises with lower σ%, higher τ, more capital, lower multiplier, lower price. Identity: **leverage ratio = volatility ratio = τ ÷ σ%** for a single instrument. (Algebraically identical to Clenow's `Shares = AccountValue × RiskFactor / ATR` once ATR is expressed as a % vol and τ↔RiskFactor.)
- **Risk = standard deviation of returns** (symmetric), *not* drawdown: Carver rejects max-drawdown (one data point, length-dependent, false security) and Sortino (confounds Sharpe with skew) and measures **skew separately** (p.49–51). **Annualisation convention: 256 business days/yr → ×√256 = ×16** for both σ and Sharpe; daily price-vol form `σ_P = Price × σ% ÷ 16`, or directly the SD of daily back-adjusted price changes (works even if price < 0) (p.45, 49–50, 73).
- **Setting τ = the *most conservative* of four limits** (p.74–80): (1) margin-implied max (≈313% for S&P micro — rarely binds); (2) prudent-leverage / survive-a-crash (survive a 1987-size 27–30% day with half capital → ~27%); (3) personal/client appetite (~10–100%); (4) **optimal-given-performance = half-Kelly**. Kelly-optimal τ ≈ SR; because returns are fat-tailed and negatively skewed, use **half-Kelly: τ = SR/2** (p.79). Carver adopts a **house τ = 20%** for the whole book (p.80) — the same half-Kelly logic as §1.5/§1.6, just with the realistic-SR-≈0.4 number plugged in.
- **The blended volatility estimator** (used for σ% everywhere): not pure EWMA but a blend of **0.3 × a long-run (e.g. ~10-yr) average vol + 0.7 × a span-32 EWMA** of recent vol — anchoring the fast estimate to a slow one so a quiet patch doesn't let leverage run away (and a spike doesn't over-deleverage). This is the practical answer to the "ATR is non-stationary" caveat flagged for Clenow in §1.3.
- **Minimum capital** (indivisibility): size to hold **≥4 contracts** at entry → `MinCapital = 4 × (notional per contract) × σ% ÷ τ`; higher for big multiplier, high price/FX, high σ%, or *low* τ. **This constraint mostly dissolves for equities/ETFs** (fractional/odd-lot shares), so the equation maps cleanly onto a stock book.
- **Risk-adjusted cost = cost-as-Sharpe** = annual cost ÷ annual σ; per-trade cost = (spread + commission) ÷ notional ÷ σ%; annual cost = (rolls/yr × 2 + turnover) × cost-per-trade (p.102–104). **Trading-speed limit:** don't let costs eat >⅓ of expected pre-cost SR (for Strategy-3 SR≈0.30 → reject instruments with risk-adj cost > ~0.01 SR/trade); the limit loosens to 0.15 SR for trading-*rule* selection (p.114, 218). (Connects to the capacity/cost limits in §7 and file 09.)

**Diversification multipliers (IDM / FDM) — the same √-of-correlation correction at two levels.** Diversifying across instruments (or across rules) shrinks portfolio vol below the per-position target, so Carver multiplies positions back up by a **diversification multiplier = `1/√(W·H·Wᵀ)`** (W = weights, H = correlation matrix; floor negative correlations at 0): the **FDM** (forecast diversification multiplier) restores the abs-10 forecast scale across *rules* (already in §1.5), and the **IDM** (instrument diversification multiplier) restores the τ risk target across *instruments* — apply IDM to the position and to the max-position check (`Max position = 2 × vol scalar × instrument weight × IDM`). More instruments / lower correlation → larger multiplier; both are capped (FDM at 2.5). This is the identical √-of-correlation logic López de Prado and Ilmanen invoke for sizing — Carver simply applies it consistently at the forecast *and* instrument levels.

**Dynamic optimisation (S25) — position rounding for small accounts (Advanced Futures p.445–472).** With only $100k, the unrounded optimal S&P position might be 0.028 contracts → rounds to zero, and most of a 100-instrument portfolio vanishes. Instead, each day compute unrounded optimal **portfolio weights** w (notional ÷ capital), then find integer-contract weights w\* that **minimise the tracking-error std-dev to w**, using a correlation matrix (6 months of weekly returns) and σ (**EWMA span-32**) → covariance:
- **Greedy algorithm:** start all-zero; each pass try adding/subtracting one contract (always in the forecast's direction — no perverse hedges) per instrument, keep the single change that most cuts tracking error, repeat until no improvement — robust, fast, never sparse.
- **Cost control + buffering:** add `50 × (turnover cost in weight terms)` to the tracking error so it prefers cheap instruments and trades less; plus **portfolio-level buffering in tracking-error units** — buffer `Bσ = 0.05 × τ = 1%` at τ=20%; if the gap between current and optimised TE is < the buffer, **don't trade**, else trade only a fraction α toward the optimum. (This is the futures analogue of Clenow's >5%-deviation rebalance filter and Grinold-Kahn's no-trade band, §4.)
- **Payoff (the key small-account result):** dynamic-opt at **$500k / 100 instruments → SR 1.22 and 0.99 correlation** with the full $50M / 100-instrument "Jumbo" (SR 1.27); at **$100k → SR 1.06, corr 0.91**, holding only ~7 positions on average, versus a static 16–27-instrument selection that reaches only adjusted SR 0.73–0.94 / corr 0.50–0.78. So **~1% of the capital captures nearly all the diversification.** Extra uses: signal on ~150 instruments but trade only ~100 (transfer risk to correlated tradeable names), set per-instrument position limits, force roll-to-flat then re-decide, proxy-trade around holidays.

### 1.6 Kelly criterion & fractional Kelly
Kelly gives the growth-optimal fraction/leverage. Forms:
- **Binomial:** `f* = p − q` (e.g. p=0.55 → bet 10%) (Hilpisch p.266–268). Sinclair's equivalent binary form: `f* = (edge)/(odds) = p − q/b` (Eq 8.5, 8.20).
- **Continuous / Gaussian (single strategy):** `f* = (μ − r) / σ²` — excess return over variance; a *leverage factor* that can exceed 1 (S&P 500 → f* ≈ 4.5×) (Hilpisch p.272–274; Halls-Moore SuccessfulAlgo p.121–122: `fᵢ = μᵢ / σᵢ²` on excess returns; Chan MachineTrading p.13: `mean(excess)/var(excess)`). **Sinclair's general small-edge form is identical: `f* ≈ μ/σ²` — expected excess return over variance — usable for any trade (including option bets) given only its mean and variance** (Eq 8.14).
- **Multi-asset / multi-strategy:** optimal allocation vector **F\* = C⁻¹M**, where C = covariance matrix of strategy returns and M = vector of mean one-period simple *unlevered excess* returns. For independent strategies this reduces to `f = m/s²` per strategy (Chan QT, p.96–97).
- **Max compounded growth:** `g = r + S²/2` (S = portfolio/annualized Sharpe), with maximum levered compound growth `= S*²/2` (Chan QT p.98; MachineTrading p.13; SuccessfulAlgo p.121–122). This is the mathematical reason a higher Sharpe lets you make more money — it permits higher leverage, and it's the *levered* return that matters; buy-and-hold is NOT growth-optimal.
- **Why risk drags growth:** `g = m − s²/2` (geometric < arithmetic mean), so a true ±1% random walk loses ~0.5 bp/period — the core argument for risk management (Chan QT p.97–98; the same `GM ≈ AM − Var/2` identity drives the diversification-return argument in §1.4, Ilmanen ch.28.2.2).

**Always use a fraction of Kelly.** Full Kelly assumes Gaussian returns, constant moments, full reinvestment, and independent bets — all routinely violated. Full Kelly produces erratic equity curves and risks ruin given fat tails; **half-Kelly is standard practice** (Hilpisch p.277; Chan QT p.98; SuccessfulAlgo p.122–123; Carver ch.9 — the vol target *is* half-Kelly). Sinclair sharpens the rationale: Kelly maximizes log-wealth ⇒ asymptotically fastest growth, zero risk of ruin, but **myopic** and **brutally volatile** — `P(double before halving) = 2/3`, i.e. a **1-in-3 chance of halving first** (Eq 8.17); growth is symmetric in f about 1, so **underbetting beats overbetting** and >2× Kelly turns growth negative. **Fractional Kelly** tunes this (½-Kelly → P(double-first) ~0.74) at the cost of much longer time-to-goal; it has no utility-max justification but is a sound "poor-man's Bayesian" haircut for **estimation error**. *Parameter risk is real:* a naive 6/10 win rate **overestimates** the true p — shrink it (Bayes posterior mean `= (w+1)/(N+2)`, Eq 8.24). **Bankroll = the amount you can lose before abandoning the strategy** (not a haircut); replenishable income justifies betting larger; the "long run" can be absurdly long (decades) (Sinclair). 

**Chan's *Algorithmic Trading* sharpens the leverage mechanics (ch.8).** **Constant leverage is the central rule** — *every* optimal-leverage method requires holding leverage constant, which mechanically means **buy after gains, sell into losses** (counterintuitive, and the same procyclical behaviour Khandani-Lo identify as the contagion mechanism in the Aug-2007 quant meltdown, §4). Kelly forms are the familiar `f = m/s²` (Gaussian) and `F = C⁻¹·M` (portfolio); treat Kelly as an **upper bound** — it routinely exceeds broker limits or would bankrupt you in backtest (max-drawdown = −1) under non-Gaussian returns, so **most use half-Kelly**. Two refinements unique to this book:
- **Non-Gaussian leverage by Monte Carlo:** maximise expected growth `g(f) = ⟨log(1 + f·R)⟩` over returns drawn from a **Pearson system** fitted to the empirical mean/std/skew/kurtosis (`pearsrnd`). For the Example 5.1 strategy, Gaussian Kelly gave f=18.4, Monte Carlo gave f≈19, brute-force on historical returns gave 18.4 — close agreement; **any f > 1/|worst single-period return| → ruin** (here f>30.2), the same hard ceiling as §3.
- **Binding max-leverage concentration:** when a max-leverage cap `F_max ≪ Σ|Fᵢ|` binds, the usual *proportional* down-scaling of all Kelly weights is **sub-optimal** — better to put most/all buying power on the single highest-growth-rate strategy (Example 8.2).
- **Max-drawdown constraint is non-linear:** halving the *allowed* drawdown can require cutting leverage ~7× (on simulated returns) or ~1.5× (on historical) — simulated DD has VaR-like statistical significance but may describe a once-in-a-million-year event and misses real serial correlation; historical DD is a realistic horizon but data-starved → use a leverage *between* the two.
- **CPPI to cap drawdown:** to bound total drawdown at −D while keeping Kelly growth, trade a subaccount holding fraction D of equity at Kelly leverage f with the rest in cash; reset to D **only on new high-water marks**, never add cash on losses. Guarantees drawdown ≤ −D and gracefully winds the strategy down; deleverages faster than plain f·D; one-strategy accounts only; can't stop overnight-gap losses (buy OTM options for that).

Even half-Kelly can be too much: cap leverage by `(max tolerable one-period drawdown ÷ worst historical one-period loss)` and use the **smaller** of that and half-Kelly. S&P worst day −20.47% (Black Monday) ⇒ max leverage ≈1, while half-Kelly says 1.26 — even half-Kelly wouldn't have survived (Chan QT p.105–106; MachineTrading: a single −20% day wipes a 5× SPY position, p.13–14). Rebalance continuously (reduce size into losses, add into gains, ≥daily) and recompute F\* on a trailing window (~3–6 months for daily-holding strategies) (Chan QT p.98, 102–103; SuccessfulAlgo p.122–123). **Optimal f (Vince)** is a related per-bet fraction, possibly more robust: `optimal f = (PLR·p − (1−p))/PLR`, PLR = avg profit/avg loss (e.g. p=0.5, PLR=2 → f=0.25); Kelly itself maximizes `G(f)=p·ln(1+B·f)+(1−p)·ln(1−f)` (Kaufman p.2094–2100; Grimes p.181). Worked Kelly example: μ=7.7%, σ=12.4% → S=0.62, f≈5.01 (5×), g≈22% (SuccessfulAlgo p.121–122).

### 1.7 Other sizing patterns
- **HRP (Hierarchical Risk Parity):** cluster correlated assets, allocate inverse-variance top-down — reduces concentration and avoids unstable matrix inversion (Jansen p.642–647). López de Prado's full recipe (MLDSBlueprints ch.8, CS3): (1) hierarchical clustering (`scipy linkage`) on the return covariance/correlation matrix, (2) quasi-diagonalization (reorder so similar assets sit together), (3) recursive bisection allocating capital down the cluster tree (correlated strategies share one bucket). On S&P 500, **out-of-sample HRP Sharpe 0.836 vs Markowitz MVP 0.787** (in-sample favored MVP); HRP is more diversified, numerically stable, has better tail-risk behavior, and avoids inverting the noisy covariance matrix. Noguer notes HRP/HSP (hierarchical) are his book's clustering route; NCO (nested-clustered optimization) is López de Prado's complementary method (QuantPortfolioOpt p.43).
- **Tail-aware diversification:** **LTDC clustering** and **CVaR / tail-risk-parity** framing target downside concentration directly rather than equal-volatility budgets — relevant for skewed equity factors (low-vol, carry-like) (Jurczenko ch.9.2; QuantPortfolioOpt CVaR below).
- **Forecast-implied sizing (the mean-variance bridge):** convert a return forecast into a position via `ω = ŷ/(γσ²)` (predicted return ÷ risk-aversion × variance) — the standard ML-to-position map (Jurczenko ch.3–4); gate the SPY/IEF split off a predicted-up probability and a risk-aversion threshold (Jurczenko ch.2). Signal confidence → bet size; **meta-labeling** judges model confidence, and separating *direction* from *sizing* eases both tasks (QuantAssetMgmt ch.9).
- **Equal-weighting** diversifies away idiosyncratic/tail/bad-data blowups; the largest alpha signals are often the riskiest ("picking up nickels in front of a steamroller") (Narang p.95–96).
- **Tiering** for discretionary day trading: size each tier to risk a fixed % of the daily stop so a $0.50-risk $3 stock and a 2.5pt-risk $670 stock carry equal risk (Bellafiore p.406, 411).

## 2. Stops & exits as risk control

### 2.1 Initial / money-management stop
Set the stop **at entry**, **outside the market's noise level**, at a price where the trade is *decisively wrong* — system-derived, never "what you can afford." Too-tight stops guarantee being shaken out by normal noise (Grimes p.101–102; Bernstein p.41–42). Place a physical stop at max acceptable loss plus a "mental" stop where the entry thesis is invalidated (BrokerTest p.258). **A stop does not guarantee price:** on trigger it becomes a market order and can fill far past the level in a gap (cotton: 70¢ stop fills at 66.90¢); only an option contract guarantees a floor/ceiling (Harris p.78–79; Kaufman p.2035–46). Tight stops hit often — for a random walk, `#hits × distance ≈ constant`, so a stop must *beat random* to add value; scale risk via leverage/size, not arbitrary dollar stops (Kaufman p.2035–46).

### 2.2 Volatility-scaled & trailing stops
- **ATR stops:** `entry ∓ k·ATR`, k ≈ 3 on 20-day ATR; trigger on intraday H/L but **exit on the close** (use intraday noise for a better fill). The stop must be far enough to clear noise (Kaufman p.1693–95).
- **Trailing types:** fixed-%, volatility-based (`high − 5×10-day ATR`), or lock-to-entry. **Kase Dev-Stop** = `trade-high − DDEV`, DDEV from the STDEV of 2-bar true ranges (factors ~1, 2.06–2.25, 3.20–3.50) (Kaufman p.2035–46). Adaptive stops (support/resistance, 3×ATR, parabolic, swing points) beat fixed stops.
- **Tick-scale (HFT):** the LC Adaptive Capital Protection Stop — for a long, `SELL LIMIT = buy price − $Range` over a ~100-tick lookback (preferred), or `buy − 2×Standard Median Deviation`; designed to cut in ~40 bp. Fixed-% literature stops do *not* work for fast high-vol tick trading (Leshik p.34, p.114).

### 2.3 Time stop & profit targets
- **Time stop:** exit after N bars if the move hasn't materialized; the asymmetric-payoff school requires a trade to "work immediately" — if it goes against entry even slightly, exit fast (Hougaard: bought 50, prints 48 → stop out; ~25% hit rate, ~25:1 win/loss, paired with uncapped trailed upside, p.53, 92–95).
- **Profit targets:** `entry ± k·ATR`, typical k = 3–4 on 20-day ATR; trigger on intraday H/L. **Scale out with 3 targets** (e.g. 2.0/3.0/4.0 ATR) to average the best pick and beat data-mining (Kaufman p.2041–46). Targets suit *short-term / mean-reversion* trading (high noise reverses favorable moves); they *hurt* long-term trend by clipping the **fat tail** — if stopped or target-hit while the trend is intact, you must re-enter (Kaufman p.1693–95; p.2041–46). Scaling out + moving the stop to breakeven mechanically converts a winner into a no-lose position (TradingZone p.166; Aziz p.56).

### 2.4 MAE / MFE-driven exits (calibrating the stop from the trade distribution)
John Sweeney's excursion framework lets the data set the stop and target (Tomasini p.66–82):
- **MAE (Maximum Adverse Excursion)** = the worst open loss during a trade; cluster all trades (profit vs MAE) to find where losers stop recovering and place the **risk stop** there.
- **MFE (Maximum Favourable Excursion)** = the best open profit; reveals whether a **profit target** can help.
- Use **percentage-based** exits (adapt to market level), not fixed dollars (Tomasini p.67).
- *LUXOR result:* a 0.3% risk stop and 0.8% trailing stop (both chosen from broad NP/DD plateaus) improved return/risk; a profit target barely helped (only ~2% targets, reached by <10% of trades) because breakouts run unpredictably. Exits cut max drawdown 10%→5%, the largest losing trade ~$2,500→$810, and time-in-market 100%→~67% — making outcomes **calculable** ("between a 0.3% loss and a 1.9% win"), which is the *prerequisite* for position sizing (Tomasini p.72–73, p.83–86, Table 3.4).

### 2.5 The stops-for-mean-reversion debate (important)
Stops are **not** universally good risk management. They help *only* in a momentum/trending regime; in a mean-reverting regime they force you to exit at the worst possible time (Chan QT p.106–107). **Chan's *Algorithmic Trading* (ch.8) makes the asymmetry mechanistic:** for **momentum**, a stop-loss is *logical and natural* — a losing momentum trade means momentum has reversed, so the **continuously-updated entry signal *is* a de-facto stop**, which is exactly why momentum strategies lack mean-reversion's tail risk. For **mean reversion**, a stop *contradicts* the entry signal, and Chan "never backtested an MR strategy improved by a stop." **But** that conclusion suffers **survivorship bias** — MR series that regime-change into a trend never enter the catalog of profitable strategies in the first place — so the practical fix is to **set the MR stop *wider* than the backtest max intraday drawdown**: it never triggers historically (so it can't hurt backtest stats) yet still defends against a future regime-change black swan. Stops are useless across closed markets / liquidity withdrawal anyway (flash-crash stub quotes; Accenture filled at $0.01). Chan also notes **strategy-specific leading risk indicators** (ch.8): VIX>35 *helps* Buy-on-Gap (17.2% APR) but *kills* the FSTX opening-gap momentum strategy (APR→2.6%); others include the TED spread, HYG, MXN/EM-FX, risk-on/off ETFs, and — possibly best at short horizons — **order flow** (large negative flow in risky assets = leading risk signal); beware data-snooping since crises are rare. In a crash, prices gap and a stop merely *realizes* the catastrophic loss. Chan's heuristic: news/fundamental-driven moves → momentum (don't fight the freight train, a stop is appropriate); sudden moves with no news → liquidity event → likely mean-reverts (don't stop out). Kaufman concurs: stops help a trending system in a trending market but hurt nontrending/mean-reversion systems, and mean-reversion tolerates **only very large stops** (p.2035–46; system trade-off table p.1966–71). For mean-reversion, *profit-taking* is essential while *stops* are minimal — the mirror image of trend following. The deeper warning: adding stops/profit-taking to a "conservation of capital" trend strategy can destroy the fat-tail edge it needs to be profitable long-term (Kaufman p.605–606). Ilmanen frames the portfolio-level version: stop-losses lower expected return *unless paired with timing skill*, and diversification fails when correlations spike in crises — so good managers add stop-losses, options, and cheap tail hedges rather than relying on diversification alone (Ilmanen ch.28.2.4, 29.3.7).

## 3. Risk-of-ruin & drawdown math

**Risk of ruin (equal win/loss):** `R = ((1−A)/(1+A))^c`, where A = advantage = 2P−1 and c = capital in betting units. Greater advantage or more capital → lower ruin (60% wins: $10k → 66%, $20k → 43%) (Kaufman p.2083–90).

**Unequal wins/losses (Vince/Griffin):**
```
Z   = AvgWin%·ProbWin − AvgLoss%·ProbLoss
A   = √(AvgWin%²·ProbWin + AvgLoss%²·ProbLoss)
P   = ½(1 + Z/A)
RoR = ((1−P)/P)^(MaxRisk/A)
```
Benchmark ($10k, win $400 / loss $200, 40% win rate, 25% MaxRisk) → RoR ≈ 10%; halving capital pushes it to 32%, then 56%; lowering the average win or the MaxRisk fraction raises RoR sharply (Kaufman p.2087–89). Practical capital reality: small accounts get wiped before compounding can work — roughly $500k at 30%/yr to net ~$150k; ignore "$5k → riches" pitches (Davey p.24).

**Risk-of-ruin by Monte Carlo (Davey — the empirical route).** Rather than a closed form, resample the trade list *with replacement* into **2,500 one-year equity curves** to get a distribution of outcomes; **ruin = closed equity falling below a set quitting point** (Davey ch.7, 14). The method assumes trades are independent — **check serial/autocorrelation (Durbin-Watson) first**; if correlated, use **start-trade / moving-start analysis** (begin the equity curve at each successive trade), which preserves sequence. Four MC outputs and Davey's thresholds: **risk of ruin <10%; median max drawdown <40%; median annual return >40–50%; return/drawdown ratio >2.0** (≈ 1-yr Calmar — his single most-trusted number). Risk-of-ruin is brutal for small accounts even with a winning system: a +EV day-trader funded at $1,500 panics on every loss, while at $5,000 (only 3.3× larger) ruin is ~20× *less* likely — underfunding is "disastrous even with a winning system" (Davey ch.7; Tomasini echoes this on LUXOR — a $5,000 fixed-ratio account reaches $1.5M net but pushes worst-case 99% DD from $21k to **$446k**, p.173–174). **Account funding & quitting point (Davey ch.20):** min funding = exchange margin + quitting-point drawdown; set the quitting point by averaging (worst WF daily drawdown × 1.5) with the MC 95th-percentile drawdown, write it down, and follow it. **Traders handle "half of what they think" they can — set DD tolerance at half your gut number** (Davey ch.7). Realistic MC expectations for a *good* system: ~60% winning weeks, ~75% winning months, ~86% winning quarters, ~99% winning years — weekly/monthly noise is huge even with a real edge, and outlier analysis often shows nearly all profit comes from ~5 big days/yr, so **take every trade** (Davey ch.19).

**Drawdown analytics:**
- Max drawdown `= (cummax − equity).max()`; longest drawdown period = max time between new equity highs (Hilpisch p.95–96, 287–288).
- **Drawdown ratio** `DR = AROR / DP`, DP = std-dev of all daily drawdowns; 1σ → ~16% chance of a larger DD over n days (Kaufman p.2003–17). Downside-risk measures: semivariance, or the std-dev of daily *drawdowns* `(peak − current)/peak` (Kaufman p.167–68); maxDD captures fat-tail/asymmetric risk better than vol-based metrics (MachineTrading p.13–14).
- **Optimal f / Kelly over-betting** → ruin; under-betting cuts profit geometrically (Kaufman p.2094–2100). The Theory-of-Runs note (p.2083–2112): market price runs are *not* random — every market except gold had runs ≥13 with a strong upward bias; a Martingale-with-trend (buy after a new trend signal then a 2–3 lower-close pullback, doubling capped at 5×) exploits this, but a naive Martingale's run-of-9 ruins it.

## 4. Portfolio-level risk

**Risk = intentional selection and SIZING of exposures, not just loss avoidance** (Narang p.67, 76; QuantAssetMgmt ch.19 frames it as "risk is a currency to be *spent*, not minimized"). Distinguish *alpha exposures* (deliberately accepted, compensated long-run) from *risk exposures* (unintended byproducts you can't forecast — e.g. a sector bet riding along a value strategy); always **eliminate** unintentional exposures (e.g. long CVX / short XOM removes market + sector risk) or **cap** them (Narang p.67–72). Two levers to limit size: **hard constraints** vs **penalty functions** (cost to exceed rises faster than linearly) (Narang p.69–71). QuantAssetMgmt's parallel taxonomy: **compensated** risk (predictable vol/credit/inflation, bounded illiquidity) vs **uncompensated** (tail wars, style drift, key-man, operational); risk should be overseen by **non-investment staff (separation of duties)** with **risk-trigger + escalation playbooks** ("train like you fight") for benchmark/liquidity/counterparty/redemption-contagion breaches (ch.19). Note also that **risk-factor timing is easier than return-factor timing** (risk factors don't trend, have larger variance, fewer arbitrageurs, scheduled events) and a risk factor can itself *generate* a return prediction (QuantAssetMgmt ch.7).

**The "bad times" lens on which risks are compensated (Ilmanen).** An asset's required premium reflects its covariance with *bad times* — periods of high marginal utility (recessions, crises, illiquidity spirals). Assets that pay off in bad times deserve LOW or even negative premia; assets that lose in bad times deserve HIGH premia (Ilmanen ch.1.2, 5.2.1). Therefore **the timing of losses matters as much as their size — correlations matter more than volatilities**, and standalone volatility is a poor guide to expected return ("all volatility is not equal"). **Safe havens:** long government bonds since the late 1990s have a negative stock–bond correlation and hedge equity crashes, so they can rationally carry a *negative* risk premium (ch.5.2.1, 9.5). Beware strategies that effectively **sell lottery tickets / tail insurance** (vol-selling, carry, harvesting illiquidity, betting-against-beta): high Sharpe in good times, concentrated losses in crises, and measured risk *understates* true risk (ch.1.2, 5.2.1, 19, 28.2.3). Expected returns vary over time and most premia are **countercyclical** — high near business-cycle troughs, low near peaks (ch.1.3, 5.2.3) — but countercyclical premia do NOT mean everyone should market-time, since high-return periods coincide with high risk aversion; at minimum, disciplined rebalancing captures a mild contrarian premium (ch.5.2.3, 8.6).

**Factor risk models** quantify return vs risk-factor exposure, shrink covariance estimation, hedge factor exposures, assess the incremental signal of new alphas, and attribute performance (skill vs factor tilt) (Jansen p.304–305). CAPM (single market β) failed empirically → **Fama-French 3-factor** (+SMB size, +HML value), **5-factor** (+RMW profitability, +CMA investment), many add momentum; **APT** allows multiple unspecified factors. Estimate premia via **Fama-MacBeth** two-stage regression (Jansen p.305–313). **Theory-driven** factor models (named systematic factors — market, sector, rates; intuitive, rigid) vs **empirical/PCA** (adaptive but slow to catch regime change, risk spurious factors); most quants prefer theory-driven, vendors BARRA/Northfield/Axioma/Quantal (Narang p.72–76).

**Building the risk model — the factor-structure covariance (QEPM).** Total stock variance = nondiversifiable + diversifiable: `V(rᵢ) = βᵢ'·V(f)·βᵢ + V(εᵢ)`. Factor-premium covariance `V(f)` is estimated from the premium series; specific variance `V(εᵢ)` from regression residuals. Stock-pair covariance `C(rᵢ,rⱼ) = βᵢ'·V(f)·βⱼ + C(εᵢ,εⱼ)`; in practice, unless T is large, **set residual covariances to 0** — `N(N−1)/2` of them can't be estimated reliably and well-diversified residuals net out. The full covariance matrix is therefore **`Σ = B·V(f)·B' + diag(V(ε))`**, the key input to every optimizer (QEPM ch.7.6). The regression **R²** of a factor model = nondiversifiable risk / total risk, so higher R² ⇒ more systematic, less idiosyncratic variance. **Estimation-error / forecasting risk (QEPM ch.8.6):** forecast variance has two parts — model-error variance **plus parameter uncertainty** (standard error of the estimated coefficients); accounting for it *always* raises the variance estimate, so ignoring it understates risk. Use a `K(L+1)×K(L+1)` parameter-covariance term or **bootstrapping** (drop-one pseudosamples → T sets of forecasts treated as independent forecasters).

**Covariance estimation — the model matrix, not the sample matrix.** Use a **model (multifactor) covariance, not sample covariance** — the sample matrix is singular for T ≤ N+1 and unstable out-of-sample (151Strategies p.55, fn.64). Build a **statistical risk model** (principal components + specific risk, with eRank to fix the number K of factors) to stabilize singular correlation matrices (151Strategies p.78–79). Two complementary repair techniques recur across López de Prado and Noguer:
- **Covariance shrinkage (Ledoit-Wolf).** The sample Σ is noisy for large N; shrink toward a structured target F: **`Σ̂ = ρF + (1−ρ)S`**, ρ the shrinkage intensity, F structured (e.g. constant-correlation), S the sample cov — well-conditioned even when N≈T (Noguer p.42; used in the book's own MVO backtests with a 60-day window, p.135). López de Prado's *AFML* / *ML for Asset Managers* cover this in more depth.
- **Denoising via Random Matrix Theory.** Analyze Σ's eigenvalues; those below the **Marchenko-Pastur** theoretical bound are noise and are filtered/clipped, keeping only the signal eigenvalues — apply *before* any `Σ⁻¹` step on large universes (Noguer p.43, Laloux et al.).
- **Regularization / sparsity.** LASSO (ℓ₁, drives weights to zero → sparse portfolios) and Ridge (ℓ₂, shrink) as estimation/selection tools; elastic net = a blend (Noguer p.43).

Exact factor-neutrality in the zero-specific-risk limit reduces optimization to a weighted regression.

**Tracking error — the active manager's primary risk control (Grinold-Kahn, QEPM).** TE is the *risk currency* for active managers; budgets range ~0.5%–10%/yr, set by the investment committee (QEPM ch.9.8; ch.15.3.3). **Spend the whole TE budget** as long as it adds expected excess return; control it via risk-adjusted-TE optimization (vary the aversion A until `TE = σₓ`). *Ex-ante* TE is built into the portfolio; *ex-post* TE = annualized SD of (portfolio − benchmark) excess return — the two never coincide exactly but move together (2%/5%/10% ex-ante → ~5%/10.3%/15.9% ex-post empirically) (QEPM ch.15.3.3, ch.17). **Benchmark/active-beta timing is hard** because it is a *single* bet: `IR_BT = IC_BT·√BR` with BR tiny — quarterly forecasts (BR=4) need IC=0.25 for IR=0.5; stock selection diversifies cross-sectionally (500 names), timing only serially. Implement active beta with **futures** (cash β=0, benchmark β=1 exactly, low cost) rather than tilting stock betas, which adds unavoidable residual risk and beta-estimation error; if no futures exist for the benchmark, timing value is very small (Grinold-Kahn ch.19, p.542–544). **Information horizon** = the **half-life of forecasting ability** (IR decays ~exponentially as you lag implementation); the half-life is an intrinsic, robust property, and value-added half-life = half the IR half-life (VA ∝ IR²) — old/lagged signals can still add value mixed with new (Grinold-Kahn ch.13, p.347–350).

**Rebalancing as a risk/cost control (Grinold-Kahn).** Trade only when a stock's marginal contribution to value added clears costs: `MCVAₙ = αₙ − 2·λ·ψ·MCARₙ`, and at an optimum `−SCₙ ≤ MCVAₙ ≤ PCₙ` — a **no-trade band** around each alpha of width = purchase + sale cost (e.g. 0.50% + 0.75% = 1.25%); inside the band, ignore new info (p.390–391). With linear costs the optimal rule (Leland 1996) is a **no-trade region** around the target — if breached, trade **only back to the boundary**, not to the target, cutting turnover/costs ~half with effectively no risk change (motivating boundary-driven rather than calendar rebalancing) (p.391). Rebalance whenever new information arrives, but shorter horizons mean noisier alphas while costs stay fixed, so over-frequent rebalancing churns on noise (p.389–390). **Dispersion** across separate accounts running the same strategy is unavoidable (transaction costs + differing histories/cash flows); zero dispersion only at prohibitive cost, so *some* dispersion is optimal — residual tracking error is bounded `ψ ≤ √(λ_TC·TC/λ_A)` and dispersion ∝ tracking error (p.402–404).

**Neutralization / crowding:** dollar-neutrality and factor-neutrality are built-in risk controls via Lagrange-constrained optimization (weights roughly suppressed by σᵢ) (151Strategies p.56–57). **Factor risk is undiversifiable but durable (compensated); alpha is idiosyncratic, diversifies away with more names, and is scarce/decaying** — some factors (SMB, put-call IV, DTC) decay as their risk perception falls (MachineTrading p.27–29, 55). **Correlation / crowding risk:** strategy correlations (Pearson) spike under "financial contagion"; uniform risk management is itself dangerous — if everyone runs constant Kelly leverage or risk parity on similar books, a loss forces *synchronized* liquidation (All Weather −7% in 2015; Aug-2007 quant meltdown: Goldman Global Alpha −22.5%, even Renaissance −8.7%) (MachineTrading p.22–23; Chan QT p.104–105; SuccessfulAlgo p.119–121). Ilmanen names this directly — **crowded-trade / de-leveraging risk is itself a factor** ("we found a new risk factor — it is us"): common levered positions + liquidity stress → synchronous unwind (Aug 2007, 1998, 2008); monitor stretched valuations, crowding, and triggers (widening TED spread, tightening funding, rising vol); *all* systematic strategies face this, not just carry (Ilmanen ch.20.2, 24.5). **Feedback loops amplify drawdowns** — wealth-dependent risk aversion, leverage/margin spirals, liquidity spirals, and VaR/stop-loss/risk-control spirals turn arbitrageurs into forced liquidators; **combining leverage + illiquid assets is the most dangerous mix** (Ilmanen ch.20.1). **Differentiate leverage *quality*, not just explicit vs embedded:** risky leverage levers to offset falling premia/vol, or levers illiquid/volatile assets, or must unwind in crises; less-risky leverage funds liquid low-vol assets for diversification via futures/short-dated options (Ilmanen ch.29.3.3). **See file 04** for the full treatment of factor models, neutralization, and crowding. **Portfolio insurance** (dynamic put-replication) is destabilizing at scale — it buys as prices rise and sells as they fall (≈ a giant stop order), does not reduce fundamental risk, only transfers it (Harris p.560–561, 582).

## 5. Risk metrics

| Metric | Formula | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Sharpe** | `(AROR − RF)/ASTD`, `ASTD = σ(daily)·√252` | Order-blind; assumes Gaussian (Kaufman p.2003–17; ChineseQuant p.7–9; QEPM ch.15.4) |
| **Information Ratio** | `(μ − μ_B)/σ_{r−r_B}`, or `α_B/σ(residual)` | Benchmark α per unit residual risk; index manager's IR = 0; used for performance fees (ChineseQuant p.7; Kaufman; QEPM ch.15.4) |
| **Treynor** | `(μ − r_f)/β` | Excess return per unit market risk |
| **Sortino** | excess / downside deviation (vs MAR threshold) | Penalizes only downside; preferred for skewed/option payouts (ChineseQuant; Kaufman; Bennett App.p.200) |
| **Calmar** | `CAGR / |maxDD over last 3 yrs|` | Prefer ≥1; better than MAR (full-history maxDD) (MachineTrading p.13–14); Davey's most-trusted single number (≈1-yr return/DD) |
| **Max drawdown** | `(cummax − equity).max()` | Captures fat-tail risk Sharpe misses |
| **Drawdown ratio** | `AROR/DP`, DP = σ of daily drawdowns | 1σ → 16% chance of larger DD (Kaufman) |

First two moments suffice only under normality; with heavy-tailed returns (1<α<2, infinite variance) replace the mean with **Expected Shortfall** (conditional mean beyond a quantile), and the self-normalized Sharpe `(r−r_f)/s` remains usable with bootstrap CIs (ChineseQuant p.45–46). Also: Geometric ratio, Ulcer Index / Average Maximum Retracement (semi-variance of drawdowns), time-to-recovery (Kaufman p.2003–17). **Annualization:** 252 trading days; `AROR = (E_n/E_0)^(252/n) − 1`; **annualized vol = daily σ × √252** (1.502% daily → 23.8% annual) (Kaufman p.162–66). **Deflated / posterior Sharpe:** Bayesian credible intervals give a *distribution* of the Sharpe ratio rather than a point estimate — granular, multiple-testing-aware risk assessment (Jansen p.475–86); see also file 07 on overfitting/deflated Sharpe.

**Always compare risk-adjusted, not raw, returns (QEPM ch.15).** A high-return manager may just be levered — you could borrow to match the riskier book's vol and beat them. Key ratios: **Sharpe** `SR = (r_P − r_f)/σ_P` (meaningful only in *relative* comparison); **Information ratio** `IR = α_B/σ(residual)` (benchmark α per unit residual risk, index manager's theoretical IR = 0); **CAPM/Jensen α** (intercept of return on the market) and **benchmark α** (market replaced by the benchmark) — sign *and* significance matter (t > 2 ≈ 95%, corrected for heteroskedasticity/autocorrelation); **multifactor α** on Fama-French 3-/4-/5-/6-factor rates managers *more harshly* and is often negative (open debate: loading on priced factors is mechanical/replicable, so use CAPM-α if you'd reward factor tilts, multifactor-α if only genuine stock-picking). Note `SR_P = α_P/σ_P + ρ_{P,M}·SR_M`, so **Jensen-α and Sharpe rankings can disagree** — a positive α need not raise the Sharpe ratio if market correlation drops enough (QEPM ch.15.4). **Statistically verifying skill needs lots of data:** the t-stat for α ties to `t ≈ IR·√T` (with a benchmark-Sharpe correction) — for benchmark SR≈0.25 and manager IR=0.5 you need **≥22 monthly returns** for a significant α, usually more than a bonus cycle allows; a fundamental limit on judging managers and live strategies (QEPM ch.15.4.5). **Measure returns carefully** (QEPM ch.15.2): use **total return** (price + dividend), remove cash flows with **time-weighted / (modified) Dietz** return `r = (Vₜ₊₁ − Vₜ − Cₜ₊₁)/(Vₜ + ½Cₜ₊₁)`, link geometrically, annualize as `(1+r)^(D/k) − 1` — the Beardstown-ladies tale (counting inflows as return turned a market-lagging 9.1% into a claimed 23.4%) is the cautionary case. **Beware metrics that flatter ill-timed risk:** penalize bad-times losses, downside-vs-upside vol asymmetry, illiquidity (smoothed returns inflate the Sharpe — Madoff had a great SR), and unquantifiable tail/operational risk; treat the 100+ metrics as a **"mosaic"** and prioritize *distributions over point estimates* (Ilmanen ch.28.2.3; QuantAssetMgmt ch.18). For attribution, rank data quality returns-based < holdings-based < transactions-based; Brinson-Hood-Beebower decomposes allocation + selection + interaction + timing (QuantAssetMgmt ch.18).

**Strategy risk ≠ portfolio risk (López de Prado, AFML Ch.15).** Portfolio risk (the CRO's VaR) says nothing about the probability the *strategy* itself fails. Model bet outcomes as a **binomial process** to expose when a strategy is uneconomic:
- **Symmetric-payout Sharpe from precision & frequency:** for n IID bets/yr with profit/loss ±π and precision p, `θ = (2p−1)·√n / (2·√(p(1−p)))`. **π cancels — Sharpe depends on precision p (not accuracy) and bet count n.** This is the economic basis of HFT: p barely above 0.5 still yields a high Sharpe if n is large (p=0.55 needs ~396 bets/yr for SR=2; weekly bets n=52 need p≈0.6336 for SR=2) (Ch 15.2). This is the same precision-times-√frequency intuition as QEPM's `t ≈ IR·√T` skill test above.
- **Asymmetric payouts (Ch 15.3):** with π⁺ (prob p) and π⁻<π⁺, `θ = ((π⁺−π⁻)p + π⁻)·√n / ((π⁺−π⁻)·√(p(1−p)))`. Invert (quadratic in p) for the precision required to hit a target SR. **Danger flag:** large n makes SR hypersensitive to tiny moves in p (n=260, π⁻=−.01, π⁺=.005: p 0.70→0.72 lifts SR from 1.17 to 2.0) — such a strategy is intrinsically fragile to small precision drops.
- **Probability of strategy failure (Ch 15.4.1):** (1) estimate π⁻, π⁺ as means of negative/positive bet outcomes (or a 2-Gaussian mixture via EF3M); (2) annual freq n = T/y; (3) **bootstrap p** — for I iterations draw ⌊n·k⌋ outcomes with replacement (k = years investors use to judge, e.g. 2), compute observed precision per draw, fit the PDF of p with a KDE; (4) find p_θ\*, the precision below which SR < target θ\*; (5) **strategy risk = P[p < p_θ\*]**. **Discard strategies with P[fail] > ~0.05** even if they hold low-vol instruments (Ch 15.4.2). Complements PSR; uniquely separates parameters under the PM's control {π⁻, π⁺, n} from those that are not (p set by the market, θ\* by the investor).

**Value-at-Risk — `P(L ≥ VaR_{1−α}) = α`.** Three methods:
1. **Parametric / variance-covariance (Normal):** fit `mu, sig = norm.fit(ret)`, `k = norm.ppf(α, mu, sig)`, `VaR = −k`. Multi-asset: `VaR = Z·√(Σ wᵢwⱼ σᵢσⱼ ρᵢⱼ)·V·√(days)` — correlations reduce total risk below the naive sum (Kaufman p.2017–25; PythonQuants p.189–91; SuccessfulAlgo p.123–26, CitiGroup example $56,503 daily 99% on $1M). QEPM's closed form: under normality `VaR = V·(μ − k·σ)` with k = 1.65 (95%), 1.96 (97.5%), 2.33 (99%), 2.58 (99.5%), sub-period scaling `√s` of annual σ — e.g. $100M, μ=10%, σ=20%, 97.5% ⇒ one-year VaR ≈ −$29.2M (QEPM ch.15.3.5).
2. **Historical (easiest, spreadsheet-able):** compute portfolio daily returns over ~99 past days, sort ascending, the 5th-worst of 100 = 5% VaR (Kaufman p.2025; PythonQuants histogram-integration variant p.190). Tsay's order-statistic form: order returns, take `r_(ℓ)` with `ℓ=np`, where `r_(ℓ)≈N(x_p, p(1−p)/(n·f(x_p)²))` — no distributional assumption beyond stationarity of the tail (Tsay p.338–339).
3. **Monte Carlo:** draw many paths from a fitted distribution, take the empirical quantile of simulated P&L (PythonQuants p.191; see file 05 for the simulation pattern).

VaR scales up with horizon (Hilpisch p.288–90; Tsay's **square-root-of-time rule** `VaR(k)=√k·VaR`, p.329–330). **Limits:** ignores tail magnitude *beyond* VaR, assumes normality, is rearward-looking — "an airbag that works except in a crash," never use it alone (SuccessfulAlgo p.123–26). Crucially, **VaR is not sub-additive** — a merged portfolio's risk can exceed the sum of its parts — so it is **not a coherent risk measure** (Tsay p.328). Equity returns are fat-tailed, so Normal VaR *understates* risk (PythonQuants p.189). As a *targeting* tool VaR is flawed: holding constant vol by varying leverage produces too little risk in calm times and too much in turbulent times (Narang p.71, 177).

**VaR / Expected Shortfall / EVT — the time-series toolkit (Tsay).**
- **RiskMetrics:** assumes `rₜ|F_{t−1} ~ N(0, σₜ²)` with `σₜ² = ασ_{t−1}² + (1−α)r_{t−1}²` (an IGARCH(1,1)/exponential-smoothing recursion, typically α≈0.94); then 1-day 5% VaR = 1.65σ_{t+1}, 1% VaR = 2.326σ_{t+1} (Tsay p.328–330).
- **Econometric (GARCH) approach:** fit mean (ARMA) + volatility (GARCH) jointly; the 1-step conditional distribution is `N[r̂ₜ(1), σ̂ₜ²(1)]`, so `VaR = r̂ₜ(1) + z_{1−p}σ̂ₜ(1)` (Gaussian) or the standardized-Student-t quantile `r̂ₜ(1) + t_v(1−p)σ̂ₜ(1)/√(v/(v−2))` for fat tails — conditions on *current* volatility, unlike historical methods (Tsay p.333–334).
- **Expected Shortfall (CVaR):** the average loss given VaR is breached; **coherent (sub-additive)**, so preferred to VaR for aggregation. Under conditional normality `ES_q = µₜ + [f(x_q)/p]σₜ` — 99% ES = µₜ + 2.6652σₜ, 95% ES = µₜ + 2.0627σₜ (Tsay p.332–333, p.338). Noguer's optimisation-ready form (Rockafellar-Uryasev): `CVaR_α = min_ν { ν + 1/((1−α)m) Σⱼ (−wᵀRⱼ − ν)⁺ }` — a convex objective or constraint for tail-aware allocation on non-elliptical equity returns (QuantPortfolioOpt p.43–45). For 1<α<2 (infinite variance) replace the mean with ES outright (ChineseQuant p.45–46).
- **Extreme Value Theory (EVT) for the tails:** normalized maxima converge to the **Generalized Extreme Value (GEV)** distribution `F*(x)=exp[−(1+ξx)^{−1/ξ}]`. Three families by shape ξ: **Gumbel** (ξ=0, thin-tailed: normal/lognormal), **Fréchet (ξ>0, power tails: stable/Student-t — the case of interest for returns)**, **Weibull** (ξ<0, finite tail). The **tail index = 1/ξ** is *invariant under time aggregation*; for IBM daily returns ξ̂≈0.33 → Fréchet, rejecting normality (Tsay p.342–350). **EVT-based VaR** (block maxima): `r*_n = β_n − (α_n/ξ_n){1−[−ln(1−p*)]^{−ξ_n}}`, linking sub-period probability to the raw series via `1−p* = [P(rₜ≤r*_n)]^n`; **peaks-over-threshold (POT)** models exceedances over a high threshold η directly (Tsay p.354–363). EVT + a Pareto tail index + parametric bootstrap is exactly the HFT recommendation for high-order risk (HFTAldridge ch.14).
- **Stationarity gate (ADF):** before treating a spread as tradeable, confirm stationarity with the **Augmented Dickey-Fuller** test — regress `Δxₜ = c_t + β_c x_{t−1} + Σφᵢ Δx_{t−i} + eₜ` and test `β_c=0` via `ADF = β̂_c/std(β̂_c)`; critical values are non-standard (Brownian-motion functionals) from simulation (Tsay p.76–77).

## 6. Practical risk limits (rules to encode)

The machine enforces what humans won't (Hougaard p.140–41). Core hard limits:
- **Per-trade %:** ≤1–2% of equity (Aziz 2% hard cap; if a logical stop forces >2% risk, skip the trade or take fewer shares, p.22–26). Kaufman's ceiling ≤5% (some ≤3%) (p.1998). Tomasini: ideally <1% (0.5%) from the entry (p.197).
- **Minimum reward:risk** ≥ 2:1 — at 2:1 you can be wrong 40% and still profit (Aziz Rule 5, p.21). *But* there is no universal optimal R:R — a random-entry study across 7 markets found most (ES, GC, EC, CT, TY) optimal R:R well *below* 1 (small targets, large stops), while Soybeans/Crude were ~3–4; test per market and ignore "3:1 is best" adages (Davey p.139–58).
- **Daily max loss / giveback:** a hard daily loss limit; after a profitable open, stop (or take a mandatory timeout) once you give back ~30% of closed P&L, enforced by a risk administrator (Bellafiore p.262–64). Davey's risk-protection add-ons: daily loss limiter, entry-delay after a losing trade, no weekend positions, a high-volatility "kill switch" — add them *before* full testing (p.180–81).
- **Portfolio open-risk budget:** cap total concurrent open risk (e.g. 6% = six trades at 1% each) (BrokerTest p.269). For volatility-parity books this is partly automatic: at 20bp/ATR-unit and 3-unit stops, whole-portfolio giveback to stops is bounded ~60bp/position (Following ch.3).
- **Gross/net exposure & leverage:** EU UCITS caps total exposure ≤2.5× equity, commodities ≤20%; futures reserves typically use only ~25% of investment (cutting 10:1 margin to ~3:1), replenish if loss >25% of initial margin (Kaufman p.2056–61). CFD/FX leverage is symmetric: 20:1 = 5% margin, and a 10% adverse move at 10:1 wipes 100% of margin and triggers margin calls (Hilpisch p.234–35). Remember **leverage ≠ risk** when cost-to-stop is equalized across sectors (Following ch.5), but levering *illiquid or high-vol* assets, or borrowing that must unwind in a crisis, is the dangerous kind (Ilmanen ch.29.3.3).
- **Mandatory mechanical rules:** a stop on *every* entry and add-on; block/flag any add to a losing position; block size *increases* during drawdown and decreases during winning runs (scale up on winners, not losers); cap trade frequency to prevent overtrade/revenge loops (Hougaard p.140–41, 117). **Never average down, never meet margin calls, liquidate the worst position first** (Kaufman p.1998, p.2094–2100; Aziz p.20–25). Predefine risk *before* entry — the single most common error to eliminate (TradingZone p.91–92).
- **Regulatory floor:** US **Pattern Day Trader** rule requires ≥$25k equity for margin day trading; swing trading avoids PDT but takes on overnight/weekend gap risk (BrokerTest p.238–39; Leshik p.10).

In automated/agent systems, expose these as a separate **veto/modify layer**: an `AbstractRiskManager` receives the already-*sized* order and may eliminate, modify, or create orders based on portfolio composition and external risk (correlation, volatility) — distinct from the strategy and from the position sizer (Halls-Moore AdvAlgo p.353, 473). LLM-agent finding: weaker configs "generate plausible market analysis but fail in position sizing, timing, or order selection" — execution discipline, not analysis, separates winners (ATLAS, p.7). Davey echoes this from the human side: the example system's whole profit came from ~5 big days/yr, so the discipline to **take every trade** is itself a risk control (Davey ch.19).

## 7. Quant-specific risks

- **Regime change / non-stationarity.** Moments are not constant across regimes — use rolling parameters (SuccessfulAlgo p.119–121). Active overlays: a **2-state Gaussian HMM** (`hmmlearn`, n_components=2, covariance_type="full") fit to SPY returns; in the low-vol state allow opens, in the high-vol state cancel all new longs and only let a *close* through if already invested. Backtest effect: maxDD ~56%→~24%, Sharpe 0.37→0.48, CAGR 6.41→6.88%, at the cost of fewer positive-expectation bets; needs periodic retraining (Halls-Moore AdvAlgo p.466–479). A **Bayesian stochastic-volatility** filter yields a full posterior of current vol (priors σ∼Exp(50), ν∼Exp(0.1), latent vol as a Gaussian random walk, fat-tailed Student-t returns), used to auto-trigger leverage reduction or veto signals in high-vol periods (AdvAlgo p.59–66). **Volatility filtering** (high-priority money-management): high vol does *not* pay — when annualized vol >45%, returns fall and risk rises; exit when vol exceeds a high threshold (e.g. 0.30) and re-enter below a low threshold (e.g. 0.15). On QQQ this cut daily-return σ from 102→56 (~45% less risk) with slightly more profit and no trading during the 2000 bubble or 2008 crisis; each market needs its own threshold (Kaufman p.1695–97). HFTAldridge formalizes the priority order — **1st-order stop losses** (tuned so natural vol doesn't trigger them), **2nd-order volatility cutouts** (halt strategies in adverse vol regimes; regress gains on rolling vol; GARCH or Markov state-switching forecasts), **3rd/4th-order VaR / Expected Shortfall** (historical not just parametric μ−1.65σ/μ−2.33σ; fat tails ⇒ EVT + bootstrap), **higher-order hedging** (delta & portfolio) (ch.14).
- **Model risk.** Strategy assumptions break under regime shift or crowding. Manage by letting Kelly drive leverage toward zero as trailing return decays — a graceful taper, not an abrupt shutdown (Chan QT p.107). Did the model break? With few trades even a 3.0 info-ratio system has ~34% chance of a net loss after 10 trades; use Monte Carlo / binomial expected loss-runs and a **chi-square** test `χ² = Σ(O−E)²/E` (significant χ² → likely overfitting) (Kaufman p.2100–12). López de Prado's **probability-of-strategy-failure** algorithm (§5) is the quantitative version: discard if `P[p < p_θ\*] > 0.05`.
- **Look-ahead-driven overconfidence.** The largest alpha signals are often the riskiest (adverse selection); equal-weighting hedges idiosyncratic and bad-data blowups (Narang p.95–96). Software risk: the live ATS must match backtested trades exactly (Chan QT p.107–108). Don't trust short-vol backtests as forward estimates — selling vol earns a real but path-dependent premium and the backtest hides the tail (Bennett p.85). See file 07 for backtest-overfitting and the deflated Sharpe ratio.
- **Tail risk — and why Gaussian risk measures understate it.** In Bayesian terms, never assign *zero* prior to "impossible" events — assign a very low probability instead; cited failures from ignoring tail risk: Barings (1995), LTCM (1998), Lehman (2008) (AdvAlgo p.14). Tail/gap risk on counter-trend and overnight swings is mostly unhedgeable except at great cost — *size for it* (Grimes p.81). Take profits on *extreme* (windfall) moves but not on small ones, which clips the biggest trends (Kaufman p.439–41). **The only true defense against a price shock is being out of the market** — prefer strategies that are in-market less and have high return per unit time-in-market (Kaufman p.1989–90). Counterparty/credit risk is real: futures accounts are not SIPC-insured (MF Global), levered accounts can go negative (EURCHF 2015, −29%/day) — trade via an LLC/LP to cap personal liability and use multiple brokers (MachineTrading p.10–12; SuccessfulAlgo p.119–121; HFTAldridge ch.14: diversify brokers — Lehman).

  **Taleb's tail-risk mechanism (Dynamic Hedging) — fat tails come from volatility-of-volatility, not exotic math.** The price of OTM "wings" above Black-Scholes is driven by **vvol — "volatility of volatility… related to the fourth moment, or kurtosis"** plus vega convexity (p.238). In a stochastic-vol (Hull-White-style) model ATM options are barely affected by vol swings while OTM options gain the most; the implied-vol smile rises in proportion to vvol (p.239–240). *Why vvol makes tails fat:* conditional on being in the tails, the most likely state is **high** volatility (high vol reaches the tails more easily), so the tails inherit the thickness of the high-vol regime while the body inherits the high peak of the low-vol regime (p.254–255). Mixing two normals (one 4× the vol) already produces fat tails + a high central peak; a 3-regime toy (5%/15%/20% vols, equiprobable) reproduces real fat-tailed histograms (p.255, 258). Real histograms (JPY, SP500, 30-yr yields, EuroMark) all show the **"high peak syndrome"** — high peaks *are* fat tails, the same phenomenon — so shorting the tails is really betting against the *peak*, profiting "when nothing happens rather than during extreme moves" (p.255). **This is precisely why Gaussian VaR understates the tails:** the Normal assumption has neither the vvol-driven tail thickness nor the high peak, so a "same-volatility normal" misprices exactly the region where blow-ups live. Taleb warns to think of fat tails as *changing volatility* rather than blowing-out variance — Pareto-Levy/stable laws (α<2 → infinite variance, no compact support) are "scary for anyone in the markets" and harder to handle than a high-vvol-on-low-vol picture (p.259). This is the intuition behind Tsay's Fréchet (ξ>0, power-tail) finding for real return series (§5).

  **Skew = correlation between price moves and volatility, and its value is on the *path*.** Skew is positive if asset-changes and vol-changes are positively correlated, negative if negatively (p.252, 258). SP500 is the canonical negatively-biased asset — "volatility drops after a small rally but increases after a large one," **"up the escalator, down the chute"** (increased vol in sell-offs, muted vol in rallies); the correlation is nonlinear, tied to the *range* not the level, and threshold-marked (p.240, 245). A **histogram hides the path**: a symmetric-looking histogram can still make a dynamic hedger money owning expensive puts and shorting cheap calls. **Path-Dependence Rule: "the value of the skew for a dynamic hedger resides more in the behavior of implied volatility along the path leading to a terminal value than in the probability of the asset ending up on such terminal value"** (p.248) — OTM puts are great assets not just because the market might crash but because of how implied vol behaves *on the way down*, which is exactly why deriving the risk-neutral density from option prices (Breeden-Litzenberger 2nd derivative) is "suspicious" — it ignores path dependency (p.248). Practical corollaries: skew is **not** a reliable risk indicator (it can be *inversely* correlated to ATM vol — low strikes "sticky," so skew rises as ATM falls); **ATM vol is the risk measure**, and for skewed/option payouts use **Sortino**, not standard deviation (Bennett p.143, App.p.200).

  **Why options aren't free, and why snapshots mislead (Dynamic Hedging).** Dynamic hedging is a *craft*, not a risk-free proposition: replication faces a permanent dilemma between **variance of returns** and **transaction costs** (p.17–18), and higher hedging frequency cuts variance but shifts the P&L distribution left via costs. **Path dependence is the central risk snapshots miss** — theta is a path-*independent* measure that cannot capture the *whipsaw* ("the path where the market snaps away from its origin then returns": theoretically improbable, "rather frequent in reality") (p.184, 220). The **stop-loss replication fallacy**: the napkin scheme (sell the call, leave a stop to buy at the strike, reverse on recross) fails because Brownian local time never goes to zero — no rebalancing frequency lets you keep the premium, and "the frequency of rebalancing does not affect the fair value of the option" (more frequent = more *precise*, same *expected* value); with frictions, hedging cost ∝ time spent oscillating across the band (p.211). **Adverse selection: you inherit the bad distribution** — "the anxiety of markets gets reflected in [option traders'] position"; market-neutral ≠ safe (the 1994 "market neutral" hedge-fund blow-up: "that guy did not get the second derivative right"); and **VaR with deltas-plus-gamma can read *zero* for a position loaded with hidden convexity, skew, and vega risk** (a risk reversal) — you must include *shadow gamma* (p.140, 145, 251). Lognormality manufactures a right tail at high vol (the median slides left, deltas shift toward 50%), and mixing log-Brownian-*price* bond futures with log-Brownian-*yield* bonds in one book is a "serious consequence at high volatility" — triple-digit vols are recurring, not theoretical (Silver/Eurodollar 1987, Oil 1990, the Irish punt at 4000% overnight in 1992) (p.142–144, 212).

- **Option-position risk via aggregate Greeks (Natenberg, *Option Volatility and Pricing*).** For an options book, position risk *is* the set of aggregate Greeks — but they are a **snapshot, not the whole story**. A book that is delta/gamma/theta/vega = 0 *today* still changes as time passes and as the underlying and IV move; sensitivities migrate (a risk-reversal / split-strike position can turn delta-*negative* on *both* a rise and a fall because gamma flips sign through the inflection point) — so always ask what happens if price moves up/down, if IV rises/falls, and as time passes (ch.21). **Vega is not additive across expirations:** summing vegas assumes IV moves equally everywhere, which it doesn't — weight each expiration's vega by its expected move relative to a *primary month* (term-structure "whippiness"), or a nominally "vega-neutral" book can still be materially long/short vol (ch.20). **Breakeven (implied) volatility of a position ≈ entry vol + theoretical edge ÷ vega** — the vol level at which the edge is erased; widen the margin for error by **raising edge or cutting vega** (ch.21). **Diversify the book** across strikes/months: concentrated risk at one strike/expiration (even when net Greeks are flat) becomes dangerous as the underlying approaches that strike near expiry. Account for **model risk** — frictions, non-constant vol, gaps (jump-diffusion), price-dependent vol (CEV), and fat-tailed (high-kurtosis) real distributions all mean model values understate option value and tail frequency (ch.23) — and **manage to clearing-firm stress limits** (survive e.g. a 20% underlying move or a doubling of IV) (ch.21). Finally, **portfolio-level volatility drag** (higher return *volatility* lowers compounded return for a given average; Sharpe frames the trade-off) is itself a rationale for hedging to reduce variance (ch.17) — the same `GM ≈ AM − Var/2` identity that drives §1.4 and §1.6. (Aggregate-Greeks management is the option-book counterpart of factor-exposure netting in §4; see file 13 for the full Greeks treatment. Taleb's caution above applies: deltas-plus-gamma VaR can read *zero* for a position loaded with hidden vega/skew convexity.)
- **Avoiding *permanent* capital loss — distress & fraud screens (Gray & Carlisle, *Quantitative Value*).** A distinct risk lens for a *long equity* book: before sizing or buying, screen out names with elevated **risk of permanent impairment** (bankruptcy or accounting fraud), since no position sizing recovers from a zero. The book's "avoid permanent loss" filters use the **Altman Z-score** (bankruptcy/distress), the **Ohlson O-score** (distress probability), and the **Beneish M-score** (earnings-manipulation / fraud likelihood) as exclusion screens — a pre-trade risk gate that complements the volatility-based sizing in §1. These screens belong to the fundamental-quality stack: **see file 14** for the full Altman Z / Ohlson O / Beneish M definitions and their use in stock selection; here they function purely as a *risk filter* (drop the candidate, don't size it).
- **Capacity / size as a binding risk constraint (the square-root law — Bouchaud).** Because slippage per unit is `(2/3)·σ_T·√(Q/V_T)`, **total transaction cost ∝ Q^{3/2}** — costs grow *faster than linearly* in size, eroding and eventually cancelling a strategy's edge. **Capacity ≈ the Q at which cumulative √-impact cost equals expected alpha**; high-turnover strategies are most exposed; the signal-driven results `Q* ∝ α` and `T* ∝ √Q` quantify the optimal bet size for a given signal strength and horizon (TQP p.384–385, 392–393). **Mark-to-market is fragile — apply an impact haircut:** instantaneous price is meaningful only for infinitesimal size, so liquidating a 1%-of-cap holding in a 2%-vol stock would move price ~1.5% (√-law) and the impact-adjusted value sits ~1.5% below the screen price; using fragile prices to value large books feeds deleveraging spirals and enables closing-price manipulation (TQP p.418–419). **Market-making inventory risk:** without control, inventory grows like √T (uncorrelated flow) or even `T^{(1−γ)/2}` (long-memory flow) → unbounded → ruin; a mean-reversion control makes inventory a bounded AR(1)/Ornstein-Uhlenbeck process — inventory control is mandatory (TQP p.323–324). See file 09 for market-impact mechanics; capacity is a *risk* limit here because exceeding it silently converts a profitable signal into a losing one.
- **Behavioral risk.** Prospect theory: the value function is concave in gains and steeper (convex) in losses, and small probabilities are overweighted (ChineseQuant p.43, 56) — which is exactly why mechanical rules must override discretion (TradingZone p.34–35; Hougaard: one trade is random, 100 are predictable, p.139; LightScalp: cut size to <10% in drawdown rather than chase recovery, ch.7). **Wealth-dependent risk aversion / habit formation** (Campbell-Cochrane) makes this systemic: as wealth nears a subsistence level, risk aversion rises sharply, creating self-reinforcing boom-bust feedback loops — the same mechanism that turns levered arbitrageurs into forced liquidators in a crowding event (Ilmanen ch.5.2.3, 20.1).
