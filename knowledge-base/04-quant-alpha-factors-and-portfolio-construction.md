## 1. What an alpha factor is

An **alpha factor** is a transformation of raw data that outputs *one value per asset per evaluation date*, designed to predict cross-sectional forward returns (Jansen, p.169). Equivalently, an alpha is "any reasonable expected return one may wish to trade on" (151Strategies, p.59). It is fundamentally a *ranking* signal: only the relative ordering across the cross-section matters, because the book is built long the high-ranked names and short the low-ranked names (winners − losers, a zero-cost long-short portfolio).

More precisely (Grinold & Kahn, *Active Portfolio Management*), an **alpha is risk-adjusted excess return** — the residual expected return after controlling for the benchmark/market and any priced factors. QEPM (Chincarini & Kim, *Quantitative Equity Portfolio Management*) formalizes three nested variants (ch.2): **benchmark α (αB)** — from `rP = α + β·rB + ε`, α is the mean residual; **CAPM α** — same but the reference is the market (S&P 500); **multifactor α (αMF)** — from `rP = α + β1·f1 + … + βK·fK + ε`, what the K-factor model cannot explain. They coincide in special cases (αMF = αCAPM when the market is the only factor); an *inefficient* benchmark inflates αB above αCAPM. The **ex-ante information ratio** is `IR = αB / ω`, where ω = S(ε) is residual-risk std — expected excess return per unit of excess risk.

Two epistemologies coexist:
- **Theory-driven** — anchored in an economic rationale, either a rational risk premium (you are paid to bear a risk) or a behavioral bias (under/over-reaction, herding). Anchoring in rationale guards against false discoveries from data-mining (Jansen, p.170-171). Ilmanen (*Expected Returns*, Foreword; ch.5) states the same dichotomy as the only two routes to a positive premium: (i) rational compensation for bearing a risk others avoid, or (ii) being paid for others' systematic mistakes. QEPM elevates "models must rest on sound economic theory" to a *tenet* — it is precisely what rules out data mining (ch.2.3).
- **Data-driven / formulaic** — alphas discovered by systematic search over an operator grammar (Kakushadze). ~250+ factors are published (Hsu); WorldQuant claims *millions* tested (Jansen, p.169). The distinction is the **academic alpha** (public, decaying) vs **proprietary alpha** divide (151Strategies, p.59-60).
- **Supervised-learning / panel** — Coqueret & Guida (*Machine Learning for Factor Investing*, ch.3) recast factor investing as **supervised learning on a firm-characteristic panel**: the data is a 3-D cube (time × assets × characteristics), each row a stock-date with ~tens-to-hundreds of features (the characteristics) and a label = forward (often risk-adjusted) return; a model `y = f(X) + ε` is fit cross-sectionally and its predictions become the alpha score. This subsumes the classic linear factor model as the special case where `f` is linear, and opens the door to **penalized regressions and tree ensembles** for nonlinear factor interactions (§7). The anchoring discipline is the same — the factor-zoo redundancy/competition tests (§2, below) guard against the publication-bias / p-hacking that inflates the count.

A single alpha is rarely tradeable; the production object is the **alpha combo** — a weighted blend of many alphas (151Strategies, p.59).

**The seven tenets of QEPM** (Chincarini & Kim, ch.2.3) — the governing philosophy of quant equity: (1) markets are mostly efficient; (2) pure risk-free arbitrage does not exist; (3) quant analysis creates *statistical* arbitrage (low- but non-zero-risk) — the opportunity; (4) it must combine *all available information efficiently* (the "information criterion"); (5) models must rest on sound economic theory (rules out data mining); (6) models should reflect *persistent, stable* patterns (parameter stability); (7) deviate from the benchmark only when estimation uncertainty is small enough (parameter uncertainty). Tenets 1-2 bound the reach, tenet 3 is the edge, tenet 4 is efficiency, tenets 5-7 are the statistical discipline.

## 2. The classic factor zoo (equities/ETF)

Canonical long-short factors, each typically a zero-cost winners−losers portfolio (151Strategies, pp.40-44, 61-64, 73-74; Jansen, p.173-187):

- **Value** (mean-reversion to fair value): cheap-on-fundamentals names outperform. Measures: book-to-price (B/P, HML), earnings/cash-flow/FCF/sales/book yields, EBITDA/EV, PEG. The classic stat-arb / market-neutral long-short.
- **Momentum** ("the trend is your friend"): long winners / short losers over a 6-12m formation window, **skipping the most recent month** (which carries short-term reversal). Drivers: under/over-reaction, feedback loops, herding. Measures: price momentum, RSI, 52-week-high %, analyst revisions, short interest.
- **Size** (SMB, small-minus-big): small-caps earn excess returns. Empirically the small-cap factor *return* is usually negative, i.e., small-caps outperform (QuantTrading, p.134-135).
- **Low-volatility / low-beta**: low-vol and low-beta stocks earn higher risk-adjusted returns, contradicting CAPM. The market chronically *underprices* high-beta names (QuantTrading, p.153). VIX vs S&P correlation ≈ −0.54 (Jansen, p.173-187).
- **Quality**: profitable, stable, low-leverage firms outperform. Measures: gross profitability, ROE, ROIC, asset turnover, current ratio, interest coverage, and **accruals** (an earnings-quality proxy). Often combined with value → "quality at a reasonable price."

**Ilmanen's style-premia framing (the "factor cube").** Ilmanen (*Expected Returns*) reframes all asset/strategy returns as bundles of *underlying factor exposures*, viewed through **three overlapping lenses**: asset classes (equity, term/bond, credit), dynamic strategy styles (value, carry, momentum/trend, vol-selling), and underlying risk factors. He deems four risk factors most important — **growth, inflation, liquidity, and tail risk** (volatility/correlation/skew) — and treats the style premia as "alternative betas": harvestable sensitivities to those factors and/or behavioral mispricings (ch.1.2; 16.1). These are *not* a clean decomposition; each asset is a bundle of exposures that largely set its expected return. The **old world** (single-factor CAPM, constant expected returns, frictionless markets, mean-variance investors) gave way to a **new world** of multiple priced factors, time-varying risk premia, skew/liquidity preferences, supply-demand effects, and frictions/irrationality (ch.5). The four (really five, adding illiquidity) **core return sources Ilmanen says to combine: value, carry, momentum/trend, defensive/low-vol, and illiquidity** (ch.29.2) — each has poor timing of its losses but a durable long-run reward, and none works every year, so harvest broadly rather than relying on the equity premium alone.

The five styles in equity detail:
- **Value (Ilmanen ch.12).** Value stocks (low P/E, P/B, P/D, P/CF) earn higher long-run returns than glamour/growth. U.S. 1926-2009 compound value premium (VMG/HML) = 4.1% vs a 5.7% equity premium, but lower vol gives it the *same* Sharpe (0.39); non-U.S. since 1975 is stronger (SR up to 0.78). Stable across decades with little decay, but hit hard in the late-1990s dot-com boom and the 2007-09 quant unwind. **Premium is concentrated where arbitrage is hard:** weakest in well-covered, liquid large-caps; strongest in low-institutional-ownership names (~3/4 of names, <10% of cap). Distressed/low-rated firms load *positively* on VMG but earn poor returns, so **quality/profitability/momentum filters that weed out distress *enhance* value** (ch.12.4). Fundamental indexing (Arnott-Hsu-Moore) and equal/min-vol/equal-vol weighting are all cap-indifferent, contrarian-rebalanced *value tilts* that beat cap-weighting (~2% in U.S. large-cap) — "value by another name with more aggressive sizing."
- **Momentum (Ilmanen ch.14.5).** Cross-sectional momentum (buy 6-12m winners, short losers) works in nearly every asset class; in equities it is the second-most-popular selection signal after value. Return ~12 months ago has the strongest relation to this month's return (annual periodicity), not concentrated in earnings months. Price momentum ≈ earnings momentum (post-revision drift); both weakened after Reg FD (Oct 2000). Lead-lag: info travels slowly from liquid large caps to illiquid small caps; profits are larger in volatile, low-grade, high-dispersion stocks, after market gains, and when sentiment is optimistic — and lower after losses / at turning points / in high vol. The spring-2009 momentum crash echoed 1932 as junk/value losers violently rebounded.
- **Carry / dividend yield (Ilmanen).** Overlaps heavily with value (a poor diversifier of it).
- **Defensive / low-volatility / betting-against-beta (Ilmanen ch.12.5-12.6, 19).** "The volatility puzzle": low-return-volatility stocks tend to *outperform* high-vol ones — and this overlaps empirically with value (low-vol = boring value, high-vol = glamour). Within every asset class the most volatile segment underperforms (high-vol/high-beta stocks, long-duration bonds, CCC credit). Frazzini-Pedersen **BAB** (long low-beta, short high-beta, each rescaled to beta 1) is profitable across asset classes with turnover near equity-value and prevalence rivaling value/carry/momentum (ch.19.5). The relation is a **hockey-stick, not linear**: sorting on recent vol, upside vol, MAX daily return, or expected skewness gives a *flat* curve except a sharply *negative* top quintile — the "lottery ticket" decile. The MAX-return effect (Bali-Cakici-Whitelaw) is ~−1%/month and *strengthens* after controlling for size/value/momentum/illiquidity (ch.19.4). **Characteristic vs beta differ in sign:** high-vol *stocks* are penalized, yet stocks with large *negative* sensitivity to rising market vol earn a positive premium (small-cap value has the most negative vol-beta). Drivers: **leverage constraints** (leverage-averse investors crowd into high-beta names, leaving low-vol cheap — "the best opportunities often involve levering up low-volatility positions") and **lottery preferences** (investors overpay for high-vol, high-skew lottery-like assets). High-beta beat low-beta before 1963 but not after, tracking value's beta sign-flip from +0.35 pre-1963 to −0.28 post (ch.12.4).
- **Illiquidity (Ilmanen ch.19).** An asset's ex-ante excess return over a more-liquid benchmark; less-liquid assets need a break-even cushion for extra trading cost *plus* a premium on top. Across stocks, illiquid names (low past turnover, high ILLIQ, high liquidity-beta) earn higher returns and are typically small-cap value with low prices; sell-side (broker) illiquidity matters more than buy-side for pricing. **Time-varying:** liquidity premia widen when liquidity suppliers are scarce and in crises (flight-to-liquidity), compress when capital is abundant. Aggregate illiquidity weakly predicts higher medium-term returns (stronger combined with value/vol signals), strongest in small caps. Harvest: long-horizon investors have a natural edge *selling* liquidity they don't need — but should act *contrarian* (harvest when premia are wide, post-crisis) and not overestimate their own liquidity needs.

**Diversifying across factors (Ilmanen ch.12.6, 14.4, 16.1).** **Value and momentum are natural opposites** (buy-after-falls vs buy-after-rises) and tend to be **negatively correlated** — combining them sharply cuts portfolio vol and lifts Sharpe; "when value and momentum clash, value almost always loses first" (be patient with value signals). Carry and dividend-yield overlap with value (poor diversifiers); **quality, net-share-issuance, and momentum are good value complements**; long-run reversal ≈ value, short-run reversal ≈ liquidity provision. Crucially, **momentum/CTAs are empirically "long volatility / long gamma"** (payoff like a long straddle, profitable in 1998 & 2008) while value, carry, vol-selling and credit are "short volatility" — so **adding trend is a crucial diversifier of the short-vol cluster**. Negatively-correlated style pairs (value+momentum, carry+trend, equity+Treasuries) are "super-diversifiers" worth adding even at slight expected-return cost (ch.28.2.4).

**Characteristics vs covariances.** Both Ilmanen (ch.5.2.2, 16.1) and QEPM (ch.3) note the empirical-vs-theoretical factor-model debate: *characteristics* (book/price, size, carry, duration, rating) are directly observable and often *less noisy* than estimated factor *betas*, and empirically the characteristic (e.g. high B/P) carries a higher premium than the covariance (beta to VMG) — which leans behavioral, though rationalists argue the characteristic is just a noisy proxy for an imperfectly-estimated factor beta. Both approaches tend to work in practice. One CAPM insight survives universally: only **systematic (undiversifiable) risk is priced**; idiosyncratic risk earns nothing — yet the empirical beta-return line across stocks is flat-to-inverted (Ilmanen ch.5.1-5.2.2; Box 8.1).

**Fama-French / Carhart.** The FF 3-factor model says excess return loads on **market beta, size (SMB), and value (HML)** (QuantTrading, p.134). FF5 adds **RMW** (profitability) and **CMA** (investment). Adding momentum gives the Carhart 4-factor. Exposures (betas) are estimated by rolling OLS of asset excess returns on the factors (e.g., `PandasRollingOLS` on Mkt-RF, SMB, HML, RMW, CMA) and the past exposures themselves can be used as features (Jansen, p.191-192). Fama-French value and size explain the cross-section better than market beta; momentum is often added as a fourth, return-driven factor (Ilmanen ch.5.2.2). Noguer (*Quantitative Portfolio Optimization*, ch.6) frames the same as APT — the noiseless factor model `e = E + Bf` — with FF3 as the OLS baseline and a "factor zoo" of 300+ factors (Cochrane's warning).

**The anomaly canon & how anomalies are vetted (Coqueret & Guida ch.3).** The unifying frame is **APT (Ross 1976):** `r_{t,n} = α_n + Σ_k β_{n,k} f_{t,k} + ε_{t,n}` (eq.3.1), which generalizes the single-factor CAPM (market only); each *extra priced factor* is precisely why these are called **"anomalies"** (conditional, time-varying-β models are more realistic). The standard long-short factors (Ken French / AQR libraries, formed as extreme-portfolio winners−losers): **SMB** size, **HML** value (high−low book-to-market), **WML/MOM** momentum (past ~12m, skip last month), **RMW** profitability (robust−weak), **CMA** investment (conservative−aggressive asset growth), **BAB** betting-against-beta. Detection/vetting toolkit (ch.3.2):
- **Portfolio sorts** — rank firms on a criterion → form `J` (2/3/5/10) equal- or cap-weighted buckets → `t`-test top vs bottom future returns. Weakness: misses *non-monotonic* effects, and the optimal #portfolios is likely >10 (Cattaneo 2020). (The same unidimensional-sort test as QEPM §5 and Tortoriello's decile method §4.)
- **Fama-MacBeth (1973)** — two-pass. (1) time-series regress each stock on the factors → `β̂`; (2) cross-sectional regress returns on `β̂` **date-by-date** `r_{t,n} = γ_{t,0} + Σ_k γ_{t,k} β̂_{n,k} + ε` (eq.3.4), where `γ̂_{t,k}` is factor *k*'s premium at *t*; test the time-averaged premium `γ̂_k = (1/T)Σγ̂_{t,k}`.
- **Factor competition / redundancy (eq.3.5)** — regress each factor on all the others `f_{t,k} = a_k + Σ_{j≠k} δ_{k,j} f_{t,j} + ε`; a **significant intercept `a_k`** ⇒ the factor is *not* spanned by the others (non-redundant). Famously, **HML is redundant given Mkt/SMB/RMW/CMA** (FF 2015, reproduced in the book, ch.3.2.5) — the workhorse test for pruning a factor combo down to its independent bets.
- **The factor zoo (ch.3.2.6)** — publication bias / p-hacking inflate the published count; remedies: higher `t`-stat hurdles, multiple-testing corrections, **LASSO + Fama-MacBeth** to score a candidate's *marginal* value (Feng et al. 2020 — most 2010s factors add little), bootstrap on orthogonalized factors (Harvey & Liu), and PCA-based three-pass premia (Giglio & Xiu). Optimal #factors is contested: 3-5 (classic) vs 15+ (DeMiguel, Freyberger, Kozak) vs a few **latent** factors (Kelly 2019) — directly extending QEPM/Robbins' "parsimony survives" and Noguer's Cochrane warning.
- **Factors vs characteristics (ch.3.3)** — Daniel & Titman (1997) show high-B/M or small firms earn more *even with negative HML/SMB loadings* ⇒ intrinsic **characteristics**, not factor *exposures*, drive returns; later work (Chordia 2019; Han 2019 penalized; Kelly 2019 latent factors with characteristic-dependent loadings) is the empirical backbone for **using raw characteristics directly as ML features** (the panel view, §1). This is the same characteristics-vs-covariances debate Ilmanen and QEPM raise above, settled in favour of characteristics-as-features for ML.

**Chan's factor decomposition (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading* ch.6).** For stocks, **total return = market return + factor returns**; a cross-sectional **dollar-neutral** portfolio cancels the market return, leaving return driven by factors — fundamental (earnings growth, book-to-price), statistical (PCA), or macro (GDP, inflation, the latter mainly for futures: total = spot + roll). Two equity **forced-flow** factors he documents: a **mutual-fund PRESSURE factor** (Eq.6.1: net % of holding funds with >5% NAV inflow/outflow) → long top-decile / short bottom-decile ≈ 17% APR pre-cost; *front-running* predicted flows adds ~17%; post-pressure *mean reversion* adds ~7%; combined ~41% pre-cost (needs CRSP data ~$10k/yr) — and a **news-sentiment factor** (RavenPack etc.): long positive- / short negative-sentiment-change stocks → APR 52-156%, Sharpe 3.9-5.3 pre-cost (evidence that slow news diffusion *causes* stock momentum). Both are cross-sectional long-short factor portfolios in the §2 mold.

**Caveat — time-series vs cross-sectional.** Regressing next-day returns *on* the FF time-series factors is a weak-to-negative predictor out-of-sample (in-sample CAGR 242%/Sharpe 3.7 collapses to negative OOS; MachineTrading, p.31-32). The robust use is **cross-sectional**: at each date, fit one linear model of stock returns on size-independent fundamental exposures. A 27-factor SPX model with a tiny in-sample R²=0.015 still delivers OOS CAGR 12.3% / Sharpe 1.7 / maxDD 7.6%; throwing in 112 size-*dependent* factors pushes in-sample R² to 0.96 but turns OOS negative (overfit) (MachineTrading, p.34-37). Lesson: **more factors ≠ better; parsimony survives, over-fit dies.** Value factors are largely useless on SPX large-caps but work on small-caps (MachineTrading, p.37-39). Robbins (*Quantitative Asset Management*, ch.11) and QEPM (ch.2.5) echo this: *adding factors raises R² but not IR* — parsimony; maximize predictor *importance AND dissimilarity*, a small theory-motivated set beating data-mined thousands (Robbins ch.7). Robbins also urges treating economic factors as **regime identifiers** (lagged/infrequent), not direct predictors, and organizing factors as a *tree, one layer per dimension* (cycle/geography/sector/instrument), where a terminating branch means "buy the index one level up" (ch.7).

## 3. Formulaic alphas (Kakushadze)

Kakushadze's *101 Formulaic Alphas* express each signal as a short formula over a fixed **operator grammar** applied to OHLCV and a few derived fields (`returns`, `vwap`, `cap`, `adv{d}` = average dollar volume over *d* days). The full operator dictionary lives in **file 10** (cross-sectional `rank`, time-series `ts_rank`, `delta`, `delay`, `decay_linear`, `correlation`, `covariance`, `scale`, `signedpower`, `ts_min/ts_max/ts_argmax`, conditional `?:`, and the cross-sectional neutralizers `indneutralize`). The shared building blocks are:
- `rank(x)` — cross-sectional rank to [0,1] (the core normalizer).
- `delta(x, d)` — change over *d* days; `delay(x, d)` — value *d* days ago.
- `correlation(x, y, d)` / `covariance(x, y, d)` — rolling over *d* days.
- `decay_linear(x, d)` — linearly-weighted moving average (recency-weighted).
- `ts_rank(x, d)`, `ts_argmax(x, d)` — time-series rank / position of the max.
- `scale(x, a)` — rescale so Σ|x| = a (dollar-neutralizing normalizer).
- `indneutralize(x, g)` — demean *x* within industry/sector group *g*.

**Representative alphas** (spanning mean-reversion and momentum; numbering per Kakushadze):

| # | Formula (schematic) | Type |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | `rank(ts_argmax(signedpower(returns<0 ? stddev(returns,20) : close, 2), 5)) − 0.5` | reversal |
| 2 | `−1 · correlation(rank(delta(log(volume),2)), rank((close−open)/open), 6)` | volume/price reversal |
| 3 | `−1 · correlation(rank(open), rank(volume), 10)` | reversal |
| 4 | `−1 · ts_rank(rank(low), 9)` | reversal |
| 6 | `−1 · correlation(open, volume, 10)` | reversal |
| 9 | `(delta(close,1)>0 && delta(close,1) past) ? delta(close,1) : −delta(close,1)` (sign-conditioned) | momentum/reversal |
| 12 | `sign(delta(volume,1)) · (−1 · delta(close,1))` | reversal |
| 21 | mean/stddev threshold of `close` over 8 vs 2 days, volume-confirmed `?:` cascade | mean-reversion |
| 33 | `rank(−1 · (1 − open/close))` | intraday reversal |
| 41 | `(high·low)^0.5 − vwap` | value-vs-price |
| 53 | `−1 · delta(((close−low)−(high−close))/(close−low), 9)` | reversal |
| 101 | `(close − open) / (high − low + 0.001)` | intraday momentum |

The recurring template is *negated, ranked correlation/delta of price against volume over a short window* — i.e., short-horizon **mean reversion**, with a minority of pure-momentum forms.

**Empirical facts** (Kakushadze, *101 Formulaic Alphas*):
- **Holding periods are short:** 0.6 to 6.4 trading days.
- **Low cross-alpha correlation:** average pairwise correlation ≈ **15.9%** — this is why a large basket diversifies well into a combo.
- **Returns correlate with volatility, not turnover:** higher-volatility alphas earn more; turnover does *not* explain returns (so chasing turnover is unrewarded).

## 4. Tortoriello's empirically-tested equity factors

Tortoriello (*Quantitative Strategies for Achieving Alpha*) back-tests single factors and combinations on US equities, reporting **average annual excess return of the top quintile (Q1) and bottom quintile (Q5)** plus consistency (% of rolling periods the factor worked) and Sharpe. The recurring lesson: **profitability/cash-flow/capital-allocation factors are strongest when *paired with a valuation factor*; raw balance-sheet levels mostly do not work over a 12-month horizon.** Sign convention throughout: a *good* factor shows a positive, monotonic Q1−Q5 spread.

**Profitability (ch.4) — works well as a group; strongest paired with valuation; Q1 skews large-cap:**
- **Return on Invested Capital (ROIC)** = NOPAT / (common equity + LT debt + preferred + minority interest). **Q1 +2.3%, Q5 −4.3%**; consistent across all sectors except telecom/utilities; strong in bear markets. (NOPAT = op profit − special items − cash op taxes.)
- **EBITDA − capex to Invested Capital** — the author's preferred *simpler* ROIC: **Q1 +2.6%, Q5 −4.5%**, slightly more consistent than ROIC. EBIT/IC and EBITDA-alone work nearly identically — "the choice is academic."
- **Return on Equity** = (income before extras − pref divs)/avg common equity. Simple and works: **Q1 +2.2%, Q5 −3.6%**.
- **Return on Assets** does **NOT** work standalone for Q1 (assets too broad); but **Q5 −3.0%** is useful — the ROE+ROA combo is a strong *negative*/short test (**Q5 −8.3%**).
- **Economic Profits** (crude EVA) = ROIC − WACC. Progressive refinement: basic (Q1 +2.7%/Q5 −0.9%) → **drop Beta** (Q5 → −1.4%) → **substitute P/S for Beta** (Q1 +3.2%/Q5 −3.2%) → **cash-ROIC + P/S-for-Beta** is strongest (**Q1 +5.1%, Q5 −5.5%, Sharpe 0.87**). Since replacing Beta with P/S consistently improved results, "Beta is a poor proxy for risk" (WACC cost-of-equity used RFR 7.5% + Beta×ERP 9%).
- **Best profitability+valuation combo:** Economic Profits (cash-ROIC/PS) + EV/EBITDA → **Q1 +8.1%, Q5 −6.0%, Sharpe 0.98** (uses all three statements + price). EBITDA−capex/IC + FCF/P → Q1 +7.8%/Q5 −5.3%.

**Cash Flow (ch.6) — among the top-3 predictive *basic* factor groups; FCF = operating cash flow − capex; flaw: standalone edge decayed post-2003 (fix by pairing with valuation):**
- **FCF / Operating Income** (earnings-quality): **Q1 +5.0%, Q5 −3.8%**; works across sectors except financials.
- **Cash ROIC** = FCF / invested capital (profitability through a cash lens): **Q1 +5.0%, Q5 −5.9%**, linear, large-cap, Sharpe 0.83.
- **FCF + net buyback + dividend to Price** (cash / capital-allocation / valuation hybrid): Q1 +5.1%/Q5 −5.2%; a building block for capital-allocation combos.
- **Strong combos:** FCF/OI + Acquisitions-to-IC (Q1 +5.9%/Q5 −8.8% — **avoid acquirers**); FCF/OI + External-Financing (Q5 −14.4%, 100% of 3-yr windows); FCF/OI + P/S (Q1 +7.9%); cash-ROIC + capex/PP&E (Q5 −12.9%); FCF+div-yield + ROE (Q1 +6.7%/Q5 −9.7%).

**Growth (ch.7) — hard to capture quantitatively alone; shines combined with valuation:**
- **FCF per Share Score** (70% 3-qtr growth index + 30% linearity over 10 qtrs): moderate **Q1 +3.5%, Q5 −2.7%**; large-cap, Sharpe 0.81 (1-yr FCF/sh growth alone works nearly as well). Combo FCF-score + EV/EBITDA → **Q1 +8%+, Sharpe 1.03 — the highest two-factor result in the book.**
- **EPS Score** (80% modified-RSI earnings trend + 20% linearity, 10 qtrs): weak standalone (Q1 +1.9%, Q5 −0.3%) but strong in valuation combos.
- **Two-Year Avg Capex/Share Growth** — used *in reverse* (low growth = Q1): **Q1 +2.1%, Q5 −3.1%**. A "Goldilocks" factor — too-fast capex growth → underperformance (becoming capital-intensive near cycle peaks). Combo with FCF/P → strong, linear Q1.

**Capital Allocation (ch.8) — how cash is deployed matters as much as how much is earned:**
- **Net Share Repurchase to IC**: Q1 +2.5%, **Q5 −4.9%**; large-cap Q1.
- **One-Year Reduction in Shares Outstanding** (cleaner than buyback-cash, strips option-dilution offset): **Q1 +3.1%, Q5 −5.2%**, linear; strongest in info tech (Q1 +7%/Q5 −8%).
- **One-Year Reduction in Long-Term Debt**: Q2 (not Q1) best at +2.7%; **Q5 −2.8% (88% consistent)** — over-borrowers underperform. *Raw debt-level factors do NOT work* (the market rewards leverage/risk over a 12-month horizon).
- **External Financing to Assets** = net (share+debt issuance − buybacks − debt reduction + Δshort-term debt)/assets. **Q1 +3.1%, Q5 −6.7%**; strong across sectors; combines with *every* major basic factor. Issuers of shares/debt consistently underperform.
- **Three-Year Avg Capex to Invested Capital** (spending *adequacy*): Q2 +1.9%/Q5 −1.3% — under-spenders fall behind; combo with FCF/P → Q1 +5.7%/Q5 −7.1%, Sharpe 0.96.
- **Acquisitions**: large cash acquisitions are a clear negative (bottom-quintile combos −8.8%+); **avoid acquirers**.

**Takeaways for building a multifactor model (Tortoriello).** (1) The most reliable single signals are **FCF/price, EV/EBITDA, ROIC / EBITDA−capex-to-IC, economic-profit (ROIC−WACC), and external-financing/share-reduction**. (2) Every strong result is a **valuation × quality (or × cash-flow / × capital-allocation)** *pairing* — value alone and quality alone each leave alpha on the table; together they reach Sharpe ~0.9-1.0 at two factors. (3) The bottom quintile (Q5) is often the *cleaner* signal (a better short / avoid list) than the top — external-financing, acquisitions, and ROA shorts are especially strong. (4) Substitute **price-to-sales for Beta** in any cost-of-capital construction ("Beta is a poor proxy for risk"). These directly corroborate Ilmanen's value×quality and value×momentum super-diversifier pairings (§2) and Koller's *Valuation* quality lens (§ below).

**Koller et al. (*Valuation*) — the fundamentals behind the factors (cross-ref T04).** A principled **quality factor** ranks on **ROIC** (NOPAT/invested capital, from *reorganized* statements — exclude excess cash, treat goodwill consistently) and especially on the **economic-profit spread (ROIC − WACC) and its persistence**, since a sustained ROIC > WACC is the accounting fingerprint of a durable competitive advantage (p.97, 208-210, 302). Industry structure dominates, so **neutralize/control for industry** when ranking. A principled **value factor** is **intrinsic DCF value vs market price**, or **EV/invested-capital relative to ROIC** (and **EV/EBITA on forward, normalized earnings**), rather than raw P/E (corrupted by leverage). The **quality×value interaction** is not a trade-off: the value-driver math says a high-ROIC, low-growth firm can deserve the same or higher multiple as a low-ROIC, high-growth firm (e.g. Ralph Lauren 23% ROIC vs Skechers 12% at the same 10× EV/EBITA) — so they must be combined, not netted (p.88-92). Avoid factors built on conservation-of-value illusions (buyback optics, accounting changes, EPS accretion from deals) — they do not move cash flows (p.99-101).

## 4b. Quantitative Value's value+quality stack (Gray & Carlisle)

Gray & Carlisle (*Quantitative Value*) run an explicit empirical horse race to fix the *winning value multiple*, then stack quality on top — a concrete, backtested instance of the value×quality pairing Tortoriello, Koller and Ilmanen all converge on. (Statement-level definitions of the metrics live in **file 14** — this entry keeps the factor-construction and performance findings.)

**The price horse race — the central finding (ch.7).** Across 1964-2011, on market-cap-weighted value deciles, the **EBIT variant of the enterprise multiple (EBIT/TEV, i.e. the "acquirer's multiple") wins decisively** on every metric — raw CAGR, value-vs-glamour spread, CAPM alpha, Sharpe, Sortino, and drawdown. All multiples expressed in **yield form** (so high = cheap = good). Value-decile CAGR / value-glamour spread / CAPM alpha:
- **EBIT/TEV — 14.55% / 7.45% / 5.23%** (winner on every metric; lowest worst drawdown 37.25%; Sharpe 0.58, Sortino 0.89).
- EBITDA/TEV — 13.72% / 6.17% / 4.46%.
- **Gross-profits yield (GP/TEV) — 13.51% / 6.09% / 4.03%** ("best clean number" — the cleanest single quality-tinged value metric).
- Book-to-market (the academic favorite) — 13.11% / 4.49% / 4.09% (*worst risk-adjusted*; Sharpe 0.33; worst DD 50.21%).
- Earnings yield (E/M) — 12.44% / 4.67%.
- Free-cash-flow yield — lowest spread 2.63%, lowest alpha 2.45%.
- **Forward analyst-estimate yield — worst, 8.63% CAGR, *underperforms* the S&P 500** — "leave forward estimates to the promoters on Wall Street."

`TEV = market cap + total debt − excess cash + preferred + minority interests` (excess cash = cash + current assets − current liabilities). Rationale for EV/EBIT: it is **capital-structure-neutral** and built on a hard-to-manipulate operating number. The deeper lesson: **glamour (expensive) deciles are uniformly disastrous regardless of which ratio** — *avoiding glamour matters more than picking the perfect value ratio*. And **normalized/composite ratios add little** (ch.8): Graham/Shiller multi-year "normalized" earnings and O'Shaughnessy sum-of-ranks composites were tested exhaustively with weak/mixed benefit; **single-year EBIT/TEV still wins** after adjusting for complexity. "Greenblatt nailed the correct price ratio" — prefer simplicity.

**Quality on top of cheapness (ch.11-12).** Adopt **Piotroski's *implementation* idea** — within the cheap cohort, separate winners from losers on quality (franchise strength + financial strength; the **Piotroski F-score** is the canonical financial-strength screen, with gross profitability as the franchise-quality measure — full metric detail in file 14). Pipeline: clean universe → rank cheapest on EBIT/TEV → within the value decile **keep the highest-quality names**. Crucially, here **quality *adds* value** — unlike the Magic Formula's ROC, which actually *detracts* (the EBIT multiple alone beat the full Magic Formula). Weighting favors **price over quality** because single-year quality mean-reverts fast (echoing Tortoriello's "single-year quality mean-reverts" and the price>quality weighting).

**Final Quantitative Value performance, 1974-2011 (ch.12):**
- **CAGR 17.68%** vs Magic Formula 13.94%, S&P 500 TR 10.46%, equal-universe MW index 10.80%.
- **Sharpe 0.74, Sortino 1.18** (vs MF 0.55/0.80; S&P 0.37/0.56); std dev 16.81% (slightly below MF).
- **Worst drawdown −32.06%** (vs MF −36.85%, S&P −50.21%) — best capital preservation, the source of most of the edge.
- Beat MF in 91% of rolling 5-yr / 99% of rolling 10-yr windows; beat S&P ~94% / 99%.
- **Alpha ≈ 6-8%/yr robust across CAPM, FF-3-factor, Carhart 4-factor (+MOM), and 5-factor (+Pástor-Stambaugh liquidity).** Loadings: MKT ~0.85, HML +0.23 (by design), SMB −0.14; MOM/LQD insignificant — i.e. the outperformance is **not explained by known factors**. Beat Sequoia, Legg Mason Value Trust, and Third Avenue net of fees, winning mainly via downside protection.

**Universe / construction conventions** (a reusable value-factor template): NYSE/AMEX/Nasdaq common equity; **exclude REITs, ADRs, closed-end funds, utilities, financials**; market-cap floor at the **40th-percentile NYSE breakpoint** (~$1.4B end-2011) for liquidity; **annual rebalance**; **market-cap-weighted** (equal-weight gives flattering, less honest numbers); fundamentals **lagged 6 months**. (The 40th-pct NYSE breakpoint and lag-the-fundamentals discipline match Quantitative Momentum's §12 conventions.)

## 5. The QEPM factor-model engine & z-score compositing

Chincarini & Kim (*Quantitative Equity Portfolio Management*) give the most explicit recipe for turning fundamentals into a tradeable score. Both factor-model flavors rest on `expected return = factor exposure × factor premium`, with the full model `r_i = α_i + β_i1·f_1 + … + β_iK·f_K + ε_i`, where `β·f` is nondiversifiable (factor) risk and `ε_i` is diversifiable (specific) risk (ch.7).

**The two factor-model approaches (ch.3, ch.7):**
- **Fundamental factor model:** factor exposures `β` are *directly observable* (e.g., the B/P ratio read off statements / characteristics); the **factor premium `f` is estimated** by *cross-sectional / panel regression* of stock returns on exposures, each period.
- **Economic (macro) factor model:** the factor premium `f` comes *first* — for macro factors the variable's value itself is the premium (e.g., 3% inflation; allow a ~1-month reporting lag; market premium = index total return − 1-mo T-bill); for fundamental/technical factors build a **zero-investment portfolio** (rank, long top 33% equal-weighted minus short bottom 33%, premium = long − short, rebalanced no more often than the characteristic updates); or via **PCA** (diagonalize `Σ = Q D Q'`, premia `f_k = q_k'r` from the top-K eigenvectors). Then each stock's **exposure `β` is estimated** by a *time-series regression* of its returns on the premiums (SE from `σ̂²_ε·(F'F)⁻¹`).
- **Equivalence (Lemma 1):** if expected return is a *linear* function of fundamental exposure, the two models are equivalent and yield identical portfolios. If the relation is non-linear, prefer the economic model (stronger theory; the cross-sectional regression is otherwise misspecified). **Bolting an ad-hoc Z-score overlay onto a basic model (a "hybrid") usually violates the information criterion (double counting) and costs measurable Sharpe.** Choosing between them (ch.3.6): the economic model wins on theory and factor flexibility (only it handles GDP, inflation); the fundamental model wins on ease of implementation and lower historical-data requirement.

**Factor categories (ch.4).** Seven **fundamental** sub-categories: (1) **Valuation** — B/P, E/P, P/S, P/CF, P/EBITDA, dividend yield, PEG, PEGY; (2) **Size** — market cap, common equity, total assets; (3) **Operating efficiency** — inventory/total/equity/fixed-asset turnover; (4) **Operating profitability** — gross/operating/net margin, ROA, ROCE, ROTC, and their *growth*; (5) **Solvency** — current/quick/cash ratio, CFO ratio; (6) **Financial risk** — debt-to-equity, interest coverage (ICR); (7) **Corporate activity** — buybacks, insider purchases, R&D. Plus **technical** (liquidity: turnover, Amihud & invariance illiquidity; price-based: momentum, MAs, Bollinger bands, beta; volume-based: short-interest), **economic/macro** (GDP growth, yield-curve slope, unemployment, inflation — mind release lags; ideally use *unanticipated innovations*), and **alternative** (analyst ratings/revisions, alternative big data, ESG). Vetted fundamental/technical factors tend to have the best out-of-sample predictive power (ch.7.2).

**Factor selection & testing (ch.4.6) — all theory-first:**
- **Univariate panel regression** of returns on one factor — a significant premium `f` means it explains returns. Cheap first-pass, but surrogates (P/E vs P/B) look redundantly significant.
- **Multiple panel regression** — keeps factor interactions; drop insignificant factors. Danger: misspecification bias if too many — keep < ~10.
- **Unidimensional zero-investment portfolio** — rank into quintiles/deciles, long top − short bottom, test mean spread ≠ 0 via `t = mean(r)/se(r)` vs t-dist with T−1 df; **rule of thumb: |t| > 2 ⇒ significant**.
- **Multidimensional zero-investment portfolio** — rank on several factors jointly (e.g. 10×10 = 100 buckets), test joint significance.
- **Reducing factors:** the **within-correlation coefficient** (correlation after demeaning each period) and **Kendall's τ** (rank correlation) flag near-redundant factors (≈ ±1 ⇒ redundant).
- **Avoid data mining:** stepwise/sequential specification search inflates t-stats and lacks theory; adjust significance (**Bonferroni / Bayesian**) when testing many factors (App.4B).

**Z-scoring / standardizing exposures (ch.3.4, ch.5).** A z-score is a standardized factor exposure: for factor k and stock i, `z_{i,k} = (β_{i,k} − mean_k) / S(β_k)`, using the *cross-sectional* mean and std across the universe. Output: mean 0, std 1, scale-independent, comparable across factors (a z of 2 = 2 std above average; under normality, <2.27% of stocks score higher). **Sign convention is critical** — orient every factor so high = good (use B/P not P/E; multiply detrimental factors' z by −1) *before* aggregating.

**Aggregate z-score (ch.5.4).** Because z-scores are scale-independent, **sum them**: `Z_i = (1/K) Σ z_{i,k}` (equal-weight, common for stability). Weighting options: equal; ad-hoc by belief/style or by each factor's historical IR; or **optimal** via panel/cross-sectional regression of returns on z-scores, `r_{i,t+1} = γ_i + Σ δ_k z_{i,k,t} + ε`, where the δ's are the optimal weights (uses the z-score/return covariance, unlike equal-weight). Organize into **factor groups/composites** (valuation, profitability, financial-soundness, technical) with intra- and inter-group weights. **Outliers:** winsorize z-scores to ±3 (or IQR / rank / percentile-rank, App.5B). Convert to expected return by regression `E[r] = γ + δ·Z`; the z-score model equals the fundamental factor model when the factor premium is inversely proportional to exposure std (ch.5.5.3). This **z-score composite (Q-score vs Z-score)** is the bridge to construction (ch.9): the aggregate **Z-score** feeds ad-hoc tilts and stratified selection; the model's **forecast expected return / α (the "Q" approach)** feeds MVO and tracking-error optimization. (For data-scarce names — mergers: cap-weighted average of the two firms' β's; IPOs: **characteristic matching** — find firms with `(z_i−z_j)'(z_i−z_j) < e` and average their exposures; ch.7.5.)

## 6. Alpha evaluation

**Information Coefficient (IC).** The headline statistic is the **Spearman rank correlation** between the factor's predictions and realized forward returns (`scipy.stats.spearmanr`), computed each period and averaged (Jansen, p.211-221). It is non-parametric, so it is robust to outliers and to the monotone-but-nonlinear factor→return relation. **An IC of 0.05-0.10 is meaningful** given enough breadth.

**Risk-adjusted IC / IC stability.** `risk-adjusted IC = mean(IC) / std(IC)`, tested with a one-sample t-test (H0: IC = 0). A factor with a modest mean IC but *stable* sign across periods can dominate one with a higher but erratic IC.

**IC decay.** Compute IC at multiple forward horizons (1, 5, 10, 21 days). A factor whose IC decays slowly tolerates longer holds and lower turnover; fast decay forces frequent (costly) rebalancing.

**Fundamental law of active management.** `IR ≈ IC · √breadth` — information ratio scales with skill (IC) times the square root of the number of independent bets per period. Breadth is why cross-sectional equity strategies (hundreds of names) can post high IR from a tiny IC. This is one of the most-cited results across the new sources, with important qualifications:
- **Grinold & Kahn (*Active Portfolio Management*)** — the origin. The companion forecasting identity is **`alpha = volatility · IC · score`** (p.382): a refined alpha is the residual volatility times the IC times the standardized score. The natural alpha scale is therefore `Std{a} ≈ volatility · IC` (e.g. IC=0.05 × 30% residual vol ⇒ ~1.5% alpha scale); if input alphas have the wrong scale, rescale them. The Muller test (IC=0.1, breadth 500 ⇒ ex-ante IR 2.24) shows QP construction gets closest to the theoretical IR while screens sometimes go negative — and *no* method reaches 2.24 because constraints shrink the effective IC.
- **QEPM (ch.2.5)** — `IR = IC · √BR`. Raise IR via more-*significant* factors (higher IC) or more *uncorrelated* factors (higher BR). The authors stress it is for *understanding*, not *doing*, QEPM, and it holds only when the manager builds the *optimal* portfolio (then IR² ≈ R² of the return-forecasting regression; BR = number of explanatory variables; IC² = average contribution of each variable to R²).
- **Ilmanen (ch.28.2.4)** — weekly views give ~√52× the IR of annual views *if skill holds*, but **trading costs and correlated trades erode this**; the realistic edge is much smaller than the √breadth multiplier suggests. (Robbins, *Quantitative Asset Management* ch.11, names the gap between risk-adjusted-alpha IC and realized performance the **transfer coefficient**, and blames *alpha/risk-factor misalignment* — especially bolting on a third-party risk model built for another purpose — more than portfolio-level control.)

**Quantile / tear-sheet analysis** (Alphalens, the standard toolkit; Jansen, p.211-221). Feed `get_clean_factor_and_forward_returns(factor, prices, periods, quantiles)` and read:
- **Mean return by quantile** — a good factor shows a **monotonic spread**, top vs bottom quintile.
- Cumulative return by quantile; violin plots of dispersion; **sector breakdowns** (to catch a factor that is really a sector bet).

**Turnover.** **Factor turnover** = the share of assets entering/leaving a quantile each period (plus rank autocorrelation) — a direct proxy for trading cost. High turnover threatens net returns; stability is preferable (Jansen, p.211-221). This is *the* decisive practical constraint in the ML-factor studies: Jurczenko (*ML for Asset Management*) finds that **turnover constraints (cap ~120-160%/month) are what preserve net alpha** after ~10bp costs — *unconstrained turnover (~259%) destroys it* even with ICs of 4-5% and gross long-short IR up to 2.16. Quantitative Momentum (Gray & Vogel) makes the same trade-off in reverse: momentum premia decay fast, so *more frequent rebalancing = higher gross return* (1-mo hold ≫ 12-mo hold), but friction forces the production system to compromise at **quarterly** rebalancing. Coqueret & Guida (ch.12.3.5) give the bookkeeping: **`Cost_t = Σ|Δw_{t,n}|·c_{t,n}`**, proxy the per-name cost `c` by market cap or bid-ask spread, compute average turnover, annualize TC as a multiple of it (**≈0.005×turnover** is a conservative figure), and deduct from returns → **TC-adjusted Sharpe** (eq.12.4); TCs are routinely ignored in academia but materially erode factor returns.

**Performance ratios.** `Sharpe = E[excess return]/σ(excess return)`, annualized ×√periods (×√12 monthly). Returns violate IID (serial correlation) so Lo (2002) adjustments apply. **IR** is the same statistic measured against a *benchmark* rather than the risk-free rate (Jansen, p.225-227). For comparing two strategies, a **Bayesian Sharpe** (Student-t returns; Normal/Uniform/Exponential priors) yields a full posterior of each SR and of their *difference* → read off P(A > B) directly (Jansen, p.483-486). Coqueret & Guida's metric checklist (ch.12.3) — always reported *vs a benchmark*: mean (**geometric, not arithmetic** — geometric penalizes vol-pumping), vol, downside semi-variance, **VaR/CVaR (expected shortfall)**, max drawdown; factor-based — regress on the market (Jensen's α) then FF3+momentum to see if α *survives*; risk-adjusted — **information ratio** (denominator = tracking error), **Sharpe** (compare two via the **Ledoit-Wolf test**), **MAR** (return/maxDD), Treynor / generalized Treynor.

## 7. Alpha combination

Given many evaluated alphas, blend them into the combo (151Strategies, p.59):

- **Equal-weight.** Average the per-factor *ranks* (Jansen, p.209-210), or sum oriented z-scores (QEPM ch.5.4). Simplest, hardest to overfit, surprisingly strong; QEPM notes equal-weighting is common precisely for its stability. Chan (*Algorithmic Trading* ch.1) states it sharply: combine factors as Z-scores (`f_i` → (f−mean)/std), sign each by historical correlation, sum to predict R (Eq.1.2); or rank-and-sign-sum (Eq.1.3) when you only need a *ranking* for a long-short decile portfolio. Quoting Kahneman — "formulas that assign equal weights … are often superior" — and Greenblatt's two-factor **magic formula** (return-on-capital + earnings-yield, equal-weighted ranks) returned 30.8% APR vs 12.4% S&P, 1988-2004. (Linear blending implies linear price prediction *and* linear, scaling-in capital allocation.) Coqueret & Guida (ch.12.2) and DeMiguel (2009) concur: equal-weight is "brutally hard to beat," optimal under high estimation risk/ambiguity — the benchmark every fancier combo must clear.
- **IC-weighted.** Weight each alpha by its (decayed, risk-adjusted) IC — let the more predictive signals speak louder. Ilmanen's normalized predictive-regression form (ch.24.2) is the cleanest statement: each contribution = **β (partial correlation / past skill) × Z (current signal strength)**, with the intercept = sample-mean premium (and can be constrained down toward zero); summing a few binary value/carry/momentum signals is the simpler cousin. **Simple beats complex out of sample** (ch.24.1, 28.2.4).
- **Regression / ML blending.** Fit weights that maximize combined predictive power. The closed-form factor algebra is OLS: `Y = Xa + ε`, `A = (XᵀX)⁻¹XᵀY`; in NumPy, `inv(X.T @ X) @ X.T @ Y` (verify `det(X.T @ X) ≠ 0`; add a ones column for the intercept) (PythonQuants, p.211-213). QEPM's "optimal" z-score weighting (`r_{i,t+1} = γ_i + Σ δ_k z_{i,k,t} + ε`) is the same idea. ML methods learn nonlinear/regularized weightings (Jansen, p.209-210); the modern instance is Jurczenko's **AI multifactor stock picker**: ~200 characteristics → random forest, GBM, neural nets, GLM, lasso/ridge/elastic-net for monthly return prediction, then **ensemble**. Blueprint worth copying: *expanding-window split* (16yr train / 8yr validation / 1yr test, refit annually, weights frozen 12 months); rank-normalize features to [−1,1] cross-sectionally; impute with the cross-sectional median; require ≥60% universe coverage. Variable importance: **technical/momentum factors dominate; balance-sheet fundamentals weak** (echoing MachineTrading's "value useless on large-caps").
- **Penalized & tree-ensemble factor models (Coqueret & Guida).** The supervised-learning panel view (§1) makes alpha combination *literally* a regression/classification fit `y = f(X) + ε` on the characteristic cube. Two families do the heavy lifting: **penalized linear regressions** — ridge, lasso, elastic-net — which both *select* (lasso zeros redundant factors) and *shrink*, the natural cure for the collinear factor zoo; and **tree ensembles** (random forests, **gradient boosting / XGBoost**) which capture **nonlinear factor interactions** a linear model misses (e.g. value only pays *conditional on* quality). A reproducible worked ML backtest (ch.12.6): **XGBoost** (80 trees, η=0.3, depth 4); label = **12-month-forward risk-adjusted return** (return scaled by an exp-quantile of volatility, a Sharpe-like target); train only on the **top/bottom 20% of labels** (Coqueret & Guida 2020), then weight EW over the **above-median-prediction half**; 5-yr training window + a mandatory buffer for the 12-month label. Result: a *modest but real* Sharpe gain over equal-weight (higher return, higher vol, **much higher turnover**) that **survives a 0.005×turnover cost** and beat EW in 10 of 12 years — the same "ML adds a little, but turnover is the binding constraint" conclusion as Jurczenko.
- **Ranking > regression (robustness).** Multi-sort (sort by the strongest factor, take top/bottom quantile, re-sort within by the next factor) is robust to outliers ("high-leverage points") that wreck regression-predicted returns. Sorting *within* groups (industry/size/value) yields factor-neutral portfolios. Reduce collinear factors via AIC/BIC stepwise selection (MachineTrading, p.53-55). This is exactly Tortoriello's decile-combo method (§4) and QEPM's multidimensional zero-investment sort.
- **Correlation-aware.** Because alphas average ~15.9% pairwise correlation, the combo should account for the covariance among signals (down-weight redundant alphas), not just their standalone ICs.

A reality check on the *output* of combination: by zero-sum accounting the average active manager underperforms after costs; winners rarely persist (luck > skill) while extreme *losers* persist (they overtrade) (Harris, p.487-489). The combo must clear realistic costs, not just gross IC.

## 8. Risk models for portfolio construction

A risk model supplies the covariance matrix Σ the optimizer needs. The naive sample Σ̂ is ill-conditioned and produces portfolios worse than equal-weight (Michaud's "error maximization"; ChineseQuant, p.22). **Markowitz's curse** (López de Prado, *AFML* ch.16.3; Noguer p.22): the more correlated the assets, the greater the need to diversify, yet the higher the covariance condition number → the more unstable the inverse → tiny estimation errors produce wildly different weights. You need ≥ N(N+1)/2 IID observations to estimate an N×N covariance non-singularly (50 assets ⇒ ~5y daily data, over which correlations are *not* stable) — which is why even naïve 1/N beats mean-variance out-of-sample (De Miguel 2009). Grinold & Kahn put it bluntly: a 500-stock QP needs 500 vols + 124,750 correlations, and the optimizer will **exploit noisy covariances to fake low risk** ("garbage in, garbage out"). Structured alternatives:

**Statistical / PCA (latent) risk models.** Diagonalize the returns covariance; the **eigenvectors are factor exposures**, top eigenvalues the factors (QuantTrading, p.136; MachineTrading, p.50-53). Construction:
1. Winsorize the daily-return panel; standardize per asset (rows ~N(0,1) — required for PCA) (PythonQuants, p.215-221).
2. `c = np.cov(normP)`; `w, v = np.linalg.eig(c)` → variances `w`, unit PCs `v`.
3. Keep the top *k* PCs as regression features to predict next-day returns.

The **1st PC ≈ "the market"** — ~55% of daily variance in 351 large-caps (2010-18), ~40% in a broader 2000-18 sample; ~10 PCs explain ~60% (Jansen, p.627-629). **Choosing #factors:** use the "elbow" of cumulative explained variance. PCA factors *never get stale* (recomputed in a rolling window, e.g., 252-day) and, unlike fundamental models, work on large-caps (SPX PCA momentum: CAGR 15.6% / Sharpe 1.4 with top-5 eigenvectors; MachineTrading, p.50-53). Limitation: PCA assumes **constant exposures**, ruling out momentum/reversion *as factors*, and on small-caps specific returns can dominate and kill the signal (QuantTrading, p.136-139).

**Eigenportfolios.** Run PCA on the **correlation** matrix of normalized returns; standardize each top PC so weights sum to 1 and use as portfolio weights. PCs are mutually uncorrelated — PC1 tracks the market, higher PCs isolate themes (e.g., a PC heavy on Visa/Mastercard) (Jansen, p.630-631). Tatsat et al. (*ML & DS Blueprints*, ch.7) implement exactly this on DJIA-30: PCA/SVD on the covariance → n uncorrelated principal portfolios → use standardized PCs as weights → backtest each principal portfolio's Sharpe vs the index and pick the best (eigen-portfolio #0 won: return 11.47%, vol 13.31%) — a dimensionality-reduction answer to Markowitz's covariance instability.

**Heterotic risk model (Kakushadze).** A hybrid that improves the pure-statistical model: cluster the universe (industry/sub-industry classification, e.g., BICS — each name in exactly one group; 151Strategies, p.128, 343), build the factor covariance *within* this fundamental clustering, and combine with the statistical factors. This stabilizes the off-diagonal structure that plain PCA estimates noisily, while keeping the data-driven adaptivity.

**Fundamental factor models (APT).** `R = Xb + u`: X = factor exposures (loadings, observable from fundamentals), b = factor returns (common drivers, stock-independent), u = specific/idiosyncratic returns. Factor and specific returns are inferred by **cross-sectional multivariate regression of stock excess returns on exposures, each period** (QuantTrading, p.133-134; identical to QEPM's fundamental model, §5). A good model with 1000 stocks / 50 factors achieves R² ≈ 30-40% (Grinold-Kahn). These are predictive only if **factor returns have momentum** (assume next-period ≈ current) and suffer steep drawdowns when valuation style shifts (growth-back-in-favor late-1990s, Aug/Dec 2007) (QuantTrading, p.135-139). Vendors: Capital IQ, Compustat, MSCI Barra, Northfield, QSG.

**Covariance denoising & shrinkage (the modern toolkit).** Because the optimizer is so sensitive to Σ errors, denoise it before use:
- **Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage** `Σ̂ = δ̂F̂ + (1−δ̂)S` — pull the sample S toward a structured target F at an optimal intensity δ̂ (ChineseQuant, p.22-28; Robbins ch.12, who notes *shrinkage beats resampling out-of-sample*).
- **RMT (random-matrix-theory) denoising** (López de Prado, *Machine Learning for Asset Managers*): the bulk of the empirical eigenvalue spectrum that falls inside the Marčenko-Pastur band is noise — shrink/replace those eigenvalues, keep only the signal eigenvalues, and rebuild Σ. This **detoned/denoised** matrix is far better-conditioned for inversion and is the input López de Prado feeds to his NCO allocator (below). Robbins (ch.12) lists RMT and Cornish-Fisher among the standard robustness fixes.
- **Black-Litterman** — blend an equilibrium prior with views (detailed in §9).

## 9. Portfolio construction & optimization

**Mean-variance (Markowitz/MPT).** `w_eff = argmin wᵀΣw  s.t. wᵀμ = μ*, wᵀ1 = 1` (ChineseQuant, p.21; QEPM ch.9.3; Noguer p.16). With only equality constraints there is a closed form (Lagrangian → `w* = …(Σ⁻¹)…`; QEPM App.9A); sweep `μ_P` from min(μ) to max(μ) to trace the (predictive) **efficient frontier** (Kaufman, p.2133-37). With a risk-free asset this collapses to the CAPM single-fund theorem; the **tangency portfolio maximizes Sharpe**, and maximizing long-horizon CAGR is equivalent — solution `F* = C⁻¹M` (Kelly, normalized to leverage 1) (MachineTrading, p.19-21). Because **covariances are far more predictable than returns**, the **minimum-variance portfolio** is often preferred in practice (the low-vol anomaly; SPLV/USMV) (MachineTrading, p.22). MVO is "error-maximizing" — outlier means/variances dominate (QEPM ch.9.3; Noguer p.22; Robbins ch.12: "optimizers are error maximizers"). **Optimizer input-sensitivity rank (actionable): expected return > risk > covariance** (Robbins ch.12) — so the highest-leverage robustness gains come from *not* estimating returns at all (the motivation for risk-parity, HRP, and Black-Litterman below). Carver (*Systematic Trading* ch.4) distrusts single-period MVO for the same reason — it treats noisy estimated means as known, Sharpe ratios are statistically indistinguishable with realistic data, so a good allocator should over-weight *diversifiers*, not high-SR assets ("optimisation is more art than science").

**Constraints ≡ alpha shrinkage (Grinold & Kahn, Eqs. 14.1-14.2).** Any constrained/complex construction can be replicated by an *unconstrained* mean-variance optimization on **modified alphas** `α' = α − (∂σ/∂h)·Δ` at the appropriate risk aversion. In the Major Market Index example, constraints (no shorts, 5% position cap) shrank alpha std from 2.00% to 0.57% — an effective **62% IC reduction hidden inside the optimizer**. Make this explicit rather than burying it under constraints. The long-only constraint is therefore costly: Grinold & Kahn show long/short (equity market-neutral) benefit comes purely from **relaxing the long-only constraint** (not diversification; the IR·√2 argument), gains largest when the universe is large, asset vol low, and active risk high (p.419-421); Robbins calls long-only "among the most performance-destroying constraints" and urges encoding underweights *at the feature stage* (ch.12).

**Alpha analysis / preprocessing before optimization (Grinold & Kahn, p.381-385).** Refine the alphas first rather than piling on optimizer constraints: (1) **scale** to the natural `volatility·IC` scale; (2) **trim** outliers — examine alphas > 3× scale, zero bad-data ones, pull genuine ones to 3× scale; (3) **neutralize** (below). **Risk-aversion calibration:** `λ = IR / (2·ψ)` (active risk aversion from desired IR and target active risk ψ; IR=0.5, ψ=5% ⇒ λ=0.05; Eq. 14.3); optionally split aversion between common-factor and specific risk to cap single-stock bets and reduce cross-account dispersion (Eq. 14.4).

**Neutralization** (the heart of equity market-neutral construction):
- **Dollar-neutral:** Σ(long $) = Σ(short $); use `scale(x)` so Σ|w| = 1.
- **Beta-neutral:** portfolio β = 0 vs the market, removing the systematic leg.
- **Sector/industry-neutral:** demean the alpha within each group (`indneutralize`) so no sector is a net bet. The workhorse is **weighted regression** — minimize Σ wᵢεᵢ² and take the *residual* as the neutralized alpha (151Strategies, p.49, 57). Industry classification (BICS) provides the binary grouping (each name in exactly one sub-industry/industry/sector; 151Strategies, p.128, 343).
- **Grinold & Kahn's taxonomy (p.383-385):** neutralization is *not* uniquely defined — choose it *a priori*, not by trying all and picking the winner. **Benchmark-neutral** (benchmark alpha = 0 ⇒ optimal portfolio beta = 1, no benchmark timing); **cash-neutral** (no active cash bet; can do both jointly); **industry/risk-factor-neutral** — subtract the cap-weighted industry-average alpha from each alpha in that industry. The principle: **neutralize against any factor you *cannot* forecast**, so the alphas carry only forecastable + specific info. (Coverage gaps: stocks forecast but not in the benchmark → add at zero weight; benchmark names with no forecast → set `α* = α − a{N1}` for covered names and `α=0` for uncovered, Eqs. 14.5-14.6.) Ilmanen (ch.12.2) reports practitioner evidence *favoring* **sector-neutral value** — uncontrolled value books carry persistent industry bets (e.g. early-2008 long financials / short energy) that raise vol; neutrality clearly boosts *risk-adjusted* returns. Koller (§4) gives the fundamental reason: industry structure dominates ROIC, so control for it.

**Bounds & costs** (keep the QP convex and tradeable; ChineseQuant, p.62-64; QEPM ch.9.3.2-9.3.5):
- Position bounds (per-name caps; e.g. the '40-Act 5% rule), trading/turnover bounds, sector caps (`Σ_{sector j} w ≤ cap`), trading-volume caps (`w_i ≤ c·x_i`, keep holdings under a fraction of ADV to limit impact), rebalancing schedule, weighting scheme (151Strategies, p.54, 57, 80, 95). Portfolio weights = dollar holdings ÷ total notional (= |long| + |short|). Inequality constraints kill the closed form → solve by **quadratic programming** (active-set/projection or interior-point).
- **Transaction-cost term** `φᵀ(w⁺ + w⁻)` and buy/sell caps are *benign* (keep convexity). A **cardinality constraint** `Σ 1{wᵢ≠0} = K` (index tracking) makes it a *non-convex* CCQP, solved by truncation heuristics, global smoothing, or local-relaxation clustering.
- Alternative MVO objectives (QEPM): maximize `μ_P` s.t. a risk target, or maximize **risk-adjusted return** `μ_P − A·σ²_P` (A = risk-aversion; A=2 ⇒ 1% more variance must be paid by 2% more return). Don't over-constrain to infeasibility.
- Always sanity-check beyond variance: one rare high-correlation event hides in the stats, so also report **max portfolio loss and VaR** and assume the worst historic loss will be exceeded (Kaufman, p.2133-37). **In a crisis all correlations → 1** — diversification fails exactly when needed (Kaufman, p.2119-28). Grinold & Kahn warn that **covariance errors are uniquely costly *in optimization*** (unlike in plain risk measurement, where they cancel): for cash-vs-market, `Loss = VA*·(1 − σ̂/σ̄)²` (Eq. 14.11) — underestimating a true 17% vol by 5 pts drives value added negative. The lesson is *not* to abandon QP but to get good risk estimates.

**Grinold & Kahn's four construction techniques** (figure of merit `α − λ·ψ² − TC`, Eq. 14.10; p.392-400):
- **Screens** — rank by alpha, take top N, equal/cap-weight. Robust (rank-only), simple, controls turnover via buy/hold/sell list sizes; but ignores non-rank alpha info, has no real risk control, and can be far riskier than imagined.
- **Stratification** — screen within exclusive cells (e.g. 10 sectors × 3 size buckets = 30), matching benchmark weight per cell. Robust to cross-cell alpha bias; risk control only as good as the chosen cells. (QEPM's "stratified sampling," ch.9.6.)
- **Linear programming** — characterize stocks along risk dimensions (industry, size, vol, beta), keep the portfolio close to benchmark on each; handles explicit costs and turnover/position limits; struggles to hit a target stock count.
- **Quadratic programming** — the standard; explicitly trades alpha vs risk vs cost and subsumes LP constraints. **QP wins empirically** (Muller test: highest and most consistent ex-post IRs; screens sometimes went negative). Mean/variance also beats alternative risk measures for most institutional managers — higher moments (skew/kurtosis) are essentially unforecastable, so alternative-risk forecasts reduce to "std forecast + noise" (options are the only real exception).

**QEPM benchmark-relative construction (ch.9.4-9.8), four approaches:**
- **Ad-hoc tilt (ch.9.5):** take the largest-cap N names, nudge weights by Z-score — pick max deviation η, `z_max = max|z_i|`, multiplier `m = η/z_max`, new weight `w_i' = w_i + m·z_i` (Z-scores renormalized to sum 0). Crude — no quantified risk vs index.
- **Stratification (ch.9.6):** partition into J buckets (industry × size = 9×3 = 27); from each, hold `N_j·N_P/N` names, choosing the highest-α/Z within each bucket.
- **Factor-exposure targeting (ch.9.7):** force portfolio factor exposures to match/band the benchmark (`B'w = B'w_B` or ranges); tilt by setting chosen exposures (size, growth) above benchmark while holding others equal. Controls factor risk but not asset-specific risk.
- **Tracking-error minimization (ch.9.8) — the recommended professional method.** TE = `S(r_P − r_B)`. Since `V(r_B)` is fixed, minimize `w'Σw − 2w'γ` (γ = stock-to-benchmark covariances) s.t. `w'μ = μ_B + δ`; equivalently in factor form, squared TE = `(w_P−w_B)'[B V(f) B' + V(ε)](w_P−w_B)`. **Ghost-benchmark tracking** handles unknown benchmark weights by tracking the benchmark's historical return stream. Best formulation = **risk-adjusted TE**: maximize expected excess return s.t. `TE ≤ σ_x`, or maximize `μ_P − A·TE²`; iterate A until the TE budget binds.

**Black-Litterman (Noguer ch.3-4; Robbins ch.12).** Blends CAPM-equilibrium returns `Π = δΣw_M` (reverse-optimized from market caps) with manager views `q = Pμ + ε_q, ε_q~N(0,Φ)` against an equilibrium prior `E = μ + ε_E, ε_E~N(0,τΣ)`. BLUE estimator:
`μ_BL = [(τΣ)⁻¹ + PᵀΦ⁻¹P]⁻¹ · [(τΣ)⁻¹Π + PᵀΦ⁻¹q]`
— a precision-weighted blend of equilibrium and views. Because views propagate through Σ, a few views shift the *whole* return vector, damping MVO's error sensitivity. Use it when you have subjective/relative views and want stable tilts vs. the market; it is the canonical "shrink returns toward equilibrium" tool and overlaps Grinold & Kahn's alpha-refinement. (ChineseQuant, p.22-28 give the same equilibrium-plus-views construction.)

**Risk parity / Equal Risk Contribution (ERC) (Noguer ch.2).** Allocate so each asset contributes equal *risk*, not equal capital (a 60/40 fund is ~93% equity *risk*). Risk contribution `RCᵢ = wᵢ·∂σₚ/∂wᵢ = wᵢ(Σw)ᵢ/σₚ`, and `σₚ = ΣRCᵢ` (Euler). ERC condition: `RCᵢ(w*) = RCⱼ(w*)`. Closed form only for equal correlations: **`wᵢ ∝ 1/σᵢ`** (inverse-vol); the general case needs a numerical solve, e.g. `y* = argmin ½yᵀΣy s.t. Σ ln yᵢ ≥ c`, then `wᵢ = yᵢ/Σyⱼ`. ERC vol sits between GMV and equal-weight: `σ_GMV ≤ σ_ERC ≤ σ_EW`. Only input is Σ (estimated more reliably than returns). Use for multi-asset/ETF sleeves; weaknesses: low raw return (needs leverage) and sensitivity to the *universe* chosen. **Risk parity** (capital ∝ 1/volatility, All Weather) is simpler but causes contagion on vol spikes — Chan suggests targeting equal *maxDD* or VaR/ES rather than equal volatility (MachineTrading, p.22-23).

**Optimization-as-weighted-regression.** Mean-variance optimization with a factor risk model is equivalent to a **weighted regression**: maximizing the objective is the same `minimize Σ wᵢεᵢ²` machinery that performs neutralization (151Strategies, p.49, 57). This unifies "neutralize" and "optimize" into one linear-algebra step — the alpha is regressed against risk factors and the optimal holdings are the (risk- and cost-penalized) residual exposure.

**Regularized / penalized optimization (Jurczenko ch.8).** Treat construction as composable "Lego bricks": objective functions (MVO, GMV, ERC, risk budgeting, most-diversified, max-entropy) × penalties (**ridge, lasso, log-barrier, Shannon entropy**) × constraints (bounds, turnover, tracking error, Herfindahl/effective-bets). MVO alone is a fragile trial-and-error process; **adding penalties improves robustness and avoids corner solutions** — lasso → sparse (few-name) portfolios; log-barrier → risk-budgeting; entropy/Herfindahl → forced diversification. Non-QP objectives are solved at scale by coordinate descent, ADMM, proximal gradient, and Dykstra's algorithm (ch.8.3).

**Constrained-MV with turnover & diversification penalties (Coqueret & Guida eq.12.1-12.3).** The clean closed-form general program: `min ½λ w'Σw − w'μ` s.t. budget, a **turnover penalty** `(w−w₋)'Λ(w−w₋) ≤ δ_R` (Λ scaling per-asset trading cost), and a **Herfindahl diversification cap** `w'w ≤ δ_D`. The closed form (12.3) has **three dials**: **λ** (↑ = risk-focused — usually wise, since risk is far more predictable than returns), **κ_R** (↑ ⇒ stay near current weights, cut turnover/costs), and **κ_D** (↑ ⇒ pull toward 1/N). Setting `κ_R = κ_D = 0` recovers classic MVO. Pedersen et al. (2020) extend it with **shrinkage of Σ toward diagonal and of μ toward an anchor** — the same shrink-the-inputs reflex as Ledoit-Wolf / Black-Litterman. This is the parametric cousin of Jurczenko's penalized "Lego bricks," and its turnover dial is the optimizer-level version of the turnover cap that §6 found preserves net alpha. Coqueret & Guida's broader construction guidance (ch.12.2): **step 1 selection** — don't invest in everything; sort into Q quantile portfolios and trade the signal only if OOS performance is *monotonic in q* (the extreme-portfolio long-short spread is the quick test, dollar-neutral if shorting is allowed); **step 2 weighting** — equal-weight is the hard-to-beat default, with **equal-risk-contribution and constrained min-variance** the advanced options that need only Σ, not expected returns. **Backtest protocol (ch.12.1):** prefer a **rolling window (2-10 yr)** over expanding, with an initial buffer to train the first weights, and choose rebalancing frequency and label horizon coherently (a long-horizon label with monthly/quarterly trading is fine; the reverse is odd).

**HRP — Hierarchical Risk Parity (López de Prado 2016, *AFML* ch.16; Jansen, p.642-648).** Avoids the unstable Σ⁻¹ inversion entirely and works even on **singular** covariance matrices (impossible for CLA/mean-variance). Fixes the three failures of quadratic optimizers — instability, concentration, underperformance. Three stages:
1. **Tree clustering (ch.16.4.1):** distance `d[i,j] = sqrt((1−ρ[i,j])/2)` (a proper metric); a second "distance of distances" (Euclidean distance between columns of D); recursively merge the nearest pair via a linkage criterion (e.g. nearest-point/single-linkage), building an (N−1)×4 linkage matrix.
2. **Quasi-diagonalization (ch.16.4.2):** reorder rows/cols by unwinding the linkage so similar assets sit together and the largest covariances lie on the diagonal — *without a change of basis* (unlike PCA), so you keep working with the original assets.
3. **Recursive bisection (ch.16.4.3):** start all weights = 1; split each ordered list into halves; give each half a cluster variance via **inverse-variance weighting** (`w = diag[V]⁻¹/tr(diag[V]⁻¹)`, `V_cluster = wᵀVw`); set split factor `α = 1 − V₁/(V₁+V₂)`; scale left half by α, right by (1−α); recurse. Guarantees `0 ≤ wᵢ ≤ 1`, `Σw = 1`.

**Why HRP wins (ch.16.5-16.6):** in López de Prado's Monte Carlo (10k runs) HRP delivered *lower out-of-sample variance than CLA* even though min-variance is CLA's own objective — σ²(CLA)=0.1157, σ²(IVP)=0.0928, **σ²(HRP)=0.0671** (~31% better OOS Sharpe than CLA, ~38% lower variance than IVP). CLA concentrated 92.66% in its top-5 holdings (and zeroed 3 assets) and is penalized by *idiosyncratic* shocks; IVP is correlation-blind and penalized by *common* shocks; HRP (62.57% in top-5) diversifies across clusters at every hierarchy level, resisting both. It is the natural way to allocate across multiple ML strategies and a stable replacement for VAR/VECM-type matrix inversions. Caveat from the Jansen backtest: HRP is not *always* best — in one 2015-17 run MV beat it (cum. 207% MV vs 133% EW vs 75% HRP; Sharpe 1.16/1.01/0.83); results are highly sensitive to universe/period. **HRP extensions (Jurczenko ch.9):** (1) cluster on the **lower tail-dependence coefficient (LTDC)** instead of Pearson correlation for better crisis diversification — gives the two smallest max drawdowns (−1.20/−1.29%) and *lower* turnover than correlation-HRP; (2) a **top-down** allocation down the full dendrogram (Ward linkage) rather than de Prado's bottom-ordering recursive bisection. But a block-bootstrap caveat: plain risk parity on *economic* factor clusters matches HRP's downside protection, so HRP's edge is partly path-specific, and correlation-HRP can suffer high turnover from dendrogram instability.

**NCO — Nested Clustered Optimization (López de Prado, *Machine Learning for Asset Managers*).** The successor to HRP: (1) **denoise** the covariance via RMT (§8); (2) **cluster** assets (e.g. via the correlation-distance + clustering used in HRP, choosing cluster count by silhouette/gap); (3) optimize *within* each cluster (intra-cluster MVO on the reduced, well-conditioned block) and *across* clusters (inter-cluster MVO on the cluster-aggregated covariance), then multiply the two weight vectors. By splitting the problem along the natural correlation hierarchy, NCO confines the unstable inversion to small, well-conditioned blocks — combining HRP's stability with explicit expected-return optimization where you trust the inputs.

**Robust-optimization summary (Robbins ch.12; Carver ch.4).** The full robustness menu: **shrinkage** (Ledoit-Wolf; *beats resampling OOS*), **robust / uncertainty-set optimization**, **RMT denoising**, **Cornish-Fisher** (for non-normal tails), **Black-Litterman** (reverse-engineer implied views, then overlay), and **SOCP** for VaR/cardinality constraints; pay only for skill via portable-alpha / core-satellite. Note: *time does NOT diversify* — a longer horizon only narrows the CI of the mean. Carver's two practitioner fixes when you distrust the optimizer: **(1) Bootstrapping** — average the weights from many optimisations over *resampled* history (weights = average of many optimisations, not one optimisation of the average), which naturally shrinks toward equal weight when data is noisy; **(2) Handcrafting** — his preferred manual method: vol-standardize so all assets are equal-vol and initially equal-SR, then build bottom-up by grouping correlated assets and assigning weights from a small lookup table (e.g. 3 assets with correlations 0.0/0.9/0.0 → 27%/46%/27%), flooring negative correlations at 0, multiplying across grouping levels, and optionally adjusting for SR only when *cost* differences are known with certainty. For dynamic trading subsystems, subsystem-return correlations ≈ **0.7 × instrument-return correlations**.

**Parameter uncertainty (NPEB).** The plug-in frontier (sample μ̂, Σ̂) is unreliable; **NPEB** (Lai-Xing-Chen) treats (μ,Σ) as uncertain, solving `max E(wᵀr) − λ·Var(wᵀr)` with the extra variance term `Var(W) = E[Var(W|Rₙ)] + Var[E(W|Rₙ)]` that plug-in ignores, choosing λ on the frontier to **maximize the IR**; it beats plug-in/shrinkage/bootstrap out-of-sample (ChineseQuant, p.25-40).

**Construction-recipe convergence.** Three independent sources land on the same default: when you *don't* trust your return forecasts, **allocate equal volatility (or equal risk) to each return source / factor unless a superior Sharpe or diversification benefit justifies an overweight** (Ilmanen ch.28.2.4; Carver's vol-standardized handcrafting; Noguer's ERC). Minimum-variance / equal-vol / max-diversification portfolios all *ignore* expected-return info — which is a feature when those forecasts are noisy.

## 10. Reinforcement-learning & clustering construction (ML frontier)

Beyond static optimization, the ML literature treats allocation as sequential decision-making and uses unsupervised learning for universe structure (Tatsat et al., *ML & DS Blueprints*):
- **RL portfolio allocation (ch.9).** Treats allocation as *continuous control* with delayed rewards rather than one-shot optimization. MDP: actions = assign/rebalance weights (DQN Q-values → weights); reward = Sharpe ratio; state = rolling correlation matrix; environment = a custom OpenAI-gym-style env over ~5-15 liquid cryptos. Result vs equal-weight: RL Sharpe 0.4445 (small +alpha, β −0.12) beat equal-weight Sharpe −0.5016.
- **Clustering for pairs/universe selection (ch.8).** Reduce the n(n−1)/2 pair-search by clustering first: features per stock = annualized mean return + variance; compare KMeans / agglomerative / affinity-propagation (cluster count via elbow/dendrogram); then run cointegration tests (ADF / Johansen, p<0.05) *within* each cluster → 32 pairs / 47 tickers, surfacing non-obvious cross-sector pairs. (See file 06 for the pairs/stat-arb backtest.)
- **Cointegration hedge-ratio construction (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading* ch.2).** Manufacture *stationary* portfolios from non-stationary legs — a constructed cross-sectional alpha where the weights come from cointegration rather than a factor score. **CADF** (pairs only, Engle-Granger): regress one leg on the other for the hedge ratio, ADF the residual — *order-dependent*, so try both orderings and keep the more-negative t-stat. **Johansen** (any n, order-independent): VECM `ΔY = ΛY(t−1)+…`; the rank r of Λ = number of independent cointegrating portfolios, the **eigenvectors are the hedge ratios**, and the largest eigenvalue gives the shortest half-life. Implementations cap at ~12 symbols, so test stocks individually vs the ETF, then assemble a constrained (positive-weight) long-only basket via genetic algorithm / simulated annealing. (Full mean-reversion backtest in file 06.)
- **CART-based performance attribution (Jurczenko ch.10).** A greedy regression tree picks the variable *and* cutoff that best explain a factor-tilted portfolio's active return, replacing arbitrary Brinson quantile buckets — explaining the majority of tracking error with only 4 terminal nodes. In-sample/ex-post only: an attribution tool, not a predictive signal.

## 11. Practical pipeline: alpha → neutralize → optimize → execute

1. **Alpha generation.** Engineer factors (load → MultiIndex(date,ticker) → resample → multi-period returns → winsorize/clip [1%,99%] → normalize / z-score with correct sign orientation), avoiding the cardinal sin: forward returns are the *labels* (`.shift(-t)`), lagged features the *inputs* (`.shift(t)`) — never mix them (Jansen, p.188-194; QEPM ch.5). Restrict to a realistically tradeable universe (e.g., `QTradableStocksUS`, or Quantitative Momentum's **NYSE 40th-percentile market-cap breakpoint**, ordinary common shares only).
2. **Evaluation.** Run Alphalens: IC, IC decay, quantile spread, turnover, sector breakdown; test single factors and pairings the Tortoriello way (Q1/Q5 spread, consistency, Sharpe). Keep alphas with stable IC and tolerable turnover; confirm `IR ≈ IC·√breadth` *after* costs.
3. **Combination.** Blend into the combo (equal/IC/ML weights, or a z-score composite), correlation-aware, given ~15.9% mean pairwise correlation; lean on negatively-correlated super-diversifier pairs (value×momentum, value×quality, trend overlay).
4. **Risk model.** Build Σ from a statistical (PCA), heterotic, or fundamental factor model; **denoise** via Ledoit-Wolf shrinkage and/or RMT before any inversion; choose #factors via the explained-variance elbow.
5. **Preprocess & neutralize the alphas.** Scale to `volatility·IC`, trim outliers to 3× scale, demean/residualize the combo to dollar-, beta-, and sector-neutral via weighted regression — making the constraints-as-alpha-shrinkage explicit rather than hidden.
6. **Optimize.** Choose by how much you trust the inputs: tracking-error min or QP when you have forecast returns and a clean Σ; ERC / risk parity / min-variance / HRP / NCO when you do not; Black-Litterman to fold in discretionary views. Apply position & turnover bounds and the `φᵀ(w⁺+w⁻)` cost term; calibrate `λ = IR/(2ψ)`; check VaR and max-loss. **Recall the optimizer-sensitivity rank (return > risk > covariance) and that turnover caps are what preserve net alpha.**
7. **Execute.** Decompose the trade into *what / how much / when / at what price* — separable subproblems (AutonomousBidding, p.34-35). For ETFs/baskets, exploit that index spreads are narrower than constituents (Harris, p.315) and that supplying (not demanding) liquidity reduces cost. Rebalance no more often than the signal updates (QEPM) — quarterly is the momentum compromise (Quantitative Momentum).

## 12. Worked construction: Quantitative Momentum (Gray & Vogel)

A complete, concrete equity factor system that instantiates the pipeline — the **12-2 momentum + "frog-in-the-pan" quality** model:

**The 5-step Quantitative Momentum process (ch.8):**
1. **Investable universe** — mid/large-cap US exchange-traded stocks (NYSE/AMEX/NASDAQ, ordinary common shares only; min market cap = **NYSE 40th-percentile breakpoint** at each rebalance; no industry exclusions).
2. **Generic momentum screen** — rank the universe on **12-2 return** (the trailing 12 months *skipping the most recent month*); keep the **top decile (~10%)**.
3. **Momentum quality screen — "frog-in-the-pan" (FIP).** Compute the FIP statistic (smoothness/path-quality of the move) on those names; keep the **smoothest half** (top 50% by FIP; lower/more-negative = a smoother, higher-quality move that drifts rather than jumps). The intuition: a steady "frog-in-the-pan" ascent under-reacts and continues, where a jumpy one is more likely to reverse.
4. **Seasonality screen** — rebalance **quarterly, before quarter-ending months** (end of Feb/May/Aug/Nov).
5. **Invest with conviction** — a concentrated, **equal-weighted ~50-stock** portfolio.

**Worked example:** universe 1,000 → top decile = 100 highest-momentum → rank those 100 on FIP, keep the smoothest 50 → equal-weight, rebalance end of Feb/May/Aug/Nov. Equal-weight is preferred live (diversification + small-cap tilt) even though the book's backtests are value-weighted for consistency. **Concentration and frequency both matter:** fewer stocks → higher return (a 50-stock universe beat a 300-stock one), and momentum premia decay fast so more frequent rebalancing raises gross return — quarterly is the friction compromise (ch.5).

**Performance (net of 1.80% fees, 1927-2014):** QM CAGR **15.80%** vs Generic Momentum 13.45% vs S&P 500 9.92%; Sharpe 0.60 vs 0.51 vs 0.41; worst drawdown −76.97%. QM is a higher-beta, higher-returning variant of generic momentum with a stronger value-diversification benefit.

**Combining with value — the "two-legged stool" (ch.4, ch.9):** value and momentum are **negatively correlated** (Asness-Moskowitz-Pedersen, "Value and Momentum Everywhere"), so combining them smooths multi-year relative-underperformance stretches. The authors' build: ~**40 momentum + 40 value** stocks (~80 total), equal-weighted, quarterly rebalanced, **50/50 allocation rebalanced annually**. Net-of-2% combination CAGR 18.10% vs 17.38% momentum / 16.98% value / 11.16% S&P, with lower vol and a smaller drawdown than momentum alone. **Three-legged stool — add trend (ch.9):** overlay a **12-month simple-moving-average** rule on the combo (if S&P 500 TR − 12-mo MA > 0 hold the combo, else hold T-bills); this cuts the worst drawdown from −60% to −26% (CAGR 16.57% vs 18.10%) — the concrete instance of Ilmanen's "trend is the long-vol diversifier of the short-vol cluster."

## 13. Portfolio-of-systems construction (Tomasini)

A complementary, systems-level view of the same construction problem (Tomasini, *Trading Systems*) — relevant when the "alphas" are whole trading subsystems rather than per-stock scores:
- **"The more the better":** even a mediocre system, applied across a basket of (uncorrelated) markets, produces a good portfolio equity line — always reason *under the portfolio constraint*, never judge a single equity line in isolation (p.4, 179, 181).
- **Correlate equity lines, not prices.** Weight markets by the correlation of their *daily/weekly equity changes*, not asset-price correlation (prefer shorter, weekly correlation to better minimize drawdown). In real futures portfolios even **−0.2** monthly correlation is valuable; truly negative correlations are rare (p.186-187).
- **Weighting:** give the **most uncorrelated** equity lines the **most contracts** to smooth the curve; or normalize by largest losing trade / losing streak / max drawdown (biggest-loss system gets fewest contracts — popular and easy, but a single largest-loss trade has low statistical significance) (p.188-192).
- **Dynamic activation:** trade a system only while its equity line is **above its own 15-30-period MA** (a risk switch that *also* re-activates dormant systems, unlike a plain drawdown stop); or the **WFA activator** — rank a "farm" of systems every 3 months by walk-forward efficiency and trade only those >50% (p.188-191).
- **Don't estimate returns/variance** for the portfolio — the future is unforecastable; control **risk** (entry-to-stop distance) and "if we limit risks, profits take care of themselves." Correlation is non-stationary, so any static correlation-based allocation is fragile (p.181-184). Diversification works best when systems have **similar average time-in-trade** so equity lines overlap and can offset (p.31-32). This is the trading-subsystem echo of Robbins/Carver/Ilmanen: *don't forecast returns, manage diversification and risk*.

## 14. Carver's factor combination & dynamic allocation (Advanced Futures)

Carver (*Advanced Futures Trading Strategies*) is the cross-asset (futures-centric) mirror of this file's equity machinery: the same "blend many weakly-correlated alphas, then size for limited capital" problem, solved with **forecast-weight handcrafting**, an explicit **diversification multiplier**, and a **greedy instrument-selection** algorithm. It is the bridge to T07 (sizing) and T11 (implementation/solvers).

**Cross-sectional factors as portfolio components.** Carver runs the same four style premia this file's equity zoo uses, but **cross-sectionally across instruments** rather than across stocks: **carry, momentum, skew, and value** (each a rank-and-go long-the-best / short-the-worst portfolio). He reports their *predictable* low cross-correlations as the reason to hold them together — **cross-sectional momentum ≈ 12% correlation, cross-sectional value ≈ 5%** to the rest of the book — and willingly *sacrifices backtest Sharpe for these predictable low correlations* (p.337-338). A **style taxonomy** organizes every rule: each is either **divergent** (profits as markets move *away* from equilibrium — trend, breakout, acceleration) or **convergent** (profits on *reversion* to equilibrium — carry, value, skew, mean-reversion) (p.221). This is the cross-sectional-mean-reversion / cross-sectional-momentum pairing of §2, generalized to all asset classes and tagged by convergent-vs-divergent payoff shape.

**Top-down forecast-weight combination (the method used everywhere; p.351-352, 362-363, 400-401).** A strict three-level hierarchy, equal-weighting at each level: (1) split rules by **style** and allocate **60% divergent / 40% convergent** by preference; (2) within a style, **equal-weight across distinct rules**; (3) within a rule, **equal-weight across the variations** cheap enough to trade. Then apply the **FDM (Forecast Diversification Multiplier** — a generic table that works for any well-diversified rule set, the forecast analogue of the IDM below) and **cap the combined forecast at ±20**. Why combine even weak, correlated rules: a rule with poor standalone Sharpe but *low correlation* lifts portfolio Sharpe. Forecasts also provide **implicit factor-timing**, so Carver (with Asness) discourages *explicit* trend-vs-carry timing — "stay diversified, don't time factors" (p.337-338, 375-376). The convergent/divergent diversification is the headline empirical result: median single-instrument Sharpe ~0.2-0.3 lifts ~**5×** to a "Jumbo" portfolio Sharpe ~1.1-1.3 for trend (acceleration up to 9×, skew only ~3×); equity trend is the persistent weak spot (≈0 Sharpe) — optionally cut its weight 60%→10% (p.338-341, 419-420). The richest daily blend (carry + three trend types + cross-sectional momentum + cross-sectional carry, ± skew) sits at **Sharpe ~1.3-1.4, ~26-29% return at 20% vol, skew ~0.6-0.7** (p.376, 383, 439).

**Instrument diversification & the IDM (p.123-124, 137, 203).** **Diversify across as many instruments as possible** — their weakly-correlated return streams lift portfolio Sharpe far above any single instrument's median (the "Jumbo portfolio" = 102 instruments, $50m notional). Because sub-strategy returns aren't perfectly correlated, a risk-parity portfolio realises *less* than target risk, so multiply **all** positions by an **Instrument Diversification Multiplier (IDM > 1)** to re-lever back to the risk target τ: rough `IDM = τ ÷ realised-aggregate-σ` (e.g. 20%/15.1% = 1.32). The **approximation table** (table 16, p.137; assuming a *diversified* set): 1 inst→1.00, 2→1.20, 3→1.48, 4→1.56, 5→1.70, 6→1.90, 7→2.10, 8-14→2.20, 15-24→2.30, 25-29→2.40, **30+→2.50** (invalid if all instruments are one asset class). This is the multi-instrument analogue of the equity IR·√breadth uplift (§6) — and the same caution applies: the multiplier only holds if the components really are diversified.

**Instrument weights via handcrafting (p.211-212).** Top-down **handcrafting**: group instruments by asset class, allocate equally within groups, sum to 1, none negative. Three drivers nudge the weights: **pre-cost Sharpe** (found *not* significantly different across instruments — so don't chase it), **cost** (cheaper = higher weight), and **correlation** (more diversifying = higher weight). This is the same Sharpe-skeptical, diversification-first handcrafting as his *Systematic Trading* method (§9) — adjust for Sharpe only when *cost* differences are known with certainty.

**Greedy instrument-selection for limited capital (the dynamic-optimization core; p.137-139).** With too little capital to trade the full Jumbo set, build it up greedily: **start from the cheapest affordable instrument; iteratively add whichever candidate maximises the trial-portfolio Sharpe** (recomputing weights, IDM, and the minimum-capital check at each step); **stop when the best new Sharpe falls 10% below the best seen.** This is an explicit, code-driven *dynamic optimization* for capital-constrained portfolios — Carver's answer to the small-account problem, and a direct cross-reference to T07 (sizing) and T11 (the solver/implementation). The combination logic throughout — allocate forecast weight across **styles → rules → variations** — mirrors this file's alpha→combo→variation hierarchy.
