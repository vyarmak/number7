**What this covers:** Classical (non-deep) machine learning applied to systematic trading — framing trading as a supervised/unsupervised learning problem (including **factor investing as supervised learning on a firm-characteristic panel**), the end-to-end ML4T workflow, the workhorse model families and their trading-relevant hyperparameters (penalized regressions, tree ensembles incl. XGBoost objectives, SVM/KNN, Bayesian/BART), the classical time-series/volatility/regime backbone (ARIMA/GARCH/VAR identification), unsupervised methods (PCA, clustering, covariance denoising), feature importance and interpretability (clustered/substitution-aware importance, variable importance, partial dependence, LIME/SHAP/breakdown), finance-specific and panel-aware validation, and the honest caveats that separate paper alpha from real P&L. Deep learning, sequence models, reinforcement-learning agents, and LLM agents live in **06**; this file references them but stays "classical."

**Why it matters:** ML is most defensible in trading not as a magic alpha box but as a disciplined, scientifically valid way to test many specification sets, weight/blend alpha factors, and optimize parameters without naïve overfitting (Narang, p.53, 61). The recurring lesson across every source is identical: financial series carry *very low* signal-to-noise, are non-stationary, and punish complexity — so the model choice matters far less than the validation discipline. López de Prado sharpens this into a thesis: most ML funds fail because they apply standard ML to finance *without economic theory* — ML is a tool for testing and refining theories, not a substitute for them (*ML for Asset Managers*).

**Primary sources:** Jansen, *ML for Algorithmic Trading* (workflow, metrics, CV); Chan, *Machine Trading* (model bake-off on SPY, aggregation/normalization) and *Algorithmic Trading: Winning Strategies and Their Rationale*; Coqueret & Guida, *Machine Learning for Factor Investing* (the dedicated ML-for-equity-factors reference — supervised learning on a firm-characteristic panel, the full model toolkit applied to factors, interpretability, and panel-aware validation); Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algorithmic Trading* (tree ensembles, SVM, Bayesian/MCMC, RF intraday strategy); Kaabar, *Deep Learning for Finance* (regressor family bake-off); Guo/Lai/Shek/Wong, *Chinese Quant* (high-dim regression, LOB ML); Tsay, *Analysis of Financial Time Series* (ARIMA/GARCH/VAR/cointegration backbone); López de Prado, *Machine Learning for Asset Managers* (covariance denoising, clustering, feature importance) and *Advances in Financial Machine Learning*; Jurczenko, *ML for Asset Management* (return forecasting, tactical allocation, sparse regressions); Tatsat/Puri/Lookabaugh, *ML & Data Science Blueprints for Finance* (applied case studies); Robbins, *Quantitative Asset Management* (method-to-task menu, causality); Kissell (black-box/HFT alpha models); Kaufman (GA/NN cautions); Hilpisch (scikit-learn spine); plus Narang, *Inside the Black Box*; Bernstein; and several strategy notes.

## 1. Framing trading as an ML problem

The canonical frame is **supervised statistical learning**: `Y = f(X) + ε`, estimate `f̂`, judge by a loss (squared or absolute) (Halls-Moore, p.272–278; *SuccessfulAlgo*, p.81–83). In trading you almost always want **prediction**, not **inference** — a forecast usable for positioning, not an explanation of which factor matters (Jansen, p.266–267; though inference matters when deciding *which* factors to keep).

**Problem types.** Combining alpha factors can be cast as (Jansen, p.266–267):
- **Regression** — predict the *return* magnitude.
- **Binary classification** — predict *direction* (+1/−1). Most sources prefer this: direction is what drives positioning, and it is an easier target than magnitude (Hilpisch, p.124, 134; *SuccessfulAlgo*, p.98). Robbins makes the strong version of the claim: reframing to classification (buy/hold/sell) beats regression on returns because it cuts the estimation burden and false-precision of a point forecast; the neutral "hold" class often *hurts*, so collapse to a binary loss-vs-profit target, and use multi-label only when you need to capture magnitude (*QuantAssetMgmt*, ch.10/17).
- **Multiclass / ranking** — predict return quintiles or rank names cross-sectionally (Jansen). Ranking is the natural frame for cross-sectional equity selection.
- **Cascades / two-stage.** A directional model feeds a sizing model: find *many* opportunities with a high-recall directional model, then magnify only the high-precision ones — this deliberately exploits the precision-vs-recall trade-off rather than fighting it (*QuantAssetMgmt*; echoes meta-labeling, see **07**).

**Predict the change, and bound the target.** Predicting *return* (the difference) rather than *price level* is mandatory: regressing on raw price just re-confirms the random walk (today ≈ best predictor of tomorrow, coefficient ≈ 1) and a non-stationary target produces a naïve forecast that merely lags the last value (Hilpisch, p.130–134; Kaabar, p.180, 193). Tsay supplies the econometric reason: a pure random walk `p_t=p_{t−1}+a_t` is unit-root nonstationary — the ℓ-step forecast is just `p_h` for *all* horizons, the error variance grows without bound (`ℓσ_a²→∞`), and shocks are permanent, so the level is structurally unpredictable and non-mean-reverting (Tsay, p.72–73). One agent (ATTac) trained on `closing − current` price, which guarantees the forecast never falls below the live quote and gives an easier learning target (*AutonomousBidding*, p.124–125).

**Factor investing *is* supervised learning on a firm-characteristic panel (Coqueret & Guida).** The cleanest equity-specific statement of the frame: the supervised baseline `Y = f(X) + ε` becomes `r_{t+1,n} = f(x_{t,n}) + ε_{t+1,n}` (eq.2.2), where `x_{t,n}` is the vector of **firm characteristics** (the factor exposures — value, momentum, size, quality, …) for stock `n` at time `t`, and `f(x_{t,n}) = E_t[r_{t+1,n}]` is the time-`t`-measurable **expected return** for `t+1`. The non-linear generalization (Gu, Kelly & Xiu 2020) is simply `r_{t+1,n} = g(x_{t,n}) + ε` (eq.3.7) — there is no reason beyond interpretability to stay linear (ch.3.5.2). Three structural points make this the canonical equity-ML setup:
> - **Common-`f` / pooled panel.** `f` is **not indexed by `n`**: one model is fit across *all* stocks simultaneously, learning a single mapping from characteristics → expected return rather than one regression per ticker (ch.2.2). This is exactly Chan's "aggregate one model over all SPX names, don't fit 500" recipe (§7) given its factor-investing justification.
> - **The time shift is the whole point.** Synchronous factor *explanation* (regressing realized return on *contemporaneous* factor realizations, eq.3.1) is useless for trading because the factor values aren't known in advance; there must be a gap between observing the state `x_{t,n}` and earning the return `r_{t+1,n}`. Once `ĝ` is fit, `g(x_{t,n})` is the predictive signal that feeds portfolio weights (ch.3.5.2) — the same "predict the change, never the synchronous level" discipline above, restated for cross-sectional characteristics.
> - **Labels need not be raw returns.** Risk-adjusted (Sharpe) returns, relative-to-benchmark returns, or **probability-of-outperformance** are all valid `y` (ch.3.5.2, 4.5) — and for classification the label is built **balanced**: `1` if the return exceeds the *cross-sectional median*, else `0`, *not* return>0 (which is unbalanced and time-dependent) (ch.1.2, 4.5.1; see §7).

Coqueret & Guida's honest caveats (ch.2.3) restate the whole file's thesis in factor terms: causality `X→y` is what you want but nearly impossible to find, so you settle for noisy *correlation*; financial data is extremely noisy and no-arbitrage erodes simple edges; **per the no-free-lunch theorem the analyst must impose economic views**; **inputs (the characteristics) matter more than the choice of model**; and *persistent* series ("what is not going to change?") are the ones most likely to hold out-of-sample. They deliberately use weak 1-month-return labels throughout the book so that baseline models give mediocre results — concrete proof that blindly feeding data does not work (this is the same low-SNR lesson Jansen and López de Prado land independently).

**The low signal-to-noise reality (read this twice).** Daily Information Coefficients are tiny — on the order of ~0.009 for a CNN technical model and 0.02–0.03 for a conditional autoencoder; best deep models early-stop after only ~4–9 epochs and "slight modifications yield significantly worse performance" (Jansen, p.830–832). Lagged price alone is weakly predictive; hit rates cluster at ~50–60% (*SuccessfulAlgo*, p.106). The goal is defined around **profitability**, not raw accuracy — a model where profitable trades outweigh unprofitable ones beats a higher-accuracy model that gets the big moves wrong (*ChineseQuant*, p.149). Kissell states the principle bluntly: financial gain depends more on *trading performance* than on *forecast accuracy*, so models (and traders) should be ranked by Sharpe/drawdown/gain-loss, not by error metrics alone (Kissell, ch.13). One naturally higher-SNR target worth predicting instead of returns is **beta**: it is far more learnable than returns, and better betas feed better cost-of-capital/CAPM inputs (Jurczenko, ch.7.2).

> **Critical:** Hit ratio ≠ profit. Above-50% accuracy routinely loses money (churns on costs, misses large moves); sub-50% can win if it nails the big moves. Always vectorized-backtest with an out-of-sample split and transaction costs — stellar in-sample results "routinely collapse" (Hilpisch, p.136, 149, 152–153).

**Why most ML funds fail (López de Prado's thesis).** Standard ML, applied off-the-shelf to finance, overfits because (1) the signal-to-noise is far lower than in the domains ML was built for, (2) labels overlap and observations are not IID, breaking the assumptions of cross-validation, and (3) practitioners search vast model spaces without an economic prior to anchor them. The remedy is **theory before algorithm**: use ML to *test and refine* economic hypotheses (which features should matter, what structure the covariance should have), not to data-mine alpha from a blank slate (*MLAssetManagers*, intro/throughout). Robbins independently lands the same warning: ML fits functions, not ground truth; causal/structural models (SCMs, DAGs, do-calculus, invariant causal prediction) are the robust complement, and high-bias models are preferred when you want a causal mechanism rather than a curve fit (*QuantAssetMgmt*, ch.10). Coqueret & Guida arrive at the identical conclusion from factor investing: by the **no-free-lunch theorem**, with only the training sample known no algorithm is superior on average, so the edge comes entirely from injecting the *correct prior structure* about the y–X relationship (wrong priors actively hurt) — **model choice matters less than the economic hypothesis behind the characteristics** (ch.12.5.2). They sharpen the non-stationarity point too: unlike vision/NLP where "a cat is always a cat," factor–return relationships are **not invariant** — they drift year to year, reverse, and blow out in downturns, there is no stable factor combination that predicts returns over decades, and even if one were found arbitrage would erode it; the only honest response is to embrace non-stationarity and retrain on rolling windows (ch.12.5.1, ch.14). Causal signals, in their framing, are precisely those *invariant across environments*; **Granger causality** (ch.14.1.1) is the simplest available check (e.g. confirming returns→sentiment rather than the reverse), the same correlation-vs-causation discipline Robbins urges.

## 2. The ML4T workflow

The reusable pipeline (Jansen, p.265–266; Kaabar Ch.7; MLDSBlueprints ch.5):

**Frame problem + pick target metric → source/clean/validate data → explore (EDA) + engineer features → build labels → select algorithm → train / validate / tune (GridSearch) → generate signal → backtest → finalize/deploy.** It is iterative, and the **No-Free-Lunch theorem** means you must tailor the hypothesis space to the task with domain priors — there is no universally best model (Jansen, p.265–266; Halls-Moore, p.272; *QuantAssetMgmt* and Coqueret & Guida ch.12.5.2 both make NFL explicit — for factors the corollary is that the economic hypothesis behind the characteristics outranks the algorithm). Tatsat et al. formalize this into a **standardized 7-step template** that every supervised case study clones from a shared notebook: define problem → load data/packages → EDA → data prep → evaluate models (train/test split + k-fold CV) → model tuning (GridSearch) → finalize (MLDSBlueprints, ch.5). Their recurring meta-lesson: **problem framing — designing the label to match the investment objective — is the highest-leverage step**, intuitive domain features beat raw price, and an out-of-time test set is mandatory.

**The black-box anatomy.** A stylized quant system is a chain of models, not one predictor: Data+Research → **Alpha model + Risk model + Transaction-cost model** → Portfolio-construction model → Execution model (Kissell, Fig.13.1). Quant trading is the scientific method applied to well-curated data, not magic; the alpha model is only one block, and a forecast is useless without the risk/cost/construction blocks around it.

**Features.** The default starting point everywhere is **lagged returns** of the same asset (autoregressive): build the matrix by shifting the return column, `data[f'lag_{k}'] = data['return'].shift(k)`; *N* lags = *N* features (Hilpisch, p.132, 146; Kaabar, p.181). This "time-delay" embedding is exactly how Tatsat et al. fold the time-series structure into a plain regression frame: to predict MSFT's weekly return they use lagged 5-day returns of correlated assets (GOOGL, IBM, USD/JPY, GBP/USD, S&P500, DJIA, VIX) plus lagged 5/15/30/60-day MSFT returns (MLDSBlueprints, ch.5 CS1). But every source stresses the bare-lag default is *arbitrary* — choose predictors by **economic intuition** (e.g., gold returns predicting S&P) or richer engineered features: lagged rolling volatility, momentum sign, SMA distance, rolling min/max (Hilpisch enriches to 6 features × 6 lags = 36 columns, p.279–280; Kaabar, p.192, 304). For a Bitcoin minute-bar classifier, Tatsat et al. engineer trend + momentum + mean-reversion indicators (SMA, EMA 10/30/200, RSI, ROC, MOM, stochastic %K/%D) and find RSI and 30-day MOM most predictive, materially lifting accuracy (MLDSBlueprints, ch.6 CS3). For limit-order-book ML, **feature engineering is *the* central problem** because the priors over which LOB features predict moves are unknown (*ChineseQuant*, p.149).

**High-frequency / tick-data caveat.** Raw ticks are *irregularly spaced*, but most ML and statistical tools assume a homogeneous (regularly spaced) series — so raw ticks need interpolation or time-operators before modeling (Kissell, ch.13). Useful HF features: price/return, realized vol, spread, tick frequency, realized skew, vol ratios across resolutions, direction-change indicators, and overlapping returns. Research proceeds as (1) discover stylized facts, (2) build an empirically grounded model, (3) test reproduction/forecast power.

**Labels.** Difference price into returns for a stationary target (Kaabar, p.180, 193). For event/direction labels, the "up-down factor" scheme labels a move only if it rises ≥ `up_down_factor × percent_factor` and does *not* fall past `percent_factor` within a look-forward window (Halls-Moore, p.413). Jurczenko's probability-scoring approach attaches **horizon-specific labels** to each trade idea and trains a supervised model to output P(success) per idea (Jurczenko, ch.5). For triple-barrier and meta-labeling, see **07**.

**Scaling.** Required for distance- and margin-based models (SVM/SVR, KNN, SGD): use `StandardScaler` inside a `make_pipeline` (Kaabar, p.195). **Fit the scaler on the training set only and apply the *same* μ/σ to test/live data — never refit the scaler** (Hilpisch, p.282). Tree-based models do not need scaling; SVM benefits from features in [−1, 1] (Halls-Moore, p.251).

**The scikit-learn spine.** The reusable API is (1) instantiate, (2) `.fit(X, y)`, (3) `.predict(X)` — identical across `LinearRegression`, `LogisticRegression`, `RandomForestClassifier`, `AdaBoostClassifier`, etc., so models are swappable with near-zero code change (Hilpisch, p.139–140, 145).

**Persistence.** Serialize the trained model with `joblib` ("model persistence") and deserialize inside the live strategy (Halls-Moore, p.412).

**Organizational hygiene.** Robbins recommends **Chinese walls between data research and strategy research** — keep overfitting-controlled feature work separate from the p-hacking-prone backtest, so the people hunting features are not the people fitting the P&L curve (*QuantAssetMgmt*).

## 3. Supervised models with trading use

Defaults/results below are from the cited bake-offs; treat reported accuracies as *illustrative of fragility*, not targets. As a reliability prior, Robbins flags **SVM/trees/KNN as more reliable out-of-sample**, with **regressions/boosting/ANN/LDA more overfit-prone** (*QuantAssetMgmt*, ch.10/17) — useful when deciding what to escalate to.

### Linear / logistic + regularization
- **OLS linear regression** — fast, low memory, interpretable; good *only* if the dependence is linear; sensitive to outliers; assumes independence/stationarity. On daily EURUSD with 500 AR lags: train acc 58.5% / test 49.5%, correlation collapsing 0.37→0.01 (textbook overfit) (Kaabar, p.190–193). Baseline via `np.linalg.lstsq`; regress on returns (or on `np.sign(return)`, which lifts hit ratio ~50%→~52%) (Hilpisch, p.130–134). In the MLDSBlueprints stock-prediction case, simple **linear/regularized models plus ARIMA were the most promising** and best resisted over/underfitting versus the tree and neural zoo (MLDSBlueprints, ch.5 CS1).
- **Logistic regression** — direction classifier; weaken regularization with large `C` (e.g. 1e6–1e7) when you want a near-unregularized fit (Hilpisch, p.147).
- **Ridge (L2)**: `β̂ = (XᵀX + λI)⁻¹XᵀY`, shrinks coefficients. **Lasso (L1)**: simultaneous selection + shrinkage. **Elastic Net**: L1+L2 blend. Essential when **p ≫ n**, using sparsity to make the problem tractable; tune λ by k-fold CV (R: `glmnet`) (*ChineseQuant*, p.51–53; Kaabar, p.191). For the L0 (exact-sparsity) problem, OGA+HDIC+Trim gives a fast forward-selection approximation (*ChineseQuant*, p.51–53).
- **Penalized regressions as factor selection (Coqueret & Guida, ch.5).** The factor-investing motivation for the same three estimators: unconstrained OLS `β* = (X'X)⁻¹X'y` (eq.5.1) needs `I > K` and yields noisy coefficients, so a structuring constraint trades a little bias to remove noise (Tibshirani 1996) — and crucially it still works when the **characteristics outnumber the observations** (`K > I`). **LASSO (L1)** `min_β Σ(y_i − Σβ_j x_{i,j})² + λΣ|β_j|` (eq.5.3): as `λ↑`, coefficients hit *exactly zero* fast, giving automatic **sparse factor selection** (for very large `λ` only one characteristic survives). **Ridge (L2)** `min_β Σ(...)² + λΣβ_j²` (eq.5.4) with closed form `β̂ = (X'X + λI)⁻¹X'Y`: the `λI` term guarantees invertibility and shrinks every coefficient smoothly toward — but never onto — zero (pure shrinkage, no selection). **Elastic-net** is the convex mix `αΣ|β_j| + (1−α)Σβ_j² < δ` (Zou & Hastie 2005), buying LASSO's sparsity *and* ridge's stability. Applied to a factor model this becomes the **penalized predictive regression** `r_{t+1,n} = α_n + Σ_k β_n^k f_{t,n}^k + ε` subject to the elastic-net constraint (ch.5.4), which gives more robust return forecasts when *combining many single-characteristic signals* — consistent with the empirical finding that only ~20–30 of 94 characteristics are useful (Han et al. 2019; Rapach & Zhou 2019). This is the factor-selection analogue of Jurczenko's elastic-net return-forecasting result below (both conclude elastic-net is the workhorse).
- **Regularized predictive regressions for return forecasting (Jurczenko).** A dedicated treatment of sparse regressions for one-month-ahead return prediction across equity value/momentum, FX, and bonds using 120 FRED-MD macro predictors (ch.3.4). Comparing ridge, lasso, **elastic net**, group-lasso, and a Bayesian **horseshoe** prior, the finding is that **elastic net is the only method with consistently positive out-of-sample R²** (~1%/yr utility at γ=5), dominating both pure-sparse (lasso) and pure-dense (ridge). Key insight: **dynamic sparsity** — the right amount of shrinkage varies over time and asset class; the horseshoe estimates global+local penalties jointly and so avoids the over-shrinkage of sequential CV.
- **Combination Elastic Net (C-ENet) (Jurczenko, ch.1.2).** A two-stage forecast combiner: first average univariate predictive-regression forecasts (strong Rapach-2010-style shrinkage), then run elastic net on the *panel of individual forecasts* to select and re-weight the relevant ones. Reported as "one of the best US market-excess-return forecasts to date," beating OLS, elastic-net-on-raw-predictors, and the prevailing-mean benchmark. Picks λ via corrected AIC (sidesteps arbitrary K-fold), and consistently retains dividend-price, volatility, T-bond yield, term spread, and a technical signal — keeping interpretability. Directly portable to ETF market-timing.
- **Stepwise regression** (forward/backward by SSE/AIC/BIC) is the recommended *starting* model: on SPY, plain multiple regression gave OOS CAGR 0.4%, but stepwise picked just `ret2` (negative coef = mean reversion) → OOS CAGR 10.6%, Sharpe 0.7. It is also the best ML use for **stock selection**: over 27 size-independent fundamentals it picked 7 (CurrentRatio, TBVPS, EBITDA margin, gross margin, NCFO growth, P/S, ROA) → OOS CAGR 4%, Sharpe 1.1 (Chan, p.87–89, 111–114).

### Tree ensembles
- **Single decision/regression tree** — handles nonlinearity and missing data but **overfits hard** (Kaabar: train 100% / test 47.4%). Mitigations: a hierarchical split minimizing child-node variance (regression) or Gini (classification), `MinLeafSize ≈ 100`, and using *only the two extreme leaves* as ready-made rules (e.g. "ret2<1.53% & ret1<−1.39% → buy" gave CAGR 3.9%; using *all* leaves overfit to in-sample 73% / OOS −7.2%) (Chan, p.89–92; Kaabar, p.203–204). Coqueret & Guida give the CART mechanics for the factor case (ch.6.1): a regression tree splits on one characteristic at a time to minimize **total within-cluster dispersion** `V = Σ_{x<c}(y_i − m_−)² + Σ_{x>c}(y_i − m_+)²` (eq.6.1, variance × cluster size summed over the two children), predicting the leaf-mean label; classification splits use **Gini** `1 − Σp_j²` (= 1 − Herfindahl, the "least-diversified" partition), **entropy** `−Σ p_j log p_j`, or misclassification `1 − max_j p_j` (avoided — not differentiable) (ch.6.1.2). The single-tree regularizers are the levers to know: `minbucket` (min instances per leaf), `minsplit` (min size to attempt a split), `cp` (split only if post-split loss `< (1−ε)×` pre-split loss), and `max_depth`; complexity falls with minbucket/minsplit/cp and rises with max_depth. Their key intuition: **early (near-root) splits are the trustworthy general patterns; deep splits fit idiosyncrasies and don't generalize** — and for a fixed-stock-count portfolio a single shallow tree is structurally inadequate, since depth 3–6 ⇒ only 8–64 leaves, so 20–30% of stocks share one identical signal and cannot be ranked (ch.6.5).
- **Random Forest** (bagging + per-split predictor subset, ~1/3 of predictors) — robust to noise, less overfit than a single tree, gives **feature-importance scores** (valuable when p is large), but slower and opaque. `max_depth` controls overfit. The headline intraday strategy: `RandomForestClassifier(n_estimators=400, max_depth=10)` predicting next-bar return sign from 5 lagged minutely returns; depth=10 is **critical** — default depth produced a 4.1 GB pickle that swapped to disk (~60 bars/s). Result: Sharpe 3.02, CAGR 10.63%, max DD ~1.7% (vs an LDA variant Sharpe 2.02); higher frequency → higher Sharpe than daily (Halls-Moore, p.412–433; Chan, p.92–98; Kaabar, p.205–206). Coqueret & Guida supply the factor-investing theory (Breiman 2001): an RF combines many trees each grown on (i) a **bootstrap resample** of instances and (ii) a **random feature subset at every split**, aggregating by equal-weighted average (regression) or majority **vote** (classification) — i.e. *bagging*. Its generalization error is bounded by `ρ̄(1 − s²)/s²` where `s` is individual-learner strength and `ρ̄` the average correlation between learners, so the goal is **decorrelated yet accurate trees**; because that bound converges to a finite limit as #trees→∞, **adding trees never overfits** (only depth/leaf-size do). For factors they recommend RF (or boosted trees) precisely because, unlike a single tree, an aggregate model emits a **unique per-asset signal** that *can* be ranked to a target stock count — and ≥5 learners already suffices (ch.6.2, 6.5).
- **Boosting.** AdaBoost reweights hard instances sequentially — accurate but **overfits beyond ~100 estimators** and is outlier/noise-sensitive and *not* parallelizable (Halls-Moore, p.251–259; Kaabar, p.207). Coqueret & Guida frame AdaBoost as sequentially reweighting instances to focus on the largest *current* errors, then weighting each learner by its quality (ch.6.3). LSBoost fits residuals iteratively but did *not* reduce overfit on SPY (Chan, p.92–98). **XGBoost** (regularized gradient boosting) is generally the best of the tree family — handles missing values, parallel, feature importance; tune `n_estimators`, `max_depth`. On EURUSD it was the best classical model (test acc 53.0%) but with a large directional bias (6.8) — watch for skew (Kaabar, p.209–211). LightGBM/CatBoost are the same gradient-boosting family with faster training / categorical handling (see Kaabar selection rule, p.190).
- **XGBoost for factors — objective, penalty & hyperparameters (Coqueret & Guida, ch.6.4).** The regularized boosting objective is `O = Σ_i loss(y_i, ŷ_i) + Σ_j Ω(T_j)`: each new tree `T_J` is fit to reduce the residual loss of the model so far `M_{J−1}` (expand directly for quadratic loss, Taylor-approximate otherwise). The **complexity penalty** `Ω(T) = γL + (λ/2)Σ_l w_l²` has two distinct knobs — `γ` penalizes the **number of leaves** `L` (effectively controlling depth) and `λ` shrinks the **leaf-output magnitudes** `w_l` (controlling variance). A candidate split is kept only if `Gain = ½(Gain_L + Gain_R − Gain_O) − γ > 0`, with `Gain_X = (Σ_{i∈X}(y_i − m_{J−1}))² / ((1 + λ/2)·#{i∈X})`, where the `−γ` is the explicit per-new-leaf cost. **Key hyperparameters:** the **learning rate** `η ∈ (0,1]` scales every new tree and is essential (aggregating ~100 un-shrunk optimized trees overfits); `subsample` (row sampling) and `colsample_bytree` (column sampling) mirror RF; **DART** randomly drops trees (Srivastava 2014; Rashmi 2015); **monotonicity constraints** let you bake in economic priors directly (e.g. force the momentum effect non-negative, a stale-value effect non-positive); typical `max_depth` is just 3–6. XGBoost also supports **instance weighting** — `O = Σ W_i·loss(...) + Σ Ω(T_j)` — where weights can depend on features, e.g. `W_i ∝ |x_i^k − 0.5|` to emphasize the *extreme* deep-value / deep-growth firms that carry the cross-sectional signal (ch.6.4.7). This is the equity-factor-specific complement to Kaabar's empirical XGBoost bake-off above.
- **Tree methods for higher-SNR targets (Jurczenko).** Predicting **beta** (not returns), LASSO/RF/XGBoost beat OLS statistically; news *volume* (RavenPack NLP) adds signal while macro-interaction terms do not, and SHAP reveals genuinely nonlinear effects (both high *and* low momentum raise beta; debt/assets flips sign post-2009) (ch.7.2). For **probability-scoring trade ideas**, RF + gradient-boosting trees output P(success) per idea, improving long ideas +1.06–1.85% and shorts +1.57–2.78%, with momentum the most informative feature, then liquidity and volatility (ch.5.3–5.5).

> **Class imbalance is the core quant-ML pitfall.** Event labels are rare, so models lazily predict the majority class — a 58% hit-rate can be a pure imbalance artifact. **Always inspect the confusion matrix, not just the hit-rate.** Serial correlation forbids naïve random down-sampling (Halls-Moore, p.413–414).

### SVM / KNN
- **SVM/SVR** — maximal-margin → soft-margin (budget `C`, slack) → kernel trick. **`C` is the bias-variance knob** (small C = low bias/high variance), chosen by CV. Kernels: linear, polynomial (degree d), **RBF** `exp(−γΣ(x−x′)²)` (localized). Strengths: high-dimensional (≥10⁶ features — ideal for text/sentiment), memory-efficient (stores only support vectors). Weaknesses: weak when p ≫ n, non-probabilistic, tuning-sensitive, compute-heavy. On SPY the **linear** kernel was the best single method (OOS CAGR 13.3%, Sharpe 0.8) precisely because the relationship was genuinely linear — nonlinear kernels merely add overfitting room (Halls-Moore, p.259–273; Chan, p.99–101; Kaabar, p.194–196).
- **KNN** — nonparametric, instance-based (no retrain), captures nonlinearity, intuitive; degrades in high dimensions ("curse of dimensionality"), slow on big data, noise-sensitive. `k` trades bias/variance; rule of thumb `k ≈ √(sample size)`, weight neighbors by 1/distance. A single-stock KNN predicts T-day forward return from normalized MA-of-price/volume predictors, averaging neighbors' realized returns; fitted weights are "often unstable" → mandatory OOS backtest (Kaabar, p.200–202; *151Strategies*, p.53; Kaufman, p.1788–93).

### Bayesian methods
Full **Bayesian inference** yields a *distribution* (with credible intervals), not a point estimate — directly usable to quantify uncertainty in a hedge ratio or volatility (Halls-Moore, p.49). Recipe: prior `P(θ)` × likelihood `P(D|θ)` → posterior `P(θ|D)` via Bayes' rule; the posterior becomes the next prior as data arrives (continual updating) (p.17–18). **Conjugate priors** give closed forms (Beta prior + Bernoulli → Beta posterior: observe z heads in N trials → `beta(z+α, N−z+β)`, p.31). When there is no conjugacy or many parameters, the evidence integral is intractable ("curse of dimensionality") → **MCMC**: the Metropolis algorithm proposes a jump (proposal width = key tuning param) and accepts with prob `P(θ_new)/P(θ_current)` — the evidence cancels in the ratio. Advanced samplers: Metropolis-Hastings, Gibbs, Hamiltonian MC, **NUTS** (default in PyMC3). PyMC3 mechanics: `with pm.Model():`, declare priors as distribution objects and the likelihood with `observed=`, sample, then discard a **burn-in** period (Halls-Moore, p.31–52). **Naïve Bayes** (Bernoulli) is a strong small-data / text baseline (high bias) — used to classify crypto direction from tweet word-presence features after stemming (*SuccessfulAlgo*, p.99–102; *151Strategies*, p.120–121). The Bayesian **horseshoe** prior (above, Jurczenko ch.3.4) is the modern shrinkage incarnation. A standard validation trick throughout: **simulate data with known parameters, then fit to recover them** (Halls-Moore, p.49).

**BART for factors (Coqueret & Guida, ch.9).** Bayesian Additive Regression Trees sum many *weak* trees with Gaussian terminal-node weights; "training" is **drawing posterior samples** via Metropolis–Hastings/Gibbs — each tree proposal is accepted or rejected by its likelihood improvement and the noise variance `σ²` is resampled on the full residual each loop, so the output is a posterior *distribution* over predictions (not a point forecast), like the Bayesian methods above. On return prediction the book reports BART as "reasonable, not impressive" — consistent with its recurring message that ML return forecasts are intrinsically weak (ch.9.5). It also surveys the remaining toolkit applied to factors — **neural networks** (the non-linear `g`, treated more deeply in **06**), **SVM**, and **ensemble / stacking** that blends the base models above — but for equity factors converges on **aggregate tree models (RF, boosted trees) as the practical default** because they emit rankable per-asset signals (ch.6.5).

## 4. Classical time-series, volatility & regime models (the econometric backbone)

Detail and cointegration math also live in **02** (statistics) and **08** (mean-reversion/pairs). Tsay supplies the rigorous identification machinery that ML methods extend; the workflow is **stationarity check → ACF/PACF/EACF identification → fit → diagnostic → forecast**.

### Stationarity & the ACF/PACF diagnostic (Tsay)
A series is **weakly stationary** if its mean and autocovariances `γ_ℓ = Cov(r_t, r_{t−ℓ})` are time-invariant; finance routinely assumes return series are weakly stationary (Tsay, p.30). The **autocorrelation** `ρ_ℓ = γ_ℓ/γ_0` is the core diagnostic (p.31). Test a single lag with the t-ratio `√T·ρ̂_ℓ` (≈ N(0,1) under iid); test jointly with the **Ljung–Box** statistic `Q(m) = T(T+2)Σ_{ℓ=1}^m ρ̂_ℓ²/(T−ℓ)`, which is χ²_m under H₀ of no autocorrelation, with rule-of-thumb `m ≈ ln(T)` (p.32). Empirically, monthly individual-stock returns (e.g. IBM) show essentially no serial correlation — consistent with weak-form efficiency — while index returns show small but significant correlation (p.33–34). This is the formal version of the recurring "barely beats random" finding.

### AR / MA / ARMA identification (Tsay)
- **AR(p):** `r_t = φ₀ + φ₁r_{t−1} + … + φ_p r_{t−p} + a_t`, white-noise `a_t` (p.37). AR(1) is weakly stationary iff `|φ₁|<1`, with mean `µ = φ₀/(1−φ₁)` (p.38). **Order identification, two routes:** (1) the **PACF cuts off at lag p** — fit nested regressions and read `φ̂_{ℓ,ℓ}`; for an AR(p) it is ≈0 for ℓ>p, asymptotic variance `1/T` (p.46–47); (2) minimize an **information criterion** — `AIC(ℓ) = ln(σ̃²_ℓ) + 2ℓ/T` or `BIC(ℓ) = ln(σ̃²_ℓ) + ℓ·ln(T)/T`; BIC's heavier penalty selects more parsimonious models (p.48–49).
- **AR forecasting & mean reversion.** The minimum-MSE ℓ-step forecast `r̂_h(ℓ) = φ₀ + Σφ_i r̂_h(ℓ−i)` is computed recursively (p.55–56); forecast-error variance grows with horizon. For a stationary AR(p), `r̂_h(ℓ) → E(r_t)` as `ℓ→∞` — **mean reversion** — and for AR(1) the **half-life** of a shock is `ℓ = ln(0.5)/ln(|φ₁|)` (p.56). This formalizes exactly how fast a mean-reverting signal decays (cross-ref **08** for the pairs application).
- **MA(q):** `r_t = c₀ + a_t − θ₁a_{t−1} − … − θ_q a_{t−q}` (p.58). Always stationary; mean = c₀; **the ACF cuts off at lag q** — the mirror image of AR/PACF, and the identification rule (p.59–60). MA(1) needs `|θ₁|<1` to be *invertible* (expressible as a convergent infinite AR). **Bid–ask bounce induces an MA(1)** structure in observed returns (p.57) — a microstructure artifact to recognize, not signal.
- **ARMA(p,q):** e.g. `r_t − φ₁r_{t−1} = φ₀ + a_t − θ₁a_{t−1}` for (1,1); combines AR+MA for parsimony (p.64). ACF and PACF *both fail* to identify the order — use the **EACF** (extended ACF): the two-way table shows a triangle of "O" whose upper-left vertex marks the true (p,q) (p.66–67). Forecasts behave like AR forecasts after the first q steps.

### Unit roots, ARIMA & the GARCH link (Tsay)
- **Random walk** `p_t = p_{t−1} + a_t` is unit-root nonstationary: forecast `r̂_h(ℓ) = p_h` for all ℓ (not predictable, not mean-reverting), error variance `ℓσ_a² → ∞`, shocks permanent (p.72–73). **Random walk with drift** `p_t = µ + p_{t−1} + a_t` adds a deterministic time-trend slope µ to log price (p.73). Beware: the constant term means different things by model — series mean (MA), the `µ(1−Σφ_i)` relation (AR), or trend slope (random walk) (p.74). **Difference to stationarity first** before ARIMA/SARIMAX modeling (Kaabar, p.180).
- **The single most useful structural insight:** a **GARCH model is an ARMA model for the squared-shock series `a_t²`** (Tsay, p.64). This unifies §4's volatility modeling with AR/MA identification — you identify GARCH orders with the same ACF/PACF logic applied to squared residuals.
- **GARCH-family volatility.** Conditional-variance forecasting that captures volatility clustering (the workhorse for the risk/vol block; Robbins notes GARCH+ML hybrids as the practical combination, *QuantAssetMgmt*). See **02** and **10** for the GARCH/EGARCH/GJR detail and the trading application.

### Multivariate dynamics, VAR & cointegration
- **VAR(p)** (Tsay) — the multivariate generalization for jointly modeling several return series: `r_t = φ₀ + Φ₁r_{t−1} + … + Φ_p r_{t−p} + a_t` (p.399). Off-diagonal Φ-elements capture **lead-lag** dependence: `Φ₁₂≠0, Φ₂₁=0 ⇒ series 1 leads series 2`; both nonzero ⇒ feedback (p.399). The *reduced form* hides concurrent dependence (inside Σ); a Cholesky factorization `Σ = LGL′` recovers the *structural form* with uncorrelated innovations (p.399–400). VAR is the engine for cross-asset/cross-sector dynamics and for cointegration testing.
- **Cointegration / stat-arb pairs (Kissell, ch.13).** The trade condition is `|payoff(St − R(St))| > TransactionCost` (eq.13.1). Process: ADF unit-root test (eq.13.6) → Engle–Granger OLS for the cointegrating vector `W = S1 − β̂2·S2 − …` (eq.13.7) → long/short the vector → **ECM/VECM** captures the speed of mean-reversion `ΔSt = a + Σβi ΔSt−i + ΣΓ Wt−j + εt` (eq.13.10), where the γ coefficients are the adjustment speed (e.g. γ = −0.027 ⇒ a 2.7% correction per 5 min). The sign/significance of the W-coefficient tells you *which leg leads*. (Full cointegration treatment in **08**.)
- **ARIMA / SARIMAX** — autoregressive integrated moving-average for stationary-differenced return series; trend-period scanning is its informal cousin (Kaufman, p.1788–93). Identify orders via the Tsay ACF/PACF/EACF machinery above; difference to stationarity first (Kaabar, p.180).
- **Kalman filter** — dynamic (time-varying) hedge-ratio / state estimation; Bayesian output gives a posterior on the hedge ratio (Halls-Moore, p.49; see **08**).
- **Hidden Markov Model (regimes)** — *unsupervised*: hidden bull/bear states with transition matrix T and emission matrix E, trained via EM (multi-restart), decoded via Viterbi for regime naming. On SPY it was modest (in-sample 8.7%, OOS only 1%) — most useful for *labeling* the regime rather than direct prediction (Chan, p.101–105).

## 5. Unsupervised learning, covariance denoising & clustering

- **PCA / eigen-decomposition** — `np.linalg.eig` on `np.cov` extracts the covariance structure underpinning factor models and eigenportfolios; the entry point to covariance-based ML features and risk decomposition (*PythonQuants*, p.179–181; see **04**). PCA is also used for dimensionality reduction *before* classification (*SuccessfulAlgo*, p.99–102). Tatsat et al. apply this directly to build **eigen-portfolios** from the principal components of the return covariance (MLDSBlueprints).

### Covariance denoising & detoning (López de Prado, *ML for Asset Managers*)
Empirical correlation matrices estimated from `T` observations on `N` assets are dominated by noise when `q = T/N` is not large — and that noise wrecks downstream mean-variance optimization. The fix has three steps:
- **Denoising via Random Matrix Theory (Marčenko–Pastur).** Under pure noise, the eigenvalues of the correlation matrix follow the **Marčenko–Pastur** distribution on `[λ₋, λ₊]` with `λ± = (1 ± √(1/q))²` (variance σ²=1 for a correlation matrix). Eigenvalues *below* `λ₊` are statistically indistinguishable from noise; the procedure **fits the M-P density to the empirical eigenvalue spectrum, finds the implied cutoff `λ₊`, and replaces all sub-cutoff eigenvalues with their average** (constant-residual-eigenvalue method), preserving the trace. The reconstructed matrix retains only the eigenvectors that carry signal. This sharply reduces the condition number and stabilizes the inverse used in portfolio weights (*MLAssetManagers*, ch.2).
- **Detoning.** Optionally remove the dominant **market (first) eigenvector** as well, so the residual correlation structure reflects sector/style relationships rather than the all-pervasive market mode — useful before clustering, because the market component otherwise swamps the cluster signal (*MLAssetManagers*, ch.2).

### Correlation → distance metrics & clustering (López de Prado)
- **A correlation is not a metric**, but `d_{i,j} = √(½(1 − ρ_{i,j}))` is a proper **distance metric** on `[0,1]` derived from correlation (so it satisfies the triangle inequality and can drive clustering). A further "distance of distances" can capture higher-order relationships (*MLAssetManagers*, ch.4).
- **Optimal Number of Clusters (ONC).** A clustering algorithm that uses **k-means on the distance matrix and selects the cluster count that maximizes the silhouette-score quality** (the t-statistic of silhouette scores), re-clustering the poorly-clustered items recursively. ONC finds the natural grouping of assets *without* a pre-specified k — useful for discovering pairs/baskets and for the clustered feature-importance and HRP machinery below (*MLAssetManagers*, ch.4). Robbins independently lists clustering for grouping assets/regimes; Tatsat et al. cluster assets to find **pairs candidates** (MLDSBlueprints).
- **k-Means (general)** iteratively reassigns points to the mean of their nearest cluster until stable; used to group assets for pairs candidates or to discover regimes (Kaufman, p.1788–93).
- **Hierarchical Risk Parity (HRP)** — clustering-based portfolio construction that sidesteps mean-variance instability by allocating along the dendrogram rather than inverting an ill-conditioned covariance (see **04** for the full treatment; flagged here as the unsupervised-learning → portfolio-construction bridge, and a natural consumer of the denoised covariance above).

## 6. Feature importance, interpretability & substitution effects

- **Mutual information** (`sklearn.feature_selection.mutual_info_regression`) extends correlation to *nonlinear* dependence and selects features most likely to carry predictive signal (Jansen, p.272–273). López de Prado uses the related **variation of information** as an information-theoretic distance between features for clustering (see §5).
- **Tree-based importances** — RF/boosting/AdaBoost emit feature-importance scores, especially valuable when p is large (*ChineseQuant*, p.149–151). But they are *in-sample* and inflate the apparent importance of substitutable features.

### Mean-Decrease-Accuracy & the substitution problem (López de Prado)
- **MDA (Mean Decrease Accuracy)** is the out-of-sample, model-agnostic importance: shuffle one feature's values across the validation set and measure the *drop in performance*. Unlike in-sample MDI (mean decrease impurity), MDA reflects genuine predictive contribution. But MDA has a fatal flaw with correlated features: **substitution effects.** When two features are nearly interchangeable, permuting *one* barely hurts accuracy (the model leans on its substitute), so both look unimportant — importance is diluted across the cluster and a genuinely informative group can be missed (*MLAssetManagers*, ch.6; *AFML*).
- **Clustered Feature Importance.** The remedy: first **cluster features** (via the correlation-distance + ONC machinery of §5), then compute importance **on whole clusters at once** — permute every feature in a cluster together (clustered MDA) or aggregate impurity reduction per cluster (clustered MDI). Because substitutes live in the *same* cluster, the substitution effect is absorbed *within* the cluster and the between-cluster importances become trustworthy. This tells you which *themes* (not which individual, redundant features) drive the prediction — exactly the robustness an economic-theory-first workflow needs (*MLAssetManagers*, ch.6).
- **SHAP** — the standard model-agnostic attribution for explaining individual predictions and global feature impact in tree ensembles; pairs with the importance scores above to sanity-check that the model keys off economically sensible drivers (Jansen; used by Jurczenko on beta forecasting, ch.7.2; see **07** for using it to reject leakage-driven "features"). Robbins rounds out the interpretability toolkit with **LIME, partial-dependence, and ICE** plots alongside SHAP (*QuantAssetMgmt*).

### Interpretability for factor models (Coqueret & Guida)
Because a factor model must be *defensible* — you have to be able to say *which characteristic* drove an allocation — Coqueret & Guida treat interpretability as a first-class topic and assemble the standard toolkit specifically for the firm-characteristic panel: **global variable importance** (which characteristics matter most across the whole model), **partial-dependence plots** (the marginal shape of expected return vs. one characteristic, e.g. confirming a value or momentum effect is monotone and the right sign), and the local methods **LIME**, **SHAP**, and **breakdown** (decomposing a *single* stock's predicted return into per-characteristic contributions). The recurring use is the same robustness check the rest of this file demands: verify the model keys off economically sensible, *persistent* drivers rather than noise — and the monotonicity constraints of XGBoost (§3) are the lever to *enforce* what these plots merely *diagnose*. This is the factor-investing analogue of the Robbins/Jansen interpretability stack above; cite them together.

## 7. Validation specific to finance

This section matters more than model choice. Full protocol in **07**.

- **Bias-variance trade-off.** Generalization error = irreducible noise + bias² + variance. Diagnose with **learning curves** (train vs. test error vs. sample size): high-bias → both errors high and converged (add capacity/features); high-variance → low train / high test (add data or regularization) (Jansen, p.273–276).
- **Three-way split.** Use cross-validation for tuning **plus a one-time hold-out test set** — because tuning over many CV iterations introduces multiple-testing bias on the validation score (Jansen, p.276–283).
- **Chronological, never shuffled.** Always split by time (80/20 or 70/30); train = in-sample, test = out-of-sample; **never shuffle across the time dimension** for sequence data (Kaabar, p.177–178; Jansen, p.829). Tatsat et al. use an **80/20 *ordered* (non-random) split** plus 10-fold CV for exactly this reason, and observe predictive accuracy *decaying ~6–7 months* into the test set — concrete evidence of non-stationarity (MLDSBlueprints, ch.5).
- **Walk-forward / rolling CV.** Use `MultipleTimeSeriesCV`: train on a multi-year window, predict the next short block, roll forward (e.g. 36 folds of 5-yr-train / 1-mo-test) (Jansen, p.821, 829). For small financial datasets, K=5 beats K=10, and picking the best fold is common (Chan, p.92–98). Jurczenko's **out-of-time validation (OTV)** is the disciplined version: horizon-specific labels with a 5–6-yr trailing training window, always using the model whose training ends just *before* each rebalance — structurally preventing look-ahead (ch.5).
- **CV's IID assumption is broken by serial correlation.** Robbins states the failure mode directly: standard CV assumes IID, which serial correlation violates, so naïve CV scores are optimistic (*QuantAssetMgmt*, ch.10). This is the motivation for **purged & embargoed CV** — remove training samples whose label windows overlap the test set, and embargo a buffer after it, to kill leakage from overlapping labels (López de Prado, *AFML*; see **07**).
- **Panel-aware validation & tuning (Coqueret & Guida, ch.10–12).** For the firm-characteristic panel the book devotes a dedicated validation/tuning treatment with two distinctively factor-flavoured points. (1) **Match the persistence of features and labels** — a slowly-moving characteristic paired with a fast (1-month) label, or vice versa, mis-specifies the panel and corrupts any CV estimate; persistence-matching is the panel analogue of purged CV. (2) **Bayesian hyperparameter optimization** is recommended over brute-force grid search for the large XGBoost/RF/NN hyperparameter spaces: build a cheap surrogate (probabilistic) model of validation-score-vs-hyperparameters and let it propose the next, most-informative configuration to try, converging in far fewer expensive fits than `GridSearchCV`. They also caution explicitly on the **ML-backtest perils** — overfitting from fully-grown trees and un-regularized boosting, the multiple-testing inflation of searching many specifications, and the non-stationarity that makes a single train/test split optimistic — the same warnings formalized in **07** (purged/embargoed CV, the deflated Sharpe ratio, backtest overfitting).
- **Aggregate and normalize across the cross-section.** Train **one** model on all SPX names aggregated (reshape T×S → vector) rather than 500 separate models, and **normalize predictors and response by past-return volatility** before aggregating (else WMT and WTW are incomparable) — this flipped a reg-tree from OOS CAGR −0.7% to +2.3%, Sharpe 0.9 (Chan, p.109–111).
- **Error metrics.** Regression → RMSE (default), RMSLE (exp-growth targets), MAE/MedAE (robust), R². Classification → confusion matrix → accuracy/precision/recall/F1, plus **ROC-AUC** = P(rank random positive > random negative), which is insensitive to class imbalance; use the precision-recall curve when one class matters (Jansen, p.268–271). Hit Rate = `(1/n)·Σ I(yⱼ=ŷⱼ)` (*SuccessfulAlgo*, p.97–98). Coqueret & Guida add the factor-classification specifics: prefer **hit ratio / accuracy** over MSE for classification labels (MSE is hard to map to P&L), but build the categorical target **balanced** — `1` if a stock's return beats the *cross-sectional median*, else `0`, never return>0 (which is both unbalanced and time-dependent) — and recognize that while ~0.546 accuracy is "satisfactory," **transaction costs push the useful threshold to ≥52%** (ch.1.2, 4.5.1, 6.1.4), the same hit-ratio-≠-profit point made throughout §1. Kissell adds a forecast-vs-trading split: for **forecast accuracy** use MAE, MAPE, RMSE, **Theil-U** (∈[0,1], 0 = perfect), and **Correct Directional Change** (eq.13.32–13.36); for **trading** use annualized return/vol, **Sharpe = R_A/σ_A**, max daily P/L, **max drawdown** `MD = min(RC_t − max_{i≤t} RC_i)`, % winning trades, avg gain/loss ratio, profit t-stat `= √N·R_A/σ_A`, and probability-of-10%-loss (eq.13.37–13.62). When P/L-testing, **strip non-risk P/L** (fees, dividends) so the alpha is measured cleanly (Kissell, ch.13).

**Key trading-evaluation formulas.** The **Information Coefficient (IC)** is the rank correlation between forecast and realized return (the Alphalens-style signal quality metric; daily ICs of 0.01–0.03 are *normal*, Jansen p.832). The fundamental law of active management links it to performance: **IR ≈ IC · √breadth**, where breadth is the number of independent bets per period — i.e. a tiny edge is only useful if you can place it many independent times. Signal-evaluation pipeline: predictions → Alphalens quintile/decile spread → long-short top/bottom names → report Sharpe *before* costs (Jansen, p.831).

## 8. Honest caveats

- **Overfitting is the default outcome.** ML methods are powerful *search* procedures that hit an optimum fast and will fit noise; "machine learning is not magic" and "the best predictor is often the simplest" (Kaufman, p.1731). GAs and NNs find a *local*, not global, peak — rerun ~10× from different seeds to confirm (Kaufman, p.1767). "In trading, complexity doesn't pay" — start with stepwise regression and only escalate (Chan, p.115). Bernstein: keep it simple, simple methods outperform complex ones (p.182, 185). MLDSBlueprints' own bake-offs land here too: across stock-return prediction the simple **linear/regularized + ARIMA** models resisted over/underfitting best (ch.5). Coqueret & Guida make the point structurally: fully-grown trees and un-regularized boosting are the standing overfit threat, controlled only by pruning (`minbucket`/`minsplit`/`cp`/`max_depth`), shrinkage (`η`), and dropout (DART) — and they *deliberately* use weak 1-month-return labels throughout so that even well-tuned models give mediocre results, demonstrating that the variance–bias tradeoff (Geman et al. 1992) and the no-free-lunch theorem, not model cleverness, govern factor-ML outcomes (ch.4.7.3, 5, 6, 12.5.2).
- **Why most ML funds fail — need for theory.** López de Prado's central warning: off-the-shelf ML on finance overfits because of low SNR, non-IID overlapping labels, and theory-free model search; ML should *test and refine economic hypotheses*, not data-mine alpha from scratch (*MLAssetManagers*). Robbins concurs: ML fits functions, not ground truth — pair it with causal/structural models (SCMs, DAGs, do-calculus, invariant causal prediction, self-healing fault detection), and prefer high-bias models when a causal mechanism is the goal (*QuantAssetMgmt*, ch.10).
- **Substitution effects hide importance.** With correlated features, single-feature MDA/MDI dilute importance across substitutes and can declare a truly informative group unimportant — use **clustered feature importance** (§6) before trusting any ranking (*MLAssetManagers*).
- **Noisy covariance ruins optimization.** An un-denoised correlation matrix is dominated by Marčenko–Pastur noise; inverting it for mean-variance weights amplifies that noise. **Denoise (and optionally detone) the covariance** before any optimization (§5; *MLAssetManagers*).
- **Regime shift / non-stationarity.** Markets are a "moving target"; historical data can mislead as participants adapt — an agent that retrained on fresh data sometimes broke (bug + regime shift). Best methods combine model-based *and* data-based reasoning, re-fit on short lookbacks (*AutonomousBidding*, p.79–80; Leshik, p.125–126). The measured ~6–7-month accuracy decay (MLDSBlueprints) and Tsay's permanent-shock random-walk result (p.72–73) are the same phenomenon from two angles. Coqueret & Guida give the factor-specific version: factor–return relationships are **not invariant** the way image/NLP labels are — they drift, reverse, and blow out in downturns, there is *no* stable factor combination that predicts returns over decades, and arbitrage would erode one even if found; the only honest response is rolling-window retraining and a preference for *persistent* characteristics (ch.12.5.1, ch.14).
- **"Barely beats random."** Lagged price alone is weakly predictive; hit rates ~50–60% and daily ICs near 0.01 (and Tsay: monthly single-stock returns show essentially *no* serial correlation, p.33–34). Treat any single model as fragile and **ensemble several best epochs/folds to cut forecast variance** (Jansen, p.830–832; *SuccessfulAlgo*, p.106).
- **Richer forecasts don't auto-improve P&L.** Learning a full price *distribution* was no better than using its mean unless the downstream bidding/sizing heuristic actually exploited the distribution; prediction accuracy correlated strongly with score (R ≈ −0.9) yet explained only ~12% of P&L variance in the wild (*AutonomousBidding*, p.64–65, 74–79). Kissell's version: financial gain depends more on trading performance than forecast accuracy — rank by Sharpe/drawdown, not error metrics (ch.13).
- **Capacity & costs.** Above-50% accuracy can lose money to costs and missed large moves; higher frequency can lift Sharpe but compounds cost/capacity limits (Hilpisch, p.136; Halls-Moore, p.433).
- **Microstructure artifacts ≠ signal.** Bid–ask bounce manufactures an MA(1) autocorrelation in observed returns (Tsay, p.57) and irregular tick spacing breaks the homogeneity most tools assume (Kissell) — both can masquerade as predictability.
- **Where ML genuinely wins.** Parameter optimization and alpha-factor weighting/blending, not raw alpha generation; supervised learning / GAs for portfolio construction are argued to be more rigorous than plain mean-variance optimization (itself a form of data mining) (Narang, p.53, 61, 111). Higher-SNR targets (beta, volatility) are more learnable than returns (Jurczenko; Robbins on GARCH+ML). For equity factors the defensible win is the **pooled supervised mapping characteristics → expected return** combined with regularization/aggregation to keep it robust (Coqueret & Guida) — not a guarantee of alpha, which the book's deliberately-weak baselines underline.
- **Reinforcement learning for allocation (cross-ref 06).** Coqueret & Guida close with a chapter casting **portfolio allocation as a reinforcement-learning problem** (state = market/portfolio configuration, action = weights/rebalancing, reward = risk-adjusted return), but flag it as data-hungry and fragile under non-stationarity — the same caution applied to every method here. The RL detail (Q-learning, deep RL agents, execution) lives in **06**; this file only notes the framing so the factor-allocation chain (characteristics → forecast → weights) connects to it.
- **Synthetic data is unreliable** — a computer exploits the construction rules, so synthetic-series results don't prove a strategy (Kaufman, p.1812).

## To validate / watch-outs
- **Confusion matrix before hit-rate, every time** — rare event labels make accuracy a lie; verify class balance and never down-sample serially correlated data naïvely (Halls-Moore, p.413–414).
- **Scaler hygiene** — fit on train only, persist μ/σ, apply unchanged to test/live; a refit scaler silently leaks (Hilpisch, p.282).
- **Walk-forward + purge/embargo are non-negotiable** — any result from shuffled or non-purged CV on overlapping labels should be treated as invalid; CV's IID assumption is broken by serial correlation (Jansen, p.829; *QuantAssetMgmt*; see 07).
- **Denoise the covariance before optimizing** — fit Marčenko–Pastur, cut sub-`λ₊` eigenvalues, optionally detone the market mode; never invert a raw empirical correlation matrix for weights (*MLAssetManagers*, ch.2).
- **Cluster features before trusting importance** — single-feature MDA/MDI are diluted by substitution effects; use clustered importance (correlation-distance + ONC) to identify the driving *themes* (*MLAssetManagers*, ch.6).
- **Identify time-series orders properly** — read PACF (AR), ACF (MA), EACF (ARMA); confirm stationarity/unit-root (ADF) and recognize bid–ask-bounce MA(1) as an artifact, not alpha (Tsay).
- **Re-fit cadence vs. regime shift** — decide and document a retraining schedule on short lookbacks; monitor for the "agent that broke after retraining" and the ~6–7-month accuracy-decay failure modes (*AutonomousBidding*, p.79–80; MLDSBlueprints).
- **Sanity-check directional bias** — high-accuracy boosters (e.g. XGBoost on EURUSD) can carry large skew; inspect predicted-class distribution and report Sharpe net of costs, not just accuracy (Kaabar, p.209–211).
- **Theory before algorithm** — anchor feature/label choices in an economic hypothesis; prefer causal/structural complements and higher-bias models when a mechanism (not a curve fit) is wanted; by no-free-lunch the economic prior on the characteristics outranks the algorithm, and *inputs matter more than model choice* (*MLAssetManagers*; *QuantAssetMgmt*; Coqueret & Guida ch.2.3, 12.5.2).
- **Build balanced labels & match persistence** — for factor classification use return > *cross-sectional median* (balanced), never return>0; match the persistence of slow characteristics to the label horizon; require accuracy ≥52% (post-cost), not just >50% (Coqueret & Guida ch.1.2, 4.5.1, 6.1.4, 10–12).
- **Use aggregate trees, not a single tree, for ranking** — a depth-3–6 single tree gives only 8–64 leaves, so 20–30% of stocks share one signal and can't be ranked to a fixed count; RF/boosted trees emit unique per-asset signals (≥5 learners suffices) and don't overfit by adding trees (Coqueret & Guida ch.6.5).
- **Tune with Bayesian optimization, regularize boosting explicitly** — prefer Bayesian hyperparameter search over brute-force grids for XGBoost/RF/NN; always set `η`, leaf/depth penalties (`γ`, `λ`, `max_depth` 3–6), and consider monotonicity constraints to bake in economic priors (Coqueret & Guida ch.6.4, 10–12).
- **Confirm the edge is real and repeatable** — require IC > 0 out-of-sample across folds and enough breadth for IR ≈ IC·√breadth to clear costs before allocating capital; rank by Sharpe/drawdown, not forecast accuracy (Jansen; Kissell).
