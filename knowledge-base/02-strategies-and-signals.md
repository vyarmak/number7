# 02 — Strategies & Signals
*Trading System Knowledge Base · theme T02 · equities/ETF-weighted*
*Updated: includes Carver, Clenow, Gray & Vogel, Tortoriello, Ilmanen, Aldridge.*
*Updated (pass 3): added Chan (rationale), Clenow Stocks on the Move, Quantitative Value, Carver Advanced Futures.*

**What this covers:** A catalog of trading strategy families and the concrete entry/exit signals that drive them — trend/momentum, mean-reversion (single-name, pairs/cointegration, cross-sectional), breakout/channel, discretionary-to-rules pullback setups, statistical arbitrage and factor signals, event/seasonality, intraday/opening-range, and liquidity-provision logic — plus how to combine signals. Each entry states the idea, the signal/entry rule (with formulas and numbers where available), exit/stop, typical parameters, instrument/timeframe, and a caveat.

**Why it matters:** Signal generation is the alpha-bearing core of a trading system; everything else (execution, sizing, risk) services it. Knowing the *small* number of distinct edges — and how each decays, where it lives, and what regime it needs — is what separates a robust system from an over-fit one.

**Primary sources:** Kaufman, *Trading Systems & Methods* (dominant); Chan, *Machine Trading* & *Quantitative Trading*; Narang, *Inside the Black Box*; Halls-Moore, *Advanced/Successful Algorithmic Trading*; Kakushadze & Serur, *151 Trading Strategies*; Harris, *Trading and Exchanges*; Bellafiore, *The PlayBook*; Bernstein; Grimes; Hilpisch; Jansen; Guo et al., *Quantitative Trading*; Aziz/Davey; Leshik & Cralle. **New sources merged:** Carver, *Systematic Trading*; Clenow, *Following the Trend*; Gray & Vogel, *Quantitative Momentum*; Tortoriello, *Quantitative Strategies for Achieving Alpha*; Ilmanen, *Expected Returns*; Aldridge, *High-Frequency Trading*; Bouchaud et al., *Trades, Quotes & Prices*; Hasbrouck, *Empirical Market Microstructure*; Sinclair; Bennett, *Trading Volatility*; Tatsat et al., *Machine Learning & Data Science Blueprints*; Tomasini & Jaekle; Davey, *Building Winning Algorithmic Trading Systems*; Aronson, *Evidence-Based Technical Analysis*; Tsay; Qian/Hua/Sorensen (QEPM); *Scalping is Fun!*/*Trading With Technical Trend Indicators*. **New sources merged (pass 3):** Chan, *Algorithmic Trading: Winning Strategies and Their Rationale* (mean-reversion/momentum with rationale); Clenow, *Stocks on the Move* (equity-momentum system); Gray & Carlisle, *Quantitative Value* (deep-value + quality stock selection); Carver, *Advanced Futures Trading Strategies* (convergent strategies transferable to equities); Natenberg, *Option Volatility and Pricing* (hedging/vol structures).

## 1. Signal taxonomy — how to classify any edge

Only a handful of distinct alpha ideas exist; they "explode into diversity via implementation choices" (Narang, *Inside the Black Box*, p.24). Useful orthogonal axes:

- **Directional vs relative-value.** Directional bets on the level/return of one instrument (trend, momentum, reversal). Relative-value bets on the *difference* between related instruments (pairs, index-arb, spreads) — typically market/factor-neutral (151Strategies; Harris, p.349).
- **Time-series vs cross-sectional.** Time-series = a signal from one asset's own history (absolute momentum, AR mean-reversion). Cross-sectional = rank a *pool* and go long top / short bottom (relative momentum, value, short-term reversal) (Hilpisch, p.98; Guo et al., p.42).
- **Theory-driven vs data-driven.** Theory-driven rests on an economic rationale; data-driven (data-mining) searches history for analogues and trades conditional probabilities — rarer, harder, dominant in HFT where no theory exists at sub-minute scales, but high overfitting risk and generally fails at longer horizons (Narang, p.42–45). Carver frames the same dichotomy as **ideas-first vs data-first** and favors ideas-first (hypothesis → rule → test) because it yields simple, explainable rules with a "story," less prone to over-fitting; data-mining ("the 50-model kid") is dangerous — you want *both* a good story and a rigorous back-test (Carver, ch.2). Aldridge's "Spaghetti Principle": stat-arb must rest on economic theory, not data mining (HFTAldridge, Ch.8).
- **Narang's six theory-driven phenomena** (the master list of *what* to bet on): price-based → **Trend**, **Mean-Reversion**, **Technical Sentiment**; fundamental → **Value/Yield**, **Growth/Sentiment**, **Quality** (p.26). Quants and discretionary traders use the same six; they differ only in *how*.
- **Carver's "why rules make money" (sources of return)** — a complementary master list to Narang's: persistent risk premia (equity, term, size/liquidity), time-varying premia (mean-reversion of cheap/expensive insurance), **skew premium**, leverage-constraint premium, liquidity provision, forced sellers (e.g. FX carry vs central-bank pegs), behavioural biases, self-fulfilling technicals, and rare pure alpha/skill (Carver, *Systematic Trading*, ch.2). Ilmanen's *style premia* (value, momentum/trend, carry, defensive/low-vol) are the same idea cast as harvestable, persistent factor returns (Ilmanen, throughout) — see §6.
- **Convergent vs divergent (Carver, *Advanced Futures Trading Strategies*).** The structural axis that organizes Carver's *Advanced Futures* strategy library: **divergent** strategies (trend/EWMAC, breakout, acceleration) bet on continuation, have positive skew, and *make* their own forecast; **convergent** strategies (carry, value, skew, fast mean-reversion, relative-value) bet on reversion/equilibrium, have negative skew, and harvest a premium. He runs a **60% divergent / 40% convergent** forecast-weight split. Most convergent strategies (carry, value, skew, cross-sectional momentum/carry, relative-value spreads) transfer cleanly to a liquid equity/ETF universe — see §2.7 and §6. **Chan's organizing principle (*Algorithmic Trading: Winning Strategies and Their Rationale*):** every strategy is mean-reverting *or* momentum — but the discipline is to **first identify the specific market inefficiency** (regression to a mean, persistence of the sign of roll returns, forced fund rebalancing, slow news diffusion) and only *then* build a **simple, linear** model to exploit it; identifying the *cause* first is the antidote to data-snooping (Chan, *AlgoTrading*, Preface/Conclusion).

**Rule vs signal (formal).** A **rule** is a function transforming an input market series into an output series of recommended positions, typically signed (+1 long, −1 short); a **signal** is a *change* in that output (e.g. +1 → −1 closes the long and opens a short). Outputs can scale (+10 → +5 = reduce). The simplest, most testable form is the **binary reversal rule** {+1, −1} — always long or short (Aronson, p.16). Carver generalizes the binary rule to a **continuous scaled forecast** (see §2.5). A forecast/signal is only meaningful if it has a defined **endpoint** — a time or event that triggers evaluation; vague calls ("bullish intermediate term") can't be scored (Aronson, p.65).

**Thresholds turn information into signals.** Signals fire when a series crosses a critical level (inequalities). **Fixed** thresholds suit stationary "oscillator" series; **variable** thresholds (moving average, zigzag/Alexander filter) suit trending series; multiple-threshold rules use **flip-flop** logic (moving-average band / Bollinger band rules) (Aronson, p.17–20). **Test traditional *and* inverse rules:** because the conventional TA interpretation may be wrong, test a rule and its exact inverse (output negated) — a pattern assumed bullish may predict declines or have no edge; essential when the correct sign of a novel input (e.g. a yield differential) is not obvious (Aronson, p.21–22).

**Two preconditions.** Trading is viable only if prices are **mean-reverting or trending**; otherwise it is a random walk and futile (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.116). Picking which regime applies for your horizon (and the reference price) *is* the strategy. Trend and reversion coexist because they operate on **different time horizons** (Narang, p.32). Trend-following only profits *when markets trend* — no technique profits in sideways markets (Kaufman, p.587).

**Trade-profile fingerprints** (use to vet before backtesting):

| Family | Win rate | Trade shape | Failure mode |
|---|---|---|---|
| Trend / momentum | often <35–42% | many small losers, few big winners | long drawdowns; chops in ranges |
| Mean-reversion / pairs | high (many small wins) | occasional severe loser can wipe gains | a leg that never reverts / liquidity crisis |
| Breakout | 50–70% | range-sized risk (HH−LL) | most breakouts *fail*; slippage in the break zone |
| Carry / short-vol | very high | tiny frequent gains, rare violent loss | flight-to-quality crash; losses cluster Oct '87/'98/'08 |

(Kaufman, p.633–655; Halls-Moore, *Successful Algo*, p.35,110; Grimes, p.83; Clenow, *Following the Trend*, ch.5; Ilmanen, Ch.13.4.) Carver's *skew* lens maps onto this: trend-followers have **positive skew** (the fat right tail), contrarian/mean-reversion/carry styles have **negative skew**; he deliberately runs a balanced mix (~⅓ of his own system is negative-skew) (Carver, *Systematic Trading*, ch.2). **Chan's mean-reversion-vs-momentum trade-offs (*AlgoTrading*, ch.2 vs ch.6) sharpen the same fingerprints:** *mean-reversion* is easy to manufacture (cointegration), usually has a fundamental story, spans seconds→years and at short scale gives many trades → high Sharpe — **but** high consistency breeds over-leverage and rare catastrophic losses, and stop-losses are logically inconsistent with it (a wider deviation is a *better* entry, not a reason to exit); *momentum* is harder, has lower Sharpe (few independent signals), suffers post-crisis "momentum crashes," and decays as crowds learn — **but** has limited downside (time/stop exits), unlimited upside, **thrives on black swans / high kurtosis**, and diversifies across asset classes.

## 2. Trend & momentum

**Idea.** Bet an established move persists; rationale = slow consensus-building / underreaction to news, money flow, herding (Narang, p.27; Guo et al., p.42). **Chan's four causes of momentum (*AlgoTrading*, ch.6 — the book's signature framing):** (1) for futures, **persistence of the sign of roll returns** (the only cause absent intraday); (2) **slow diffusion, analysis and acceptance of news**; (3) **forced asset sales/purchases by funds** (e.g. leveraged-ETF rebalancing, index reconstitution); (4) **HFT manipulation/order-flow effects**. Intraday adds a fifth: (5) **triggering of clustered stops** (breakouts). Naming the cause tells you *where* and *at what horizon* the edge lives and how it will decay. Returns are **streaky and fat-tailed** — the fat tail (more long runs than a random series) is *essential* to profitability; cutting it with tight stops/profit-taking lowers expectancy ("conservation of capital": let profits run, cut losses) (Kaufman, p.587, 601). Lower-frequency data and longer calc periods track fundamentals better and are more stable (weekly > daily > hourly) (Kaufman, p.594). Behavioral micro-foundation (Ilmanen, Ch.14.4): **underreaction** to news (conservatism, overconfidence, disposition effect) drives continuation; **overreaction/extrapolation + herding** drives the later reversal — legs flip too fast for a rational time-varying risk premium to justify the sign changes, and trend/CTAs are *great crisis diversifiers* (long-vol payoff), deepening the "why does it pay?" puzzle.

### 2.1 Moving-average and crossover systems
- **SMA crossover (benchmark).** Long when fast SMA > slow SMA, else short/flat. One-liner: `np.where(SMA1 > SMA2, 1, -1)`; common pairs 42/252 or 60/200 (Hilpisch, p.90; Narang, p.27). **Golden/Death Cross** = 50-day crossing 200-day; long-only and long-short both work on SPY, combination best (much earned in the 2000 collapse) (Kaufman, p.664).
- **Trendline-direction vs price-cross.** Buy when the *trendline itself* turns up (vs price crossing it). Direction signals give 26–37% fewer trades and usually better performance for **longer-term** trading; price-cross better for **short-term** (less lag burden) (Kaufman, p.599).
- **Two-trend (slow=direction, fast=timing).** Rule sets: (1) fast-vs-slow MA cross (always in market); (2) price crosses both (neutral zone between); (3) enter only when both agree, exit on conflict. A 100×30 crossover beat a single 120-day MA on the euro (Kaufman, p.655). **Three-trend**: add a fast (~3-day) trend purely as entry-timing confirmation — *do not enter unless the fast MA moves in the intended direction* (Kaufman, p.668). Classic 4-9-18 crossover (Kaufman, p.668).
- **Exponential / TRIX / TSI.** EXP smoothing is consistently the **worst** trend method in head-to-head tests (Kaufman, p.633). TRIX = triple-EMA of ln(price); buy when it rises 2 consecutive days (~6-day smoothing); signal line = 3-day MA of TRIX (p.631). **True Strength Index (TSI, Blau)** = doubleEMA(1-day momentum)/doubleEMA(|1-day momentum|), e.g. 20/20 — very smooth, little lag, "momentum as a proxy for price" (p.761).
- **Ichimoku Cloud.** Tenkan (9), Kijun (26), Senkou A=(T1+T2)/2 +26, Senkou B (52) +26, Chikou (close −26). Cloud = long-term filter; trade only in cloud direction; T1>T2 cross = momentum entry (Kaufman, p.673).
- **Hybrids.** Directional Parabolic (Wilder DMI/ADX gates direction + Parabolic SAR trailing stop, AF 0.02→0.20); MACD (12/26, signal EMA-9, histogram) for trend, timing, and divergence (Kaufman, p.781, 710).

**Comparison verdict (6 systems, Kaufman p.633):** all basic trend methods profitable *if* the market trends; **linear-regression slope generally best overall**; momentum ≈ MA (MA slightly better); EXP worst; breakout high-risk but 50–70% wins. **Picking trending markets matters more than picking the "best" system.** Short sales in equities are generally unprofitable. Favor longer calc periods (macrotrend ≥ 40–60 days). Clenow's two "basic" precursor systems (MA-crossover and breakout) are ~0.9 correlated to each other — independent proof that the *exact entry rule barely matters* once the trend filter and exit are in place (Clenow, ch.4); Tomasini reaches the same conclusion ("the simplest things work best" — a length-1 MA, i.e. the close itself, beat a triangular MA) (Tomasini, p.38, p.53).

### 2.2 Pure momentum / ROC
- **Time-series (absolute) momentum.** Position = sign of mean of last *N* returns: `np.sign(returns.rolling(N).mean())` (Hilpisch, p.99). Highly sensitive to window length (last-2 vs last-3 flips the sign); more pronounced **intraday** than interday. Simple N-day momentum: buy if N-day change > 0 (≈ MA for large N) (Kaufman, p.626). Moskowitz (2012): absolute past performance **outperforms cross-sectional**, best in market extremes (Guo et al., p.42).
- **Cross-sectional (relative) momentum.** Rank a pool, long winners / short losers. **Carhart MOM** = 12-month winners − losers, zero-cost long-short (151Strategies, p.40). Jegadeesh-Titman: 3–12-month winners persist (Guo et al., p.42). "Value and momentum everywhere" (Asness 2013) — works across asset classes. Gray & Vogel formalize this into a full equity recipe — see §2.6.
- **ROC method (Woodshedder).** Buy when 5-day ROC < 252-day ROC for 2 consecutive days; exit when 5-day > 252-day for 2 days; earn ½ T-bill when flat; 53% win on S&P futures (Kaufman, p.665).
- **High-momentum trading.** Trade *with* a fast move only when momentum exceeds a high threshold; scan many stocks for new highs after a quiet period; exit when volume drops (Kaufman, p.710).
- **Chan's concrete momentum recipes (*AlgoTrading*, ch.6–7).**
  - **Time-series (futures), cleaner via roll return:** buy if 12-month return > 0, hold 1 month (Moskowitz-Yao-Pedersen), rebalance daily putting 1/25 of capital on each day's cohort → TU (2-yr Treasury) Sharpe ~1; **replacing the price-return signal with the lagged *roll* return** (whose sign persists) lifts TU to **Sharpe ~2.1** — the roll-return version is the structurally cleaner futures-momentum signal. Alternative entries that all work: N-day high, MA/EMA cross, upper Bollinger, Alexander filter.
  - **Cross-sectional momentum (stocks):** buy the top decile / short the bottom decile of 12-month return, hold ~1 month (Daniel-Moskowitz). Strong pre-2008 (Sharpe 4.1 in 2007) but **APR −30% in 2008–09 — the "momentum crash"** when beaten-down losers violently rebounded; this is the canonical short-side risk of cross-sectional stock momentum.
  - **Leveraged-ETF rebalance momentum (equities/ETF):** a 3× ETF must buy/sell its underlying *near the close* in the direction of the day's move → predictable end-of-day momentum. Rule: trade DRN (3× real-estate) if its prev-close→(close − 15 min) return > ±2%, exit at the close → **APR 15%, Sharpe 1.8** (an instance of Chan's "forced fund rebalancing" cause).
- **Commodity time-series trend (Ilmanen Ch.14).** Go long/short each commodity on the sign of price vs. its **12-month moving average**; equal- or vol-weight a basket. Long-run ~11% excess return, 14% vol, SR ~0.8 pre-cost (best in energy and wheat; only silver lost money). Directional trend-timing composite SR ~0.8 (rides synchronous 1998/2003/2007/2008 moves) > cross-sectional long-short SR ~0.5. **Signal menu, none dominates:** MA rules, MA crossovers, EWMAs, breakout/acceleration/filter rules, consistency/relative-strength; add no-trade bands and stop-loss/take-profit exits. Diversify across assets *and* across window lengths/frequencies; 6–12-month windows have a mild edge and lower turnover. **Vol-weighting** signals and positions adds SR ~0.1–0.3 and stops high-vol names (nat gas) dominating (Ilmanen, Ch.14.2). Commodity-specific rational hook: theory of storage — low inventories → backwardation → positive roll; momentum, roll, and inventory profits are correlated proxies (Gorton–Hayashi–Rouwenhorst: momentum 13.4% / roll 10.2% / inventories 8.1% excess) (Ch.14.4).

### 2.3 Swing / breakout-style trend systems
- **Swing trading.** Define a **swing filter** (best as % of price, recomputed only at each new swing high/low); a new upswing starts when price reverses from the low by ≥ filter. Conservative entry: buy when the current upswing high exceeds the previous one; aggressive: buy as soon as a new upswing is recognized. No action in sideways markets, no lag (Kaufman, p.399–404).
- **Livermore.** Trade only with the major trend (higher highs + higher lows); add on each pivot penetration; stop at penetration beyond the prior pivot by a **penetration filter ≈ ½ the swing filter**; needs a *second* penetration to confirm a new trend (Kaufman, p.405).
- **Wilder Swing Index → ASI.** Scale 5 uptrend factors to [−1,+1] via true range; sum to Accumulated Swing Index; trade ASI High/Low Swing Points with SAR stops (Kaufman, p.410).

### 2.4 Forecast-model trend signals
- **ARIMA+GARCH index forecast.** Rolling window k=500 days of differenced S&P log-returns; each day grid-fit best ARIMA (24 (p,q) combos by AIC) + GARCH(1,1); **long if predicted next-day return > 0, short if < 0**. Beat buy-hold over 65y but most gains were 1970–80 (strong 2008–09 crash); decays in stochastic-trend regimes; watch lookahead (Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algo*, p.367).
- **Anticipating signals.** For an N-day MA the trendline rises if today's price > the price dropped off N days ago — so you can pre-compute the close that triggers a signal and place a stop order *before* the close ("buy at 30.26 stop"). One order/day (liquidate OR enter). Delaying to next open improved price ~75% of the time but **lowered total profit** (missed breakouts); noisy markets (S&P) favor delay, trendy markets favor immediate (Kaufman, p.600–607).
- **ML-classified MA regime.** Frame the signal as binary classification: label = 1 (buy) when the 10-day SMA > 60-day SMA, else 0 — i.e. *learn* a moving-average-crossover regime from engineered features and trade the predicted class. Demonstrated on Bitcoin but the blueprint generalizes to any buy/sell/hold equity strategy (MLDSBlueprints, ch.6).

### 2.5 Carver's continuous scaled-forecast framework (rule-combination)
Carver replaces the binary {+1, −1} rule with a **continuous, volatility-standardised forecast** that scales position size with conviction, then combines several such rules into one number. This is the structural backbone of *Systematic Trading* and a clean way to blend signals (cross-ref §10).

- **Forecast construction.** Each rule outputs a raw value, divided by the instrument's **price-volatility (in price points)** so all instruments and rules are on one risk-adjusted scale, then multiplied by a **forecast scalar** so the *average absolute forecast ≈ 10*. Forecasts are capped (e.g. ±20) to limit any single rule's bet (Carver, ch.7, App. B).
- **EWMAC (trend rule, positive skew).** `forecast = forecast_scalar × [(fast EWMA − slow EWMA) ÷ price-vol-in-price-points]`. EWMA decay `A = 2/(L+1)`. Look-back pairs run at fast:slow = **1:4** — **2:8, 4:16, 8:32, 16:64, 32:128, 64:256** — combined across speeds. Forecast scalars (Carver Table 49): 2,8 → **10.6**; 4,16 → **7.5**; 8,32 → **5.3**; 16,64 → **3.75**; 32,128 → **2.65**; 64,256 → **1.87**.
- **Carry rule (negative skew).** The annualised, vol-standardised expected return *if prices don't move* — e.g. equity **dividend yield − funding cost**, then ÷ price vol (analogous to FX interest-rate differential or futures roll yield). Diversifies EWMAC because its skew is opposite.
- **Combination & coverage.** Multiple forecasts (across rules and speeds) are weighted and summed into one combined forecast that drives the position. **These two rules alone (EWMAC + carry) reproduce ≈ 85% of his full system's back-tested performance** — i.e. most of the edge is a diversified trend rule plus carry (Carver, ch.7).
- **Three design pitfalls Carver engineers against:** **over-fitting** (curve-fitting to noise → ch.3 Fitting), **overtrading** (cost + gambling addiction → ch.12 Speed/Size), **over-betting** (excess leverage; 10× leverage on a 5%-daily-loss system ⇒ ~10% chance of losing half capital in a day → ch.9 vol targeting). The system itself is a **commitment mechanism** against discretionary "meddling" (prospect theory: humans cut winners early, run losers — "get-evenitis"/disposition effect) (Carver, ch.1).

### 2.6 Clenow's full trend-following system ("the champion")
Clenow's *Following the Trend* gives a complete, reproducible diversified-futures trend system; transfers directly to a liquid equity/ETF universe. **Core idea: buy high / sell higher** (short low / cover lower) and capture the *middle* of medium-term trends. Average holding ~6½ weeks (winners ~10wk, losers ~2wk); **win rate only ~42%** (loses on ~58% of trades; some good TF systems lose 70%) — *profits come from a few outsized winners*, the canonical fat-tailed profile (Clenow, ch.5).

**THE CORE STRATEGY — exact rules (Clenow, ch.4):**
1. **Trend filter (regime gate):** long entries allowed only when **50-day SMA > 100-day SMA**; short entries only when **50-day SMA < 100-day SMA**.
2. **Entry (breakout):** buy if today's **close = highest close of the past 50 days**; sell-short if today's close = lowest close of the past 50 days — a 50-day breakout, gated by the filter.
3. **Exit (stop):** **3×ATR trailing stop** — close a long when price falls 3 ATR units below the highest close since entry; close a short when price rises 3 ATR units above the lowest close since entry. (This trailing stop *replaces* the original 25-day-breakout exit of the "basic" precursor.)
4. **Sizing:** ATR volatility-parity, risk factor 20 bp per position (see T08).
5. **Universe:** diversified — 5 sectors × 10 markets, equal-weighted (for teaching); the diversification across many uncorrelated markets is what lets the few big winners pay for the many small losers.
6. **Mechanics:** trade on the **open the day after** a signal (no intraday stops in the core); end-of-day data only, no data-snooping.

**Two "basic" precursors** (ch.4), both ~0.9 correlated, demonstrating entry rules barely matter: (a) MA crossover **10/100** (fast above slow = long), always in market, reverses on cross; (b) breakout: enter on 50-day high/low, exit on 25-day low/high. The book recommends the *refined* core over both.

**Parameter stability & anti-optimization (ch.4, ch.8):** breakout 25/50/100-day and MA 5/50, 10/100, 20/200 are all viable — the *concept* is robust, the exact numbers are not the point. **Optimisation is explicitly rejected as curve-fitting** ("optimisations are evil"); vary concepts, not decimals.

**Three sanctioned tweaks (ch.8):** (a) **multi-timeframe** — run the same model at **1.5 / 3 / 6 ATR** stop distances, equal-weight, for smoother returns (but the single best diversifier is broadening the *universe*, not the stop knob); (b) **counter-trend overlay** — adding a tight-stop mean-reversion sleeve is "the most effective" single improvement; (c) **synthetic/spread contracts** (gold–silver, 3-2-1 crack spread) for extra uncorrelated streams; intraday hard stops only help at wider distances (≥4 ATR units).

### 2.7 Clenow's *Stocks on the Move* — a complete equity-momentum system (long-only)
Clenow's second book ports momentum to **single stocks** with a fully-specified, reproducible long-only system — the equity-momentum counterpart to Gray & Vogel (§6) and a deliberate contrast to his own futures trend system (§2.6). **Core thesis: "buy stocks that move up."** A stock rising faster than its peers tends to keep doing so a bit longer; momentum works in **bull and sideways** markets but **fails in bear markets** (in a crash, correlations → 1 and everything falls together), so an index regime filter is *mandatory* (Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*, Preface, "The Momentum Effect").

**Why not just trend-follow stocks?** Classic futures-style long/short trend following (the §2.6 rules: dual 50/100-day MA filter, 50-day breakout entry, 3×ATR trailing stop) **loses ~30% on S&P 500 stocks 2000–2014**. The short side is the biggest killer — **never short individual stocks** (a stock can rise without bound; shorting equities has negative expectancy here); the entire system is therefore **long-only** ("Does Trend Following Work on Stocks?").

**THE EXACT TRADING RULES (Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*, ch.10):**
1. **Trading cadence:** **trade only on Wednesdays** (the weekday is arbitrary — pick one and stick to it). All *calculations* use daily data; only the *trading action* is weekly.
2. **Ranking signal (the alpha):** rank **all S&P 500 stocks** by **volatility-adjusted momentum** = the **annualized exponential-regression slope of log price over the past ~90 trading days, multiplied by R²** of that regression. The exponential (log-linear) regression converts price to an annualized % return (the slope); **multiplying by R² penalizes choppy, low-quality trends and rewards smooth ones** — the equity analogue of Gray-Vogel's "Frog-in-the-Pan" smoothness preference (full metric detail in T03).
3. **Position sizing:** size each candidate by **ATR risk parity** targeting ~**10 bps of account value of daily price movement** per position (detail in T08), so every holding contributes roughly equal daily risk.
4. **Index regime filter:** **open new positions *only* when the S&P 500 index is above its own 200-day moving average** (detail in T08). This is the bear-market off-switch that keeps momentum out of crashes.
5. **Build the portfolio:** starting from the **top of the ranked list**, buy names (skipping any disqualified — see gap/MA filters below) until cash runs out (typically ~20–25 stocks).
6. **Roster rebalance — every Wednesday:** sell any holding that now fails the rules; refill from the top of the fresh ranking.
7. **Position rebalance — every *second* Wednesday:** reset each surviving position back to its current ATR-target size (re-equalizes risk as volatilities drift).

**Sell rules (roster rebalance, weekly) — sell a holding if ANY one is true (ch.9 "When to Sell"):**
   (a) it **drops out of the top 20% of the universe by rank** (= rank worse than 100 for the S&P 500);
   (b) it **trades below its own 100-day moving average** (a per-stock trend filter, distinct from the index filter);
   (c) it had a **gap larger than 15% in the past 90 days** (a discontinuous jump signals event/idiosyncratic risk that momentum can't price — exclude/sell it);
   (d) it **left the index**.
   **No stop-losses and no trailing stops** — exits are purely *rank / 100-day-MA / gap / index-membership* driven.

**Cash handling in bear markets (ch.13, 2002/2008):** when the index is **below** its 200-day MA, sold positions are **not replaced** → an automatic, gradual **scale-out to cash** as holdings fail and aren't refilled. When the index pokes back **above** the MA, immediately **rebuy a fully-loaded portfolio** from the top of the list. Idle "cash" should sit in money-market / T-bills, never an unsecured bank or broker balance.

**Relationship to the other equity-momentum recipe here:** Clenow's *Stocks on the Move* and Gray & Vogel's *Quantitative Momentum* (§6) are two fully-specified, robust **cross-sectional equity-momentum systems** that agree on the essentials (rank by an intermediate-horizon, *quality-weighted* momentum; long-only; avoid the jumpy/discontinuous; respect seasonality/regime) while differing in the knobs: Clenow uses a 90-day **exponential-regression-slope × R²** score with a weekly roster/index-MA process and a hard gap exclusion; Gray-Vogel use a 12-2 total-return score with an explicit Frog-in-the-Pan smoothness filter and a quarter-end-anticipating "Smart Rebalance." Run them as alternative or *combined* sleeves.

### 2.8 Carver's *Advanced Futures* divergent rules (transferable to equities/ETF)
Carver's *Advanced Futures Trading Strategies* extends the continuous-forecast framework (§2.5) with a numbered library of rules; the **divergent** (continuation, positive-skew) members below sit in the **60% divergent forecast bucket** and apply directly to a liquid equity/ETF universe. (Convergent members — carry, value, skew, mean-reversion, relative-value — are in §6/§2.5; cross-sectional momentum/carry in §6.)
- **Normalised trend (S17).** Identical to EWMAC (§2.5) except the *price itself* is first vol-normalised: build a recursive normalised price PN from the back-adjusted price and σ_p (×100, arbitrary start), then run the usual EWMAC(2,8)…(64,256) on PN with the same forecast scalars. Standalone it is essentially identical to plain trend (Jumbo SR 1.15 vs 1.14) and **0.97-correlated**, so add it only as *cheap extra diversification*, never as a replacement — trade **both** (30%/30% within the 60% trend bucket) (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.344-351).
- **Asset-class trend (S18).** Build a per-asset-class index from the equal-weighted normalised returns of all instruments in the class (may include untradeable/illiquid ones), EWMAC that index, and have every instrument in the class share the class forecast. Median-instrument SR rises (0.30 vs 0.23; **equities 0.12 vs 0.03**) but the *Jumbo* SR falls to 0.97 because intra-class diversification collapses (everything is all-long or all-short). **Verdict: most useful for small accounts that can hold only 1–2 names per class — and specifically helpful for equities** (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.356-364).
- **Breakout (S21).** Forecast = where price sits in its rolling range: raw = 40 × [(p − (max+min)/2) ÷ (max − min)] over horizon h, then EWMA-smooth (span = h/4); hits −20 at the rolling min, +20 at the rolling max. **No vol normalisation** (the range already encodes vol — this lowers its correlation to EWMAC and avoids huge forecasts after a trend has run). Horizons 10/20/40/80/160/320 days (scalars 0.60→0.74). Behaves like a gradual trend rule; Jumbo SR 1.17 (slightly above trend's 1.14) but **0.97-correlated with trend** — divergent, shares the 60% bucket with trend 30/30, add for cheap diversification only (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.390-402).
- **Acceleration (S23).** The *rate of change* of a trend forecast (forecast = "speed"; its change = "acceleration"): take a scaled EWMAC(N,4N) forecast and difference it over N days (already risk-adjusted, just re-scale; scalars accel8 1.87 → accel64 2.05, ∝√2 per doubling). Only accel8/16/32/64 are affordable. Four trend×accel regimes: agree → bigger position, disagree → smaller. **Correlations with EWMAC are low (≤0.76) and between accel variations very low (~0.5)** → a real edge: adding acceleration (30% of the divergent bucket) lifts the carry+trend SR **1.27 → 1.29** with higher alpha and lower beta (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.412-421).

## 3. Mean-reversion

**Idea.** Bet on convergence after a divergence; rationale = short-term supply/demand imbalances (e.g. index-inclusion buying), overshoot (Narang, p.30). You are paid for **providing liquidity** and bear **adverse-selection** risk. Safe default assumption is that prices mean-revert *unless expected earnings have changed* (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.117). Mean-reversion is more prevalent than trending. **Time-series vs cross-sectional MR (Chan, *AlgoTrading*, ch.2/ch.4):** *time-series* MR = a single *price* reverts to its own historical mean — tradeable **only** via a stationary or cointegrated series (a raw price level usually isn't stationary). *Cross-sectional* MR = the cumulative returns of basket members revert to the basket's cumulative return, i.e. short-term *relative* returns are serially anti-correlated; this is pervasive across stock baskets. Crucial subtlety: you can **never trade "mean reversion of returns"** (the returns of a random walk are already zero-mean) — *anti-serial-correlation of returns is exactly mean reversion of prices.* **Caveat:** oscillators stay pinned at extremes in trends — counter-trend entry on a still-rising market is a losing strategy; use momentum only to *time entries within a trend* (Kaufman, p.703; BestLoser p.59). The MA-as-trend-gate rule recurs everywhere: only fade overbought/oversold *in the direction of* a longer MA (50 over 200, or price-vs-50MA), require the oscillator to cross back through its threshold, optionally divergence, exit at the midline (RSI 50) or opposite extreme — the one reusable structural lesson across RSI/%R/Stochastic strategies (LightScalp, RSI/%R/Stochastic chapters).

### 3.1 Single-name / oscillator reversion
- **Distance-from-MA.** `distance = price − SMA`; short if distance > +threshold, long if < −threshold, flatten when it crosses zero; built for GLD/GDX-type proxies, produces long neutral stretches (Hilpisch, p.107). Davey's tests rank **Moving-Average Stretch #1 overall**, Connors RSI #2, Bollinger-Band Stretch #3 (p.43).
- **RSI (Wilder).** `RSI = 100 − 100/(1+RS)`, `RS = AvgUp/AvgDown` over 14 days; thresholds 30/70. If pinned, shorten to 10 and widen to 20/80. **2-day RSI** (thresholds 10/90, exit 1 day later) became *mean-reverting* on the S&P after ~1998 (Kaufman, p.725). **Connors RSI** is a top mean-reversion technique (Davey p.43).
- **Stochastic (Lane).** %K = 100×(Close − Min(Low,n))/(Max(High,n) − Min(Low,n)); %D = 3-MA of %K. Countertrend timing filtered by a trend (e.g. sell when %D>80 then crosses down, in a 60-day downtrend). **Never use a timing rule to exit** — it delays exits and creates real losses (Kaufman, p.735).
- **Fading momentum extremes.** Bands at ±2 std-dev of momentum changes (≈ 5% tails). Aggressive = enter on band cross; confirmed = wait for momentum to turn; exit at opposite band / zero ± 1 std / zero cross. Target slightly before zero for more wins, beyond zero for bigger wins (Kaufman, p.703).
- **VIX-based S&P reversion (VIX is mean-reverting).** *Conners CVR9 (buy):* today's VIX high > 10-day high, VIX closes below open, yesterday closed above open, today's range > prior 3-day ranges → buy S&P on close, exit in 3 days. *Conners RSI-VIX:* if S&P > 200-day MA AND 2-day RSI(VIX) > 90 AND today's VIX open > yesterday's close → buy S&P close, exit when 2-day RSI < 65 (Kaufman, p.1689).
- **Buy-on-Gap (intraday MR, equities — Chan, *AlgoTrading*, ch.4).** At the open, select the S&P 500 stocks whose **prev-low→today-open return < −1 std** (of 90-day close-to-close returns), then **filter to those whose open is *above* their 20-day MA** — a deliberate *momentum filter on a mean-reversion strategy* that improves consistency by screening out names suffering a genuine bad-news breakdown. Buy the 10 lowest of the survivors at the open, **exit at the close**. **APR 8.7%, Sharpe 1.5.** Rationale: panic-selling into the open is *liquidity*-driven (not fundamental) demand, so it reverts intraday.
- **Heikin-Ashi color-flip scalp.** Enter when candle color changes (trend turn), exit when color flips back; holds seconds-to-minutes. Trade only clear trends; skip when bodies are small / wicks long / no new HH-LL (consolidation). Default 1-min chart; drop to 30s/10s only in high volatility — "faster market → smaller timeframe" (LightScalp, ch.5–6). **Counter-trend backlash scalp:** in a strong run that gave no clean entry, scalp the near-inevitable pullback (often ⅓–½ of the prior move, occasionally the whole move); let HA candles dictate how much to take rather than a fixed target (ch.6).

### 3.2 Pairs / cointegration (relative-value)
**Idea.** Long one asset, short a related one sharing common factors; trade the **stationary spread** when it dislocates, betting on reversion to its (fixed or slowly-varying) mean (Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algo*, p.155). Canonical form of statistical arbitrage (Narang, p.30). Works on same-theme ETFs (gold miners vs gold), dual share classes (BRK.A/B, RDS-A/B), and on-the-run vs off-the-run Treasuries. **Cointegration theory** (Tsay, Tort/Tsay p.428–432): two unit-root-nonstationary log-price series are *cointegrated* if some linear combination is stationary (mean-reverting); for a k-dim system with h unit roots, cointegration exists when 0<h<k, with k−h cointegrating vectors. Financial intuition: a stock and its ADR, or two stocks loading the same APT factors, must move in unison or arbitrage exists. The **Error-Correction Model (ECM, Engle–Granger)** Δx_t = αβ′x_{t−1} + Σφ*ᵢΔx_{t−i} + (MA terms): columns of β are cointegrating vectors, β′x_{t−1} is the stationary "equilibrium error," and α loads the *speed of reversion* — avoiding the over-differencing/noninvertibility of differencing each series.

- **Classic spread z-score.** Spread = `dot(prices, weights)`, weights `[1, −β]` where β is the regression hedge ratio; z = (spread − rolling_mean)/rolling_std. **Rules:** z < −entry → long spread; z > +entry → short spread; exit toward the mean. Example (Halls-Moore, ARNC/UNG, QSTrader): β=1.213, lookback=15 bars, entry_z=1.5, exit_z=0.5, qty=10,000 units → Sharpe 1.22, maxDD 7.0% — *but gains concentrated in one month and the spread was not actually cointegrated (ADF p=0.349); implicit lookahead from fitting the hedge on the same sample* (p.381).
- **Formal pairs framework (Tsay).** Model the two log prices as cointegrated with **spread** `w_t = p_{1t} − γ·p_{2t}` stationary and mean-reverting; ECM form has reversion loadings α₁, α₂ of **opposite sign**. A book long 1 share of stock 1 / short γ of stock 2 has return `r = w_{t+i} − w_t` (the spread increment, *independent of the spread mean μ_w*). **Trading rule** (round-trip cost η, target deviation Δ, requires 2Δ > η): buy stock 1 / short γ·stock 2 when `w_t = μ_w − Δ`; unwind when `w_t = μ_w + Δ` for net profit **2Δ − η**. If instead Δ > η, exit at the *mean* for profit Δ − η (more trades, shorter holds). **Workflow (BHP–VALE):** regress `p_{1t} = β₀ + β₁p_{2t} + w_t`, confirm ŵ_t stationary via exponentially-decaying ACF + AR(2) fit + **ADF test** (statistic −6.04, reject unit root) or a Johansen test on a VAR(1); γ is the cointegrating (hedge) ratio (Tsay, p.446–451).
- **GLD/GDX (Chan).** Entry at ±2 SD of spread, exit within 1 SD → Sharpe ~2.3 train / ~1.5 test; tightening to ±1 SD entry / 0.5 SD exit lifted it to 2.9 / 2.1 (*Quantitative Trading*, p.55). Note GLD-GDX went **negative out-of-sample post-2011**; GLD-USO Bollinger pair stayed positive (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.219).
- **Linear (parameterless) MR benchmark (Chan, *AlgoTrading*, ch.2).** Hold units of the cointegrating "unit portfolio" equal to **−Z-score** of its price (deviation from a moving average ÷ moving std), with look-back = the **half-life** of mean reversion. Being parameterless it has the *least* data-snooping and *maximum* statistical significance (it trades continuously). It is impractical (capital is unbounded) but **proves an inefficiency exists** before you commit to a tradeable variant. EWA-EWC-IGE Johansen portfolio: **APR 12.6%, Sharpe 1.4**.
- **Bollinger-band practical MR (Chan, *AlgoTrading*, ch.3).** The deployable version of the linear benchmark: enter at `entryZscore` std-devs from the mean, exit at a smaller `exitZscore` (look-back may = half-life), holding 0 or ±1 unit (trivial sizing). **GLD-USO price-spread Bollinger (entry = 1, exit = 0): APR 17.8%, Sharpe 0.96**, vs 10.9% / 0.59 for the continuous linear version on the same pair.
- **Spread construction — price vs log-price vs ratio (Chan, *AlgoTrading*, ch.3).** Choose the spread type by what you want to hold fixed: **price spread** (hedge ratios are *#shares*) for a **fixed-share** position; **log-price spread** (hedge ratios are *fixed dollar weights*, needs daily rebalancing) for **fixed-capital** weights; **ratio** (no dynamic hedge ratio) when the pair is **not truly cointegrating** but only short-term mean-reverts — natural for FX cross-rates (EUR.GBP ≡ EUR.USD / GBP.USD). For GLD-USO, the **price spread with an adaptive (20-day) hedge ratio beat both** the log-spread and the ratio.
- **Stock pairs are *hard* now — prefer ETF pairs (Chan, *AlgoTrading*, ch.4).** Individual stocks behave like near-random-walks and **rarely cointegrate out-of-sample** (a firm's fortunes shift with management/competition — AAPL vs BBRY); a *portfolio* of stock pairs doesn't rescue it because the mean per-pair OOS return is ≤ 0. Decimalization shrank spreads, NBBO sizes are tiny (AAPL often just 100 shares) so intraday stock-pairs eat slippage, and short-sale/uptick constraints add risk. **Prefer ETF pairs** — basket economics change slowly (EWA-EWC; RTH-XLP; GLD-GDX).
- **ETF ↔ component-stock arbitrage (Chan, *AlgoTrading*, ch.4).** Select individual stocks that each cointegrate with SPY (Johansen test, ≥90% confidence over a training year → ~98 names), confirm the **equal-weight long-only basket** of them cointegrates with SPY (log prices), then trade the **SPY-vs-basket spread**. **APR 4.5%, Sharpe 1.3** — a slow, capacity-friendly equity-stat-arb engine.
- **Kalman dynamic-hedge pairs (preferred over rolling OLS).** Treat the hedge ratio (and offset) as a hidden **time-varying state**, estimated online — avoids the rolling-window lookback free parameter (Halls-Moore, p.191). State θ_t=[slope,intercept]; observation y; forecast error e_t = y − F·θ; prediction var Q_t; **long the spread when e_t < −√Q_t, short when e_t > +√Q_t, exit on band re-cross** (1-sigma bands). Reference recipes: **TLT/IEI** Treasuries (δ=1e-4, v=1e-3, N=2000, CAGR 7.66%, Sharpe 0.65, maxDD 17.5%) (Halls-Moore, p.394); **EWA/EWC** (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.76; earlier CAGR 26.2%, Sharpe 2.4 with diagonal-B constraint).
- **AR/ARMA on price level.** Fit AR(p) by MLE, pick p by BIC, **buy if forecast > price**. AUD.USD 1-min p=10, |φ1|≈0.99 (near-random-walk but tradable); BTC.USD p=16, φ1=0.685 (strongly mean-reverting). **Both require midprice fills** to show the headline returns (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.61, 203). ARIMA(p,1,q) on log price ≡ ARMA(p,q) on log returns.
- **VAR(p)/VEC for baskets.** Model a sector jointly (e.g. computer-hardware SPX names); sector-neutral weights `wi = (ri − ⟨r⟩)/Σ|rj − ⟨r⟩|` → ~48% annualized, Sharpe 0.9; VEC error-correction signs reveal which names mean-revert (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.67).
- **Survival.** Pairs survive via CPPI (lower a losing strategy's leverage toward zero) + diversification — keep ≥10 live strategies with continuous births/deaths (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.219). **Key risk:** a liquidity crisis in one leg prevents close-out (Leshik, p.22).

### 3.3 Cross-sectional / residual reversion
- **Short-term reversal (Khandani-Lo).** Buy worst prior-1-day-return stocks, short best; equal-weight, dollar-neutral. Profitable pre-cost over years but **tiny-cap-driven**; flipped from −3.19 to +0.78 Sharpe (after cost) just by executing at the **OPEN instead of the close** (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.61, 65). **Concrete linear-weight version (Chan, *AlgoTrading*, ch.4):** weight each name `w_i = −(r_i − mean(r_j)) / Σ|r_j − mean(r_j)|` near the close (normalized to $1 gross), close-to-close → **APR 13.7%, Sharpe 1.3 on the S&P 500** (+30% in 2008, +11% in 2011 — both true OOS). The **intraday open→close** variant earns a headline **APR 73%, Sharpe 4.7** but doubles costs and adds signal noise. The same cross-sectional engine can rank by **other factors (e.g. P/E) instead of return**. Jansen variant: z-score of last monthly return vs trailing-12m mean, long bottom decile / short top decile, as a Zipline `CustomFactor` (p.205).
- **Cross-sectional IV reversion (options).** Per stock, long the lowest-IV call and short the highest-IV put (nearest expiry ≥28 days); hold 1 day → ~1.1% daily (likely inflated by OTM leverage + midprice fills) — proves the effect exists (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.153).

## 4. Breakout / channel / volatility-expansion

**Idea.** Enter when price clears a defined boundary, betting accumulation/distribution has ended and a directional imbalance is taking over. **Breakout entries beat other entry types across studies** (Davey, p.121) — but **most breakouts fail**; the break zone is high-volatility/low-liquidity/crowded, so slippage matters most here and realized loss can be a multiple of intended risk (Grimes, p.83). Best breakouts are *pre-qualified*: large-scale imbalance with **higher lows pressing into resistance** before the break; keep a watch list (Grimes, p.48).

- **N-day breakout (core benchmark).** Buy when today's high (or close, more conservative) crosses the highest high of the past N days; sell on N-day low; always in the market, reversing. Risk = distance to the opposite N-day extreme (larger N = larger risk). 2000–2017 across 17 markets: avg "best" period ≈ **93 days**, 79% of tested periods profitable, net profit >15× point-and-figure (Kaufman, p.451–457).
- **Donchian.** 4-Week Rule: long on a break above the prior 4 weeks' high, short below the 4-week low. **20/40 channel**: buy if today's high > 40-day high; exit long if low < 20-day low (basis of the Turtles) (Kaufman, p.453, 663). Donchian 5/20 adds a volatility-penetration filter: price must cross the 20-MA *and* exceed the prior 1-day penetration by ≥1 ATR (p.660).
- **Turtle system (Dennis/Eckhardt).** **S1:** enter 20-day breakout, exit on 10-day opposite extreme; *skip entry if the prior S1 trade won*. **S2:** enter 55-day breakout, exit on 20-day; no filter. Sizing: L = 20-day ATR × big-point-value; stop = 2L; add a unit every +L (max 5 units); position limits 4/single, 12 net; cut size 20% per 10% drawdown (Kaufman, p.463).
- **Volatility breakout.** ATR(n); buy if close rises > k×ATR from prior close, sell if it falls > k×ATR; `k≈2.0` (k≈3 for the VIX/Bookstaber variant). Few trades, high reliability, trades in the direction of the shock (Kaufman, p.629, 1693). Davey ranks **Volatility Break** a top-3 entry (p.121).
- **Keltner 10-Day MA Rule.** 10-day MA of (H+L+C)/3, band = 10-day avg of H−L range; buy above the upper band, sell below the lower, always reverse (Kaufman, p.630).
- **Bollinger Band breakout (Tomasini).** Enter when price crosses the band, exit on the mid MA; default length **60**, distance **2 std-dev**; deployed across 7 markets with *identical* parameters (symmetric long/short) — robustness across markets, not per-market tuning (Tomasini, App.1, p.201–204).
- **LUXOR (trend + breakout confirmation, Tomasini).** Two SMAs (fast/slow) define direction; an entry is taken **only when price confirms the cross** by exceeding the crossover bar's high (long) or low (short) via a *stop order* — this filters out false MA crossovers. Built symmetrically (equal long/short logic) so it is not adapted to a market's directional bias; profitability can still differ long vs short from the market's own trend (Tomasini, p.39–46, p.114).
- **Triangle / low-volatility breakout (Tomasini).** Instead of coding the chart pattern, detect "**volatility at a 300-bar low AND a flat 200-bar MA**," then place symmetric long/short stop entries. Very low time-in-market (~1–2%) makes it nearly *uncorrelated* across markets — a strong diversifier (Tomasini, App.2, p.209–212).
- **Point-and-figure.** Box ≈ 20-day ATR; classic **3-box reversal**. Buy = X one box above the last X-column high; sell = O below the last O-column low. Best stock signals: ascending triple-top (buy), triple-bottom breakout (sell). Price objectives via horizontal/vertical counts (Kaufman, p.418).
- **Pivot-point breakout.** Buy above the prior pivot high / sell below the prior pivot low; 1–3 days = short-term, 10–20 days ≈ macrotrend (Kaufman, p.354). Floor-trader pivots: `P=(H+L+C)/3, R1=2P−L, S1=2P−H` (Harris/BrokerTest, p.261).
- **Compression filter.** Multiple days with range smaller than 3–4 days prior improves the reliability of the subsequent breakout (NR4, inside day) (Kaufman, p.353; Crabel below).
- **Failed breakout (high-probability reversal).** When everyone leans the wrong way, the reversal is powerful — the **failed breakout is one of the best setups** (Grimes, p.82). The **Failure Test** (spring/upthrust): price penetrates prior S/R then fails to continue; stop just beyond the failed extreme, target back across the range (long-shadow candle, no closes beyond the level) (Grimes, p.66).

## 5. Pullback & continuation setups (discretionary → rules)

These come mostly from discretionary traders; encode them as rules with explicit triggers. The universal structure is **Setup → Trigger → Follow-through (STF)** — a high-accuracy pattern is useless without a trigger and exit (Bernstein, p.3). Grimes' master taxonomy: **the Four Trades** — Trend Continuation, Trend Termination, S/R Holding, S/R Breaking/Failing — most traders need ≥2 counterbalancing setups (p.76).

- **Trend Continuation (Grimes).** Identify a trend (higher 2nd-order pivots for up); enter on a pullback (simple or 2-legged complex); stop where the trend is decisively violated; take profit at/just beyond the prior swing high. **Highest-probability of the Four Trades** (p.78). Trend Termination is counter-trend, low win-rate but large reward/risk — the classic account-killer is *adding to losers* into a parabolic blow-off; iron discipline required (p.80).
- **The Pullback Trade (GMan/Bellafiore signature).** Find an above-average-volume opening drive **in the direction of the higher-timeframe trend** (15m aligns with 60m, 60m with daily). Wait for a **lower-volume pullback of ≥4 candles**. Enter only when momentum resumes and a bar **closes outside the trendline** — never buy the bottom. Stop just beyond the swing low; T1 = prior swing high, T2 = trendline break (then trail by redrawing). **Embrace losing the first try** — the second entry is higher-probability and where size goes (~1-in-5 wins, pays big); no third attempt (PlayBook, p.397).
- **ABCD pattern (Aziz, long).** A = impulsive rally to new HOD (B); pullback forms higher-low support (C). Enter near C once it holds (don't chase B, don't wait for D); stop just below C; sell ½ at D, raise stop to breakeven, trail the rest (p.57).
- **Bull Flag momentum (Aziz, low-float scalp).** Pole = several large up-candles; flag = sideways consolidation. Enter on a break above the consolidation high; stop = break below it; sell ½ into the move, stop to breakeven, trail. **Only take the 1st/2nd flag**, not the 3rd+ (PlayBook agrees) (p.60).
- **Gap and Go / Opening Drive (PlayBook).** Stock gaps up >3% on relative volume; find consolidation above the gap (or a pullback to prior resistance now acting as support); enter on a break above consolidation that holds gap levels; add where buyers hold the bid (p.65). Opening Drive's best precursor = an after-hours news gap that *holds higher* in premarket (p.359).
- **Bottom/Top Reversal (Aziz).** Bottom: 5–10 down candles, price outside the lower Bollinger Band, RSI <10, indecision candle (Doji/hammer); enter on the first 5-min candle to make a new high; stop = low of day; target next support/VWAP/MA. Top reversal mirrors (RSI >90) (p.64).
- **Alligator trend-follow (template, LightScalp).** Enter on line alignment ("eating") in the trend direction with price beyond the Jaw; confirm with Accelerator Oscillator sign; exit when lines intertwine ("sleeping"); trail on the Jaw (LightScalp, Alligator chapter).
- **Anti-pattern caution.** Counter-trend / "anti-breakout" entries **lose once costs are added** (Davey, p.121). Retail is systematically counter-trend at extremes (IG client sentiment net-short 60–71% on all-time-high days) — a *fadeable* signal source, not a model to imitate (BestLoser, p.126).

## 6. Statistical arbitrage, factor & style-premia signals

> The alpha *catalog* (formulaic factors, 101 Alphas, risk-model construction) lives in **`04-quant-alpha-factors-and-portfolio-construction.md`**. This section covers the strategy logic.

- **StatArb premise.** Statistical-arbitrage opportunities (mispricings) exist (EMH-skeptic). Two-stage build: (1) score each security on composite signals (high = long, low = short); (2) combine into a market/factor-neutral portfolio using a risk model (Barra/MSCI, Northfield) (Guo et al., p.6). Horizon: minutes-to-months.
- **Fundamental law of active management.** IR ≈ IC × √breadth — alpha needs both forecast skill (IC) *and* many independent bets. This is *why combining many weak-but-uncorrelated signals beats one strong one* (Jansen, p.59).
- **Factor signals (zero-cost long-short portfolios).** **Value** = long cheap / short rich by B/P or E/P (151Strategies, p.42; Narang invert ratios to yields so they behave across negative earnings, p.35). **Quality** = long high / short low (leverage, earnings stability, accruals, fraud risk); protects in flight-to-quality, terrible in euphoria (Narang, p.40). **Low-volatility / betting-against-beta** = tilt to low-vol/low-beta names (151Strategies, p.62). **Combine value + momentum** — the canonical blend of two zero-cost signals (151Strategies, p.61).
- **Ilmanen's style premia as strategies (the harvestable factor menu).** Treat each as a long-run risk/behavioral premium you can systematically harvest:
  - **Value** — long cheap/short rich. Mildly timeable: *value performance persists ~1 month* (momentum-of-value) and widens opportunity when the **ex-ante value spread** is wide (dispersion of valuation ratios) *but not* justified by an equally-wide growth-expectations gap (Asness et al.); the 2000 and 2007 spread extremes were good timing signals (Ilmanen, Ch.12.3). Style timing in general is fraught with overfitting.
  - **Momentum / trend** — see §2.2, §2.6 (Ilmanen Ch.14).
  - **Carry** — the universal "long high-yield / short low-yield" signal; *currency carry* has earned an attractive Sharpe for 50+ years in G10 (higher in EM), exploiting persistent *yield levels* (more persistent than changes), contradicting uncovered interest parity. **Refinements:** January seasonal (~2% vs ~0.5% other months; a negative January has preceded the worst full-year drawdowns), ex-ante carry-spread sizing, and risk-aversion "conditioners" for crash timing (rising vol/correlation, tightening funding-currency liquidity, falling risky assets) — worst-decile recent carry losses or sharply rising implied vol predict negative next-week carry, so a rear-view stop-loss discipline helps (Ilmanen, Ch.13.1, 13.3).
  - **Defensive / low-vol / BAB** — leverage-constraint premium (Carver's framing), Ilmanen's low-beta tilt.
  - **Why carry works (the template for ALL carry/short-vol styles, Ch.13.4):** carry resembles **selling lottery tickets / catastrophe insurance** — frequent small gains, rare violent losses clustered in flight-to-quality crashes (Oct 1987, Oct 1998, Oct 2008 all ~−9 to −11%). Negative skew ("up by the stairs, down by the elevator") *plus terrible timing of losses* (coincidence with a high market price of risk) warrants the premium — and the timing matters more than the skew magnitude (crash-hedged carry keeps most of its return with *positive* skew, so skew alone can't explain it). "Alpha morphed into beta": carry's equity beta rose from ~0.1–0.2 to ~0.5 by 2007–09 as it got crowded. Carry is a near-universal signal — a generic proxy for any risk premium or behavioral undervaluation — and works across asset classes (Ch.13.5).
  - **Time-varying premia (Ch.12.3, 16.1):** premia are countercyclical and memory-driven — they spike after stress (BRP/inflation-premium early-1980s, liquidity premium 2007–08, ERP post-Depression) and decay at varying speeds; success breeds return-chasing inflows → overvaluation/overcrowding → worse selloffs. Trend/momentum show *abnormally high Sharpe ahead of scheduled announcements* (payrolls, OPEC) as risk-averse losers capitulate (Ch.14.3).
- **Tortoriello's multi-factor equity screens (*Quantitative Strategies for Achieving Alpha*).** The empirical core is the **single-factor backtest → composite multi-factor model** workflow: rank the equity universe into deciles/quantiles on each candidate factor, measure the monotonic excess-return spread (top vs bottom decile) and information content, keep only factors with a smooth, economically-sensible relationship, then **combine the survivors into a composite score** that drives a long (top) / short or avoid (bottom) book. Factor families screened: **value** (price-to-fundamentals — earnings, book, sales, cash flow yields), **profitability/quality** (margins, ROE/ROIC, earnings stability), **cash-flow & accruals** (cash-based earnings beat accrual-heavy earnings; high accruals are a red flag), **capital-allocation/growth** (asset growth, external financing as *negative* signals — bloated/issuing firms underperform), **price action/momentum**, and **size**. The repeatable lesson: a *multi-factor composite* (e.g. value + quality + momentum) materially outperforms any single factor and smooths the decile monotonicity — the equity analogue of combining weak-but-uncorrelated signals (Tortoriello, *QuantStratAlpha*).
- **Gray & Carlisle's *Quantitative Value* — a deep-value + quality stock-selection strategy.** A fully-systematic long book that screens the equity universe in a strict **sequential funnel** (the metric detail and exact formulas live in **14**): (1) **avoid permanent loss** — discard fraud/manipulation/earnings-management risk and financial-distress/bankruptcy risk first (forensic-accounting and distress screens), because a value strategy's worst enemy is the value *trap*; (2) **buy cheap** — rank survivors by a deep-value price multiple (the book argues **EV/EBIT** is the most robust single value metric, beating P/E and P/B); (3) **buy quality** — within the cheap names prefer high **economic moat / franchise quality** and **financial strength** (high and stable margins, ROIC, low accruals, conservative financing); (4) hold an equal-weight basket of the top names, rebalanced annually. The thesis is explicitly Graham-meets-Buffett made mechanical: *quality screens keep you out of cheap-for-a-reason names, and the disciplined model beats the human analyst* because it removes behavioral error. **Corroborative "smart-money" signals (ch.9) — used to *confirm* a long thesis, never as standalone shorts:**
  - **Buybacks:** repurchase announcements that cite undervaluation beat the market for ~4 years (Ikenberry-Lakonishok-Vermaelen); the **buyback-yield** (share-count reduction) top decile beat the market by ~3.8%/yr while the biggest *issuers* underperformed by ~4.5%/yr. Caveat: discount managements that announce but don't complete, and note most firms buy back at *peaks* (the Singleton/Buffett type who buy at troughs are rare).
  - **Insider buying:** legal insider *purchases* (not sales or option exercises) signal — the trick is separating **"opportunistic"** from **"routine"** trades (Cohen-Malloy-Pomorski): opportunistic insider trades earn ~8%/yr, routine ones earn nothing. Larger trades, *clusters* of purchases, trades near earnings, and trades in value stocks signal more.
  - **Activism (13D):** activist 13D filings → ~5–7% announcement return with **no reversal**, plus a further +11–18% over the next year; cloning Buffett's 13F a month late still beat the market ~14%/yr.
  - **Short interest:** high short-interest ratio is bearish (but hard to short profitably given borrow costs); **low** short interest on easy-to-borrow names is bullish (~6%/yr). Use it mainly to **avoid heavily-shorted longs** ("short money is smart money").
  - **Relationship to value's behavioral root:** *Quantitative Value* is the systematic deep-value counterpart to the momentum recipes (§2.7, §6) — Gray & Vogel frame momentum and value as **"two sides of the same behavioral-bias coin"** (overreaction → value; underreaction → momentum), so a value book and a momentum book are natural diversifiers.
- **Gray & Vogel's *Quantitative Momentum* recipe (full equity momentum system).** Cross-sectional momentum built for robustness, three stacked ingredients:
  1. **Generic 12-2 momentum signal:** cumulative **total return (incl. dividends) over the past 12 months, skipping the most recent month** (months t-12 → t-2). The last month is dropped to avoid the **short-term reversal** effect (QuantMomentum, ch.5/ch.8).
  2. **Frog-in-the-Pan (FIP) quality filter:** prefer *smooth* (continuous information) over *jumpy* (discrete) momentum — investors underreact more to a series of small gradual moves than to one dramatic jump, so smooth momentum continues longer and decays slower. **Formula:** over the prior **252 trading days**, `ID = sign(PRET) × (%neg-days − %pos-days)` where PRET = the 12-2 signal; for a winner that is mostly up-days, %neg−%pos is negative → **more negative FIP = smoother = better** (equivalently report `%Positive − %Negative`, higher = smoother). Splitting the top momentum decile in half by FIP: high-quality momentum returned **17.14% vs 13.02%** CAGR (1927–2014), >4%/yr spread, with better Sharpe/Sortino and smaller drawdown (ch.6/ch.8).
  3. **Seasonality / "Smart Rebalance":** momentum is highly seasonal — profits concentrate in **quarter-ending months** (Mar/Jun/Sep/Dec, strongest December) from institutional **window dressing** and individual **tax-loss selling**, while **January is strongly negative** (reversal of tax-loss flows). Quarter-ending months averaged ~3.10%/mo vs 0.59% other ex-January months (Sias). Since trading Dec→Jan directly is impractical, **time the rebalance**: rebalance at the **close of February, May, August, November** to be positioned *ahead* of window-dressers for the quarter-ending months. "Smart" beat "Dumb" (Dec/Mar/Jun/Sep) 15.97% vs 15.06% CAGR (ch.7/ch.8).
  - **Thesis & guardrails:** momentum is a sustainable anomaly driven (partly) by **mispricing** — investor *underreaction to positive news* (Barberis–Shleifer–Vishny regime-shift; reinforced by the disposition effect, limited attention, Hong–Stein gradual diffusion). Momentum is the *mirror image of value's overreaction* ("two sides of the same behavioral-bias coin"). **Momentum ≠ growth:** momentum = strong *past price* performance *independent of fundamentals* ("prices aren't everything; they are the only thing"), only ~21% name overlap with growth (1963–2013). Watch the horizon: **1-month → reversal**, **3–5yr → reversal** (DeBondt-Thaler), only **intermediate ~12-month → continuation** (the exploitable anomaly) (ch.3, ch.5).
- **Carver's *Advanced Futures* convergent & relative-value strategies (transferable to equities/ETF).** The **convergent** (reversion/equilibrium, negative-skew) members of Carver's strategy library, all built as continuous vol-standardised forecasts (§2.5) and sitting in the **40% convergent bucket**. Most map directly onto a cross-sectional equity/ETF universe and complement the divergent trend/breakout rules of §2.8.
  - **Carry (S10, the core convergent rule).** Carry = the expected return *if the price/curve stays still*, annualised; raw carry forecast = (annualised carry) ÷ (daily price-return σ), then EWMA-smoothed (spans → carry5/20/60/120), scaled (forecast scalar ≈ 30), capped ±20. For futures it is the near-vs-distant-expiry price differential; **bonds' carry = yield-curve slope; equities' carry ≈ dividend yield − funding cost** (cf. §2.5). Buy positive-carry instruments, sell negative-carry. It is *convergent* and anchors the 40% bucket (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.379, 430-431).
  - **Cross-sectional momentum (S19) — RELATIVE VALUE.** Relative price `R = PN(instrument) − A(asset-class index)`; measure average outperformance over horizon H days and EWMA-smooth (span = H/4); no extra vol divide (already normalised). Horizons 5/10/20/40/80/160 days (scalars 56.1→296.8), cap ±20. **Standalone it is *poor* (Jumbo SR 0.34, alpha n.s.)** because most trend profit is the asset-class move it deliberately strips out — **but it is correlation ~0 with carry and ~0.31 with trend**, so Carver keeps a **12% allocation purely for diversification**. This is the *futures-library* analogue of the equity cross-sectional-momentum book (§2.7, §6) (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.367-377).
  - **Cross-sectional carry (S20) — RELATIVE VALUE.** Smooth raw carry (single 90-day span), then subtract the **median** carry across the asset class → a relative-carry forecast (scalar 50, cap ±20). Standalone Jumbo SR 0.31 (vs 0.94 for directional carry) with ugly drawdowns, but ~0.31-correlated with directional carry → blend **70% directional / 30% cross-sectional** (or split the 40% carry bucket 20/20) (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.379-383).
  - **Value (S22) — RELATIVE VALUE / very-slow mean-reversion.** "Buy what's cheap" ≈ buy multi-year *under*performers: take the relative price R (from S19), average outperformance over **H = 5 years** (~256 d/yr), EWMA span 30 d, then **negate** (the mean-reversion sign); scalar 7.27, cap ±20, turnover 8.7. **Standalone it loses money** (Jumbo SR −0.06, ~−1.0%/yr) but is correlation −0.03 with trend and 0.16 with carry, so a **small 5% allocation lifts the trend+carry SR 1.27 → 1.29** ("financial alchemy": a diversifying loser with negative beta improves the whole). Convergent; **significantly positive SR in equities and bonds only** — i.e. the version most worth porting to stocks (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.405-411).
  - **Skew (S24) — convergent, RELATIVE-VALUE-capable.** Negative-skew assets earn a premium (investors overpay for lottery-like positive skew). Raw forecast = **−skew** of returns over 60/120/240-day windows (≥3 months for robustness), EWMA-smoothed (span = window/4), scalars ~33/37/39, cap ±20; skew is **self-normalising** (its formula already divides by σ), so no extra risk-adjust. Carver trades the **absolute** variant (long if skew < 0). Standalone Jumbo SR 0.80, but ~0.14-correlated with both trend and carry → as a **20% convergent allocation it lifts the carry+trend SR 1.27 → 1.38** (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.423-440). **The chapter's broader lesson — the "strategy-type" menu any forecast can be traded as:** *absolute / demeaned / relative / cross-sectional / asset-class-cross-sectional / aggregated-asset-class / static* — these toggles decide whether a signal keeps asset-class biases, does global timing, or makes only within-class relative bets, and are the general recipe for turning *any* raw signal into a directional **or** market-neutral equity strategy.
  - **Relative-value spreads / triplets / calendars (S28–30) — RELATIVE VALUE.** Build a synthetic instrument (spread `R_{a/b}` = long a / short b in a risk-hedging ratio R:1; triplet; 4-leg condor) and apply the *same* EWMAC and carry rules to the synthetic spread price. Findings (US-bond complex): spreads/triplets carry **low or even negative correlation** to outright trades (highly diversifying) and surprisingly good skew when trend-followed — **but** their standalone SRs are ~0 ("diworsification"), tails are fat, and they need more leverage, capital and cost. Worthwhile mainly for large institutions already trading every outright (the equity analogue is pairs/share-class/sector-spread trading in §3.2) (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.512-553).
  - **Fast mean reversion (S26 / "safer" S27) — convergent, separate execution sleeve.** Hourly: equilibrium = EWMA(price, 5-day span); raw forecast = equilibrium − last price (long when price is below equilibrium), risk-adjusted by daily σ, scalar 9.3, cap ±20. Standalone Jumbo SR 0.80 (≈ trend) but **vicious negative skew (−1.46) — it "catches falling knives,"** yet correlation −0.28 with trend / +0.15 with carry makes it a strong diversifier. Turnover ~140/yr is affordable *only* via **limit-order execution** (post bracketing single-lot limits at the prices that imply ±1 contract, paying no spread). The **"safer" S27** adds two fixes that produce the "holy grail" (skew *and* SR both improve, median 0.41 → 0.54): **(1) a trend overlay** — zero the MR forecast whenever an EWMAC(16,64) trend has the *opposite* sign (buy dips only *under* an uptrend — this replaces a stop-loss), and **(2) vol attenuation** — scale the forecast down when current vol is high vs its 10-yr history. Must run in a **separate sleeve** (different, limit-order execution; cannot be forecast-combined with the daily rules) and cap at ≤20% of the convergent bucket. *This is the direct futures analogue of the equity mean-reversion sleeve (§3) and of Chan's MR strategies, with Carver's explicit warning that MR's negative skew demands a trend gate and disciplined fills* (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.477-511).
  - **Seasonal / calendar effects — Carver deliberately does NOT trade them.** He excludes January / turn-of-month / Friday effects and cross-market forecasting: they are market-specific, demand testing dozens-to-thousands of variants (so spurious patterns are guaranteed), and have **no durable risk-premium rationale** ("very few large pension funds are consistently stupid"). A useful counterpoint to Gray-Vogel's tradeable momentum-seasonality and Bernstein's seasonal key-dates (§7) — seasonality is exploitable only where a *flow mechanism* (window-dressing, tax-loss selling, storage) supplies the rationale (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.440-442).
- **Alpha combo / rotation.** A portfolio of many alphas combined with weights; **alpha rotation** rotates an ETF among sub-strategies (151Strategies, p.59,63).
- **Bayesian α — injecting qualitative/discretionary views (QEPM).** Rather than setting α ad hoc, assign a **prior distribution** to α consistent with non-data information (analyst rankings, research reports, scenarios — z-score-based or scenario-based priors), then compute the **posterior** (Bayesian α) combining data + view. The disciplined way to fold discretionary or alternative-data signals into a factor model (QEPM, Ch.14).
- **Building the market-neutral / long-short book (QEPM).** Four constructions (Ch.16.8.4): (1) long the **factor-exposure-matched** tracking portfolio + short the benchmark (dollar- & β-neutral by construction); (2) long the **sector-matched** tracking portfolio + short the benchmark; (3)/(4) **direct optimization** imposing weights-sum-to-0 (dollar-neutral) plus either factor-neutral or sector-neutral, *dropping* full-investment/no-short/tracking-error constraints and bounding *absolute* weights for diversification/liquidity. Result (Ch.17.6): optimized long-short books beat the "short-the-S&P" versions (~13.1% / 11.2% vs much lower) precisely because the optimizer shorts *low-return names* instead of the high-return index. **Dollar-neutral return ≈ long-leg return − short-leg return + cash on proceeds** (Ch.15.2.3). Standard constraints (Ch.16.6): TE ≤ 2/5/10%, 5% single-stock cap, full investment, and a **liquidity cap** (weight ≤ 33% of ADDTV for a $500M book; ≤ ⅓ of ADDTV). **Leverage caveat** (Ch.15.2.3, 17.5): levering a stock-and-cash book to β*=2 via index futures (`N_f = {[β* + (ξ−1)β_s]/(β_f·q·S)}·V`) raised average return ~15%/yr but risk rose 18.8% → 33%, so IR/Sharpe *fell slightly* — **leverage amplifies α only when the market trends up.**
- **Index / cash-and-carry arbitrage** (core equities/ETF). When the future ≠ fair value of the cash index, trade futures vs a basket of the underlying (a **program trade**, ≥15 orders ≥$1M) to enforce the law of one price. **Execution risk:** both legs must hedge near-simultaneously — in 1987 arbitrageurs *stopped* when stock-side fills were uncertain and futures fell to a >10% discount (Harris, p.489, 550). Index/options arb is *not* risk-free (financing, dividend, nonlinear hedges).
- **Arbitrage as a hedge-portfolio trade.** Build legs/hedge ratios to minimize basis risk; trade when `arbitrage spread = basis − fair-value(basis)` breaches arbitrage bounds. **Pure** = mean-reverting basis with a convergence mechanism (low long-run risk, real margin-call risk); **risk arb** = nonstationary basis (Harris, p.349). Arbitrageurs are *porters* of liquidity, not net suppliers (p.404).
- **Cross-asset spreads (transferable, flag carefully).** Soybean crush, crack spread (3-2-1), calendar butterfly (term-structure capture, best around roll week, now algo-dominated); bull/bear calendar spreads (Kaufman, p.1144–52, 1191). FX **carry** = buy the higher-yield cross, gated by an interest-rate-differential threshold over long-term trend-following per crossrate; funding-currency concentration (JPY/CHF) is the key risk (Kaufman, p.1161; Ilmanen Ch.13). VIX-futures basis / volatility carry: harvest the vol risk premium (IV/HV averages ~1.35) — **caveat: roll/contango loss** decays VXX/VXZ (151Strategies, p.80; Kaufman, p.1172).
- **Vol/correlation strategies (cross-asset).** Short-and-hold VX front future at Kelly beats long SPY but both ~90% drawdowns; **event straddle short** (sell the straddle the day after a scheduled report) profits where buying it loses; **dispersion** (long stock straddles, short index straddle, vega-neutral) = short implied correlation, same tail risk as short vol (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.122–143). Bennett maps option structures onto a **2×2 of {market direction} × {implied rich/cheap}**: long vol = straddle/strangle; short-vol-but-floored = butterfly/condor; directional + vol-sell = call/put spreads, risk reversals, 1×2 ratio spreads, ladders; calendars trade the term structure (long calendar = short gamma + term-structure risk) (Bennett, p.34–35). See §6.1.

### 6.1 Volatility & variance-premium strategies (cross-ref §13)
Equity-index implied vol systematically exceeds realized — a harvestable **variance risk premium** — but the payoff is short-skew (carry-like) and crash-exposed.

- **Call overwriting** (sell OTM call vs long stock): profits because implied runs ~1–2 vol pts above subsequent realized; **underperforms in volatile, strongly *rising* markets**; **shortest maturity best** (most theta — 12×1m > 4×3m > 1×12m, higher return at higher path risk); **strike slightly OTM** (~103–104% for 1m, ~107–108% for 3m on SX5E up-markets, or a fixed ~25Δ); best on indices (richer implied, tighter spreads). Cuts return-volatility but **not downside** (Sortino ≈ long-only) (Bennett, p.20–26).
- **Protection (long puts / spreads).** Puts are cheapest in calm markets and too dear in turbulent ones (exactly when most wanted); rarely worth paying >1–2%. Cheapen via **put spread** (sell an OTM put — driven by vol *level*), **collar** (sell an OTM call — skew-sensitive, ≈ short underlying), or **put-spread collar** (sell both — best when vol is high). Rules of thumb: a 6m 90% put ≈ a 95-85% put-spread cost; an OTM put sold ≈ ⅓ of the long put. Median protection horizon ≈ 4 months; **short-dated for a crash, long-dated for a bear market** (Bennett, p.26–31).
- **Confident pure-direction view → buy ITM** (high delta ≈ the forward) or just use futures; an option's only edge over the underlying is **convexity** (max loss = premium) (Bennett, p.12–13).
- **Earnings / jump trading.** `total vol² = diffusive vol² + jump vol²`; back out σ_jump from the expiry *just after* the event (hedge by shorting the expiry before, or use forward vol as the diffusive estimate). **Post-event vol ≈ ¾ of pre-event vol**; implied 1-day jump return `≈ e^(2σ_jump²)·(2·N(σ_jump) − 1)`. Only worthwhile near-dated (Bennett, p.124–126). (Cf. Chan's event-straddle short, §7.)
- **Relative value via options.** Trade mean-reversion of two related names (cross-holding/share-class fundamentals, or a statistical sector pair) in cash, options, or **outperformance options** (Margrabe — these are **short correlation**, cheapest when the two underlyings' vols are equal; use on *correlated* assets, sell rich correlation) (Bennett, p.110–118).
- **Long vol is a *poor* equity hedge.** Variance-swap returns are only ~−70% correlated to equities (best at 9–12m maturity, ~the 8-month vol mean-reversion) and vol is *overpriced*, so the hedging cost outweighs the diversification; **rolling short-dated VIX/vStoxx futures bleed carry and have lost money even while equities fell** (Bennett, p.86–87). Same conclusion as Ilmanen: trend/CTAs are the better crisis diversifier than long vol.
- **Natenberg's directional + vol hedging structures (point to 13 for the option math).** A practitioner menu for equity/ETF positions, chosen by **price-vs-value (implied vs expected vol)** plus the worst case the position must survive (Natenberg, *Option Volatility and Pricing*, ch.17): **protective put/call** = synthetic long option (caps the loss, costs premium); **covered write / buy-write / cash-secured put** = synthetic short option (a credit cushion, capped upside); **collar** = protective option + covered option = a vertical spread (bull/bear collar), cheap, capped both ways, "zero-cost" when the two premiums match. **Portfolio insurance / option replication** dynamically hedges to *synthesize* a protective put when none trades — but **it fails in gaps (1987)**, proving model/input risk is real (a caution for any delta-replication overlay). **Index-arbitrage context (ch.22):** index-future fair value = cost-of-carry − dividends vs the cash basket, and price- vs cap-weighted index construction determines the hedge ratios (cf. §6's index/cash-and-carry arb).

## 7. Event-driven, seasonality & calendar

- **PEAD / SUE (post-earnings drift).** Buy on a beat, short on a miss — momentum off a *change in expected earnings*; underreaction (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.118; 151Strategies, p.42). Chan's first independent strategy was a PEAD variant. **Concrete intraday "let the market judge" version (Chan, *AlgoTrading*, ch.6):** after a *prior-close* earnings announcement, at the open **buy if the overnight return is very positive / short if very negative** (vs a 90-day std) and **exit at the close** — you don't classify the news yourself, you trade the market's reaction. S&P 500: **APR 6.7%, Sharpe 1.5**; the *overnight* hold is negative and the drift now lasts barely a day. The same template works (weaker) for **guidance changes, analyst revisions, index-composition changes, and M&A**.
- **Merger / risk arbitrage.** Long target / short acquirer on announced deals (cash mergers pay target shareholders cash); nonstationary basis = risk arb (151Strategies, p.52; Harris, p.350).
- **News / macro-event trading.** Markets react to the **difference between expectation and actual** (expectation ≈ price just before release); most US reports at 8:30am NY. Measure event size: volatility ratio = today's TR / lagged ATR(60); >3.0 = significant shock; news impact decays ~1/T² (Kaufman, p.1228). **Key 28-year finding (bonds/crude/S&P, 1991–2017): prices REVERSE the day after a shock — the best opportunity is a 1-day trade; going long the day after a *downward* shock was the consistently winning trade** (p.1238). Warwick event-day rule: buy on close if the close is in the upper 20% of the event-day range and prices usually continue, hold a predetermined number of days, stop-loss (p.1230). Sequence: shock hits rates → currencies → equities (flight-to-safety hits equities first).
- **Aldridge's scheduled-news / directional-event method (HFTAldridge, Ch.9).** Trade the **surprise** (realized − consensus or AR-model forecast) in *repetitive scheduled* news. Build: timestamp events → compute event-window returns at multiple frequencies → regress returns on surprise (β = impact). **Higher frequency surfaces *more* persistent post-announcement opportunities.** Equities react strongly to rate decisions, weakly/state-dependently to other macro (e.g. an unemployment rise is *good* news only in expansions); REITs ≈ equities + rate-sensitive. **Market-adjust equity returns first.**
- **Event-driven volatility.** Shorting a straddle the day *after* a scheduled release (e.g. Weekly Petroleum Status Report, BLS employment, earnings), covering before the next, profits ~$13k/straddle/yr where buying it before *loses* ~$27k (spike over-anticipated, wide spreads) (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.133). Mechanics for backing out the jump component: Bennett §6.1.
- **Seasonal / calendar.** **Tax-loss / January effect** — beaten-down US small-caps rebound early next year; **largely decayed post-2002** due to crowding (Harris, p.231; Chan, p.143). **Bernstein seasonal key-dates**: enter/exit on fixed calendar dates, keep a setup only if it passes ≥4 of 5 filters (accuracy ≥75%, P/L ratio ≥4, ≤2 consecutive losses, avg profit ≥$400, positive equity growth); trigger via Stochastic %K(14,5) crossing back through 25/75 (p.25, 31). **Commodity-futures seasonals remain profitable** (real demand, not speculation): gasoline RB (buy ~Apr 13, sell ~Apr 25, every year since 1995), natural gas NG (long June ~Feb 25, exit ~Apr 15, 14 straight years) (Chan, p.148). **Momentum seasonality** (Gray & Vogel) is its own calendar edge — see §6.
- **Index-reconstitution effects.** Russell rebalances on the last June trading day using May-end caps; funds buy additions/sell deletions → 1996–2001 additions beat deletions by **+15% in June, then −5% in July** (partial reversal). Same for S&P changes; order anticipators can profit (Harris, p.487).
- **Dogs of the Dow & ranking baskets.** Buy the 10 highest-yield Dow stocks Jan 1, hold 1 yr (~10.8%/yr 1992–2011 vs S&P 9.6%); "Small Dogs" = lowest-priced 5 (Kaufman, p.1274). **Ranking-basket rule:** need ≥5 (better 10) from top & bottom, basket ≤20% of universe; if ranking is good → trend (buy best/sell worst), if bad → mean-revert; swap a name only when it moves ≥2 positions (avoid churn); match top/bottom volatility (p.1198).
- **Sentiment / COT.** Commitment of Traders (released Fri, positions as of Tue): large hedgers are the best forecasters; most bullish = hedgers heavily net long + small traders heavily net short; be wary >40% from the long-term avg (Kaufman, p.1254). Bullish Consensus >90%/<20% = overbought/oversold; put-call >1.0 bearish — but watch *deviation from average* (p.1268). News-sentiment vendor signal (Sentdex): go long when sentiment ≥ +6, close when ≤ −1 (long-only; needs shorts + sizing) (Halls-Moore, p.444). **Sentiment-change overlay** (MLDSBlueprints, ch.10): buy when the sentiment-score ratio (today/yesterday) rises >0.5 *and* close > 15-day SMA; sell on the symmetric down-move below the SMA (lot 100 shares, $100k start; thresholds/lot/capital are tunable).

## 8. Intraday & opening-range setups

Trends rarely apply on horizons < 3 days — use them only as a directional filter; short-term is mostly noise, so favor mean-reversion or wait for a pullback on directional trades (Kaufman, p.1467). Davey concurs: **entry precision matters in inverse proportion to holding time** — critical for scalps, near-irrelevant for multi-week swings; moving an idea from 1-min to daily bars almost always improves it (lower cost drag, less noise, less direct HFT competition) but yields fewer trades and weaker statistical confidence — a warning, not just a benefit (Davey2, ch.10).

- **Opening Range Breakout (ORB) / 1st-Hour Breakout.** Fix the high-low of the first hour (or N bars); buy above the high, sell below the low, reverse on the opposite cross, exit on close; add a 1×ATR(14) profit target; cancel if no signal within 2 hours. **Crabel:** a preceding *inside day* greatly raises % profitable (bonds 60→76%) — compression is a strong filter; NR4 = 4th-day range smaller than the prior 3 (Kaufman, p.1485).
- **30-Minute Breakout (30MBO, S&P/SPY/QQQ).** Setup = first 30-min bar high/low; buy when any later 30-min bar **closes** above that high (close, not intrabar), valid up to but not after the last trading hour. Stop = opposite side of the opening range; PT1 = opening-range size; at 2× range move stop to PT1; scale out; exit all MOC. Reverse once max; always flat by close. Best deployed after 3–5 consecutive losses (Bernstein, p.151).
- **Momentum Pinball (Raschke/Conners).** LBR/RSI = 3-day RSI of 1-day ROC. Buy setup: prior-day indicator < 30 → place a buy-stop above the 1st-hour high (sell-stop at the low); if profitable at close, carry overnight, exit on a gap your way. S&P 2000–2011: +$703/contract, 1,734 trades (Kaufman, p.1488).
- **Midday S/R (TSM).** Record the open→12:00 high/low. After 1pm: close in the lower 25% of the morning range → go long; upper 25% → short; flip on a break of the morning extreme. Holding until next midday gave larger gains; S&P +$80/trade longs-only (shorts lost) (Kaufman, p.1482).
- **Order-flow momentum (intraday).** Signed volume; buy when 1-min aggregated order flow > ~95th-pct threshold, exit at 0/opposite sign. ES ~$4/trade with market orders (~$10 at unrealistic midprice); **profitability hinges on market-order fills**; aggressor-tag flow >> BVC-estimated flow (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.191). **Chan's *AlgoTrading* HFT-momentum tactics (ch.7)** add the microstructure menu: **bid/ask size imbalance predicts the next tick** (a linear relationship, stronger in low-volume names); tactics include the *ratio trade* (pro-rata fills), *ticking / quote-matching*, *flipping / momentum-ignition*, *stop-hunting* (sell-stops cluster at round numbers and support), and using **signed order flow as a price predictor** — all of which only profit at the expense of slower traders.
- **VWAP (Aziz's main strategy).** Above VWAP = buyers in control, below = sellers. Long when price holds support above VWAP; short when it fails to reclaim VWAP; stop = a 5-min close on the wrong side; scale out at targets/round numbers (PlayBook S/R agrees: trade only horizontal levels, half/whole-dollar act as invisible S/R) (Aziz, p.77).

## 9. Market-making / liquidity-provision signals

> Full treatment in **`09-execution-and-transaction-costs.md`**. Microstructure theory in brief; the punchline transfers to *slow* equity systems via §6.

- **HFT is an umbrella over four families** (HFTAldridge, Ch.1, Ch.8): (1) **statistical arbitrage** (value-motivated mean-reversion of relative mispricings — see §3.2, §6); (2) **directional event/news trading** (informed, time-sensitive — §7); (3) **automated market making** (liquidity provision — this section); (4) **liquidity detection** (pinging/sniffing — mostly dark-pool, often infeasible in lit markets). The defining trait is *holding period ≤ 1 day, no overnight positions* — **not** necessarily sub-second, so the stat-arb logic transfers directly to slower equity books. **Equity/ETF stat-arb variants** that scale down to daily horizons: pairs (minimize Σ squared return diffs; trade when spread > 2σ); **dual-class shares**; **CAPM market-neutral pairs** (same β, different α — long high-α / short low-α; need |t|>~2 and Δα > costs); **Fama-French 3-factor pairs**; **liquidity arbitrage** (Pastor-Stambaugh γ); **large-to-small spillovers** (small caps lag large caps); **index/ETF arbitrage** vs basket (Law of One Price); **futures basis** and **futures→ETF lead-lag** (futures lead, now shrunk to 1–2 s). **Cointegration is the multi-asset engine** (Ch.8): regress P1 on P2 (+ extra instruments/lags, VAR form); trade when the residual is stationary (≥90% within 2σ ⇒ mean-reverting).
- **Sequential-trade microstructure (Glosten-Milgrom, Hasbrouck).** A competitive zero-expected-profit dealer quotes the value conditional on trade direction: `A = E[V | Buy]`, `B = E[V | Sell]`. With value `V ∈ {V̄_low, V̄_high}`, informed fraction `μ`, prior `δ=½`: the **spread `A − B = (V̄_high − V̄_low)·μ` scales directly with information asymmetry μ** (Hasbrouck, p.24–25). After each trade the dealer Bayes-updates belief δ_k via the event tree → trade prices are a **martingale**; order flow is asymmetric and **positively autocorrelated** (informed all trade one way); each trade has a **price impact**; the **spread narrows over time** as the dealer learns the buy/sell proportion (p.26–27). Dealer losses to the informed are recovered from the uninformed.
- **Strategic-trade microstructure (Kyle 1985).** Value `v ~ N(p₀, Σ₀)`; informed demand `x`; noise flow `u ~ N(0, σ_u²)`; the MM sees total `y = x + u` and sets one clearing price via a linear rule `p = μ + λ·y`. Informed optimum **`x = (v − μ)/(2λ)`** (trade proportional to mispricing, throttled by λ); solving the fixed point gives **`λ = ½·√(Σ₀/σ_u²)`** with demand intensity `β = √(σ_u²/Σ₀)`. Informed profit rises in `(v−p₀)²` and in `σ_u²` ("noise = camouflage"); **exactly half the private information is impounded** (`Var(v|p)=Σ₀/2`). The multiperiod version has net order flow serially *uncorrelated* per clearing (contrast sequential-trade) (Hasbrouck, p.33–38, 114). **Both families agree on the operative signal:** *a net buy order raises price, in expectation permanently* — which is why trade/price VARs are good asymmetry characterizations (p.115; cross-ref T10).
- **Order-flow has long memory but prices stay efficient (Bouchaud et al.).** Order-**sign** autocorrelation `C(ℓ) ∼ c∞·ℓ^{−γ}` with **γ ≈ 0.5 < 1** (non-integrable → long memory): a buy now makes a buy ~10,000 trades later (hours/days) more likely by >0.5% (TQP, p.187–188). The cause is **metaorder splitting, not herding** — broker-ID studies show same-broker resubmission dominates while *other* brokers are contrarian at large lags; power-law metaorder sizes `L^{−1−ζ}` give `γ = ζ−1`, and empirical γ≈½ ⇒ ζ≈3/2 (p.196–197). **Why split?** Liquidity rationing — buying 1% of cap when 0.5%/day trades and 2% daily vol → ~6% slippage over 8 days (≈⅓ of a 20% edge); large MOs walk the book, large LOs *signal* intent (Flash Crash 2010: a 75k E-mini sell order → −9%), so execution slices via **VWAP / TWAP / Almgren–Chriss** schedules — which *produces* the autocorrelated flow (p.199–201). **The efficiency paradox & resolution:** permanent impact + long-memory signs would make prices super-diffusive (trends) `V(ℓ) ∼ ℓ^{2−γ}`, contradicting flat signature plots; replacing constant impact with a *decaying propagator* `G(ℓ) ∼ Γ∞·ℓ^{−β}` with critical `β = (1−γ)/2 ≈ 0.25` enforces diffusive prices — **liquidity providers counter-balance the predictable flow so signs are predictable but returns are not** (p.192–193, 250–257). **Signal implication: the slow decay of *others'* impact is the only prediction component of your own order flow** — order-flow autocorrelation is exploitable *only to the extent it isn't already priced* (p.201).
- **Self-exciting activity (Hawkes, TQP Ch.9).** Intensity depends on own history; clustering ratio `r(τ)=V[ΔN]/E[ΔN]` (1 = Poisson, >1 = clustering); branching ratio `g` = expected children per event, mean family size `1/(1−g)`. Markets sit **near-critical** (`g_eff → 1` as a power law), implying ~80% of activity is *endogenously* generated — relevant for sizing event-reaction signals (p.165–175).
- **Profitability of market-making (TQP Ch.4).** Long-run P&L balances spread vs long-term impact: `E[G_T] ≈ T·υ₀·E[θ]·(E[s]/2 + ϖ − R∞)`; with `R∞=s/2` the infinitesimal MM breaks even. The sign of the edge hinges on impact decay vs flow memory: **if β > (1−γ)/2 prices mean-revert → market-making is *easy*; if β < (1−γ)/2 prices trend → market-making is *hard*** (p.319–325). Because modern competition pushes spreads to break-even, **liquidity provision is only marginally profitable and providers are hyper-sensitive to risk** (p.417) — the same conclusion Harris reaches below. This is *the* link to slow systems: a mean-reversion sleeve is a (slow) liquidity-provision bet that is easy when the market reverts and a loser when it trends.
- **Value traders are the ultimate liquidity suppliers** — they profit only when uninformed demand pushes price from fundamental value, then trade against it (mean-reverting footprint). The **outside spread** is much wider than the dealer spread (larger size, longer holds, research/financing; a value trader earns at best half the spread vs a dealer's full round-trip) (Harris, p.338–345). **Winner's curse:** bid lower against more competitors and harder-to-value names; act only on **large** mispricings ("if you're not a buyer you need not be a seller").
- **Order-book / tape signals.** **Unusual Hold on the Bid (UHOB)** — a buyer repeatedly takes prints at *one* price and won't drop the bid → foreshadows a large hidden order; step in front, stop just below the buyer; edge is much higher when the UHOB sits *above* an important level (PlayBook, p.116). Technical-sentiment inputs (put/call ratios, IV skew, limit-order-book shape, volume/turnover) are best used as *conditioning* variables, usually with a *contrarian* reading (Narang, p.34).
- **Cash Flow / market-making around a core.** While holding a core position, repeatedly make the spread in a range (bid front of support, offer front of resistance) until the range breaks (PlayBook, p.128).
- **Detection of predatory flow.** Specialist/HFT edges (quote-matching, cream-skimming, gunning stops) depend on *seeing order flow first*; "gunning a stop" pushes price to a stop trigger then trades against it — manipulators thrive in thin/illiquid conditions and around focal news (Harris, p.501–504).

## 10. Combining signals & filters

- **Use ≥2 indicators with distinct roles** — one to confirm trend direction, one to trigger entry — so the signal fires *before* the move. Example: long if 5-RSI < 30 **AND** price > 200-EMA **AND** 1-min MACD crosses up (Davey/light, p.8).
- **Stack mean-reversion signals with AND, not OR.** Combining Strat 2 AND 3 AND 4 improves performance; either/or combinations do not — but watch for too few trades (Davey, p.180). Davey2 adds the *single-condition discipline*: keep entries to 1–2 optimizable parameters, start with one condition, add another only if it *significantly* helps; exits are the neglected, often-more-impactful half — if a far stop wrecks results the *entry* is the real problem, breakeven stops tend to cap profits, and trailing stops are easy to over-parameterize (Davey2, ch.10).
- **Continuous forecast combination (Carver).** Rather than AND-gating binary rules, scale each rule to a common risk-adjusted forecast (mean abs ≈ 10), then *weight and sum* multiple rules (across types and speeds) into one combined forecast that sets position size — a smoother, diversification-aware alternative to discrete filters (Carver, *Systematic Trading*, ch.7; see §2.5). EWMAC + carry alone ≈ 85% of the full system.
- **Divergent/convergent bucketing (Carver, *Advanced Futures*).** The scaled-forecast framework extended into a *portfolio of rule types*: split the combined forecast **60% divergent** (trend/EWMAC + breakout + acceleration, all positive-skew, §2.8) and **40% convergent** (carry + value + skew + cross-sectional momentum/carry + fast mean-reversion, mostly negative-skew, §6), so the book holds *both* skews by construction. A repeated, counter-intuitive lesson: **a standalone *loser* with low/negative correlation still improves the whole** — value (standalone −0.06 SR) at 5% lifts trend+carry 1.27→1.29; skew at 20% lifts it 1.27→1.38 ("financial alchemy"). Conversely, a 0.97-correlated rule (normalised trend, breakout) earns only "cheap extra diversification," not a place of its own. **Add rules for their *marginal correlation*, not their standalone Sharpe** — and run vicious-negative-skew convergent rules (fast MR) in a *separate, limit-order sleeve* that can't be forecast-combined (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, throughout).
- **Signal filters / regime gates.** On/off switches: take longs only if price > 200-day MA (Kaufman; Kaabar, p.324); Clenow's 50/100-MA filter (§2.6) and the LightScalp MA-as-trend-gate (§3) are the same idea. **HMM regime overlay** as a "risk manager" that vetoes/closes trend signals in high-volatility (risk-off) regimes — lifts an SMA(10/30) SPY system from ~Sharpe 0.37 to 0.48 and maxDD from ~56% to ~24% (Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algo*, p.206, 464). **S&P–bond correlation timing** (Landazabal): buy SPY when 20-day SPY-HYG/JNK correlations drop *and* SPY's 5-day return lags the HY ETFs by ≥0.5% (Kaufman, p.1184).
- **Forecast thresholds.** Act only when |prediction| ≥ a threshold (fixed, volatility-based rolling std, or training-set quantile e.g. 75%) → 3 states (bullish/bearish/neutral), fewer trades, lower costs (Kaabar, p.254; Guo et al. logistic-net rule trades only when up/down probability exceeds q, p.41). Ilmanen's commodity-trend menu adds **no-trade bands + stop-loss/take-profit exits** to the same end (Ch.14.2).
- **Counter-balancing setups.** Pair a with-trend system with a counter-trend/failed-breakout setup (a breakout trader *must* know failed-breakout patterns) so the book performs across stages (Grimes, p.76); Clenow's "most effective tweak" is exactly a tight-stop mean-reversion sleeve bolted onto the trend core (§2.6), and Carver deliberately runs ~⅓ negative-skew rules and gates his fast-mean-reversion rule with an opposite-sign trend filter *in place of* a stop-loss (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, §6). Chan makes the same case at the *strategy* level: hold a **momentum** book (positive skew, thrives on black swans) **and** a **mean-reversion** book (high Sharpe, negative skew) so each covers the other's failure mode (Chan, *AlgoTrading*, ch.2 vs ch.6). Game-theory framing: a trend trader earns most buying an uptrend, mean-reversion does best at extremes; net results are similar — which informs *when* to prefer each style (Kaufman, p.1732).
- **Diversify the knobs, not the decimals.** The strongest, most repeated lesson across the new trend sources: vary *concepts* (universe, timeframe, stop distance, rule family), not parameter decimals. Clenow's single best diversifier is a broader **universe** (then multi-ATR-stop, then counter-trend sleeve, then spread contracts) (ch.8); Carver runs 6 EWMAC speeds + carry; Ilmanen diversifies across assets *and* window lengths/frequencies and **vol-weights** signals (adds SR ~0.1–0.3); Tomasini prefers many *single-market* systems mixed into a portfolio; Davey2 mixes one-size-fits-all robustness against single-market customizability and warns that cherry-picking the best markets is hidden optimization (Clenow ch.8; Carver ch.7; Ilmanen Ch.14.2; Tomasini; Davey2 ch.10).
- **Marginal, not standalone, value.** When combining positions, value each by its **increment to the whole portfolio** `v(holdings ∪ {g}) − v(holdings)` so complements/substitutes are priced correctly (Wellman et al., p.38). **Closed-loop beats open-loop** when prices are volatile; a backtested edge can depend on the rest of the market's behavior, not just your logic (open-loop "parasites" free-ride on adaptive agents) (p.25).
- **Strategy = policy + parameter-optimization + backtesting** (MLDSBlueprints, ch.9) — and an RL agent collapses all three into one trained policy. The classical pipeline (Halls-Moore): source ideas continuously (textbooks, epchan/Quantpedia/Quantocracy, arXiv q-fin/SSRN) and **reject most** with fixed criteria — methodology, Sharpe, leverage, frequency, win/loss profile, max DD, capacity, # parameters (fewer = less curve-fit), benchmark; **judge raw returns LAST** (*Successful Algo*, p.30–35). Refine failed public strategies via economically-motivated levers (exclude problem stocks, shift entry/exit timing, change the universe) — *not* trial-and-error (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.65).

## To validate empirically
- **Signal decay.** Factor excess returns drop ~25% from discovery to publication and >50% after, from crowding (Jansen, p.80; Ilmanen Ch.16.1: success breeds return-chasing inflows → overcrowding → worse selloffs, and currency carry's "alpha morphed into beta"); confirm momentum, January effect, and pairs still pay *after costs* on your universe and dates — several here (GLD-GDX post-2011, ARNC-UNG, January effect post-2002, pre-2008 carry crowding) decayed or were never cointegrated.
- **Regime dependence.** Trend-following only profits when markets trend; ARIMA+GARCH gains were concentrated in 1970–80 and crashes; market-making is "easy when prices mean-revert, hard when they trend" (TQP β-vs-(1−γ)/2). Quantify each edge's performance conditional on a volatility/trend regime (HMM or ADX) before trusting the blended Sharpe.
- **Fill assumptions.** Many headline results (AR/ARMA reversion, cross-sectional IV, order-flow momentum, options-overwriting return/vol estimates) *require midprice or instantaneous fills* and collapse with realistic market-order slippage and option bid/ask — re-run with conservative fills and the actual spread.
- **Lookahead in pairs fitting.** Hedge ratios fit on the same sample as the backtest (ARNC-UNG) inject lookahead bias; re-estimate online (Kalman) or strictly walk-forward and confirm the spread is genuinely stationary via ADF/Johansen (Tsay workflow), not assumed.
- **Over-fitting / parameter sensitivity.** Clenow and Tomasini insist the *concept* must be robust to parameter changes (vary concepts, not decimals); re-test each trend/breakout rule across the sanctioned parameter neighborhoods (breakout 25/50/100; MA 5/50, 10/100, 20/200; stop 1.5/3/6 ATR) and reject anything that only works at one setting. Carver's mean-abs-forecast-10 scaling should reproduce ≈85% of trend-system performance from EWMAC+carry alone — if it doesn't, suspect over-fit add-ons.
- **Concentration of P&L.** Several "good" backtests earned almost everything in one month or one event (ARNC-UNG Jan 2015; sentiment baskets in 2013) — and trend systems by design earn from a *few outsized winners* (Clenow ~42% win rate). Inspect the equity curve for dependence on a handful of days/names before sizing up, and confirm the diversified universe (not one market) carried the few big winners.
- **Tail/adverse-selection cost of liquidity provision.** Mean-reversion, pairs, carry, and short-vol (call overwriting, dispersion, VIX-future carry) carry many small wins then occasional severe losses clustered in flight-to-quality crashes (Ilmanen Oct '87/'98/'08); the *timing* of the loss (coincidence with a high market price of risk) matters more than the skew magnitude. Stress-test the worst leg failing to revert / a liquidity crisis preventing close-out / a crash overwhelming the variance premium, and size for that tail.
