# 07 — Backtesting & Validation
*Trading System Knowledge Base · theme T07 · equities/ETF-weighted*
*Updated: includes López de Prado (AFML), Aronson, Pardo, Davey, Tomasini, Carver.*
*Updated (pass 3): added Coqueret & Guida (ML-backtest protocol), Chan, Quantitative Value, Clenow, Carver.*

**What this covers:** The full research-to-live workflow, the biases that make backtests lie, the validation methods that defend against them (walk-forward, purged/embargoed CV, Monte Carlo, sensitivity), the performance metrics and significance tests that decide whether an edge is real, and a practical pre-trust checklist.
**Why it matters:** A backtest is an *idealised upper bound* on live performance (Halls-Moore, *Successful Algo*, p.16). Almost every documented gap between backtest and live trading traces to one of a small set of biases — overfitting, costs, look-ahead, survivorship, regime change. Getting validation right is the difference between a strategy that compounds and one that bleeds capital while confirming a delusion. The deepest statistical risk is *selection across multiple trials*: most discoveries in finance are false, and a backtest reported without its trial count cannot be evaluated at all ("Marcos' Third Law of Backtesting"; López de Prado, *AFML*, Ch 14.7.3).
**Primary sources:** Halls-Moore (*Successful Algo*, *Advanced Algo*); Jansen, *ML for Algo Trading*; Chan (*Quantitative Trading*, *Machine Trading*); Hilpisch; Kaufman; Narang (*Inside the Black Box*); Grimes; Kakushadze & Serur (*151 Strategies*); Davey/Aziz; Harris; Kaabar; Lachowicz; Leshik. **Added:** López de Prado (*Advances in Financial Machine Learning*; *ML for Asset Managers*); Aronson (*Evidence-Based Technical Analysis*); Pardo (*The Evaluation and Optimization of Trading Strategies*); Davey (*Building Winning Algorithmic Trading Systems*); Tomasini & Jaekle (*Trading Systems*); Carver (*Systematic Trading*); Chincarini & Kim (*QEPM*); Grinold & Kahn (*Active Portfolio Management*); Aldridge (*High-Frequency Trading*); Tortoriello (*Quantitative Strategies for Achieving Alpha*); Gray & Vogel (*Quantitative Momentum*); Covel (*Trend Following*); Ilmanen (*Expected Returns*); Hasbrouck (*Empirical Market Microstructure*). **Added (pass 3):** Coqueret & Guida (*Machine Learning for Factor Investing*); Chan (*Algorithmic Trading: Winning Strategies and Their Rationale*); Gray & Carlisle (*Quantitative Value*); Clenow (*Stocks on the Move*); Carver (*Advanced Futures Trading Strategies*).

## 1. The research workflow (idea → live)

The discipline is **scientific method under non-stationarity**: observe → theorise → deduce testable consequences → *try to disprove* (Popper). Because markets are non-stationary, research is *continuous*, not one-and-done as in physics (Narang, p.147–149). Idea sources: market observation, academic literature (Fama-French, Markowitz), researcher migration, and lessons from discretionary traders (Narang, p.149–151).

### From: Tomasini, Davey2
The same Popperian / **hypothetico-deductive** loop is the explicit backbone of trading-system development: observe → hypothesise a relationship → predict → **verify on new data** → conclude with confidence intervals/tests (Tomasini, p.7–8). A trading system *is* "objective TA" — repeatable, unambiguous, back-testable (Tomasini, p.7). Davey frames the whole pipeline as a series of **gates**, each of which a candidate must *pass to advance* — and if it fails a gate you **scrap it, you do not tweak-and-retest** (which silently re-optimizes and burns the data toward a curve fit): SMART goals + wish-list → trading idea (entry/exit/market/bar/data) → *limited* testing on a small data slice → in-depth walk-forward → Monte Carlo → incubation → live, starting tiny (Davey2, ch. 8–14). Davey needed **~100–200 ideas to find one tradable system** (Davey2, ch. 8, Conclusion).

**Canonical pipeline:**

1. **Hypothesis with a sound premise.** Prefer theory-justified strategies over data-mining (Jansen, p.357). Define expectations *first* — interval, relative risk, % profitable (trend ≈ 30–40%, mean-reversion/arbitrage ≈ 75–80%) — so the backtest *confirms or refutes a concept* rather than discovering one (Kaufman, p.1802, 1867). "Testing is for VALIDATION, not discovery"; "the computer validates a belief, it must not create one" (Kaufman, p.870). **Gather theoretical evidence a strategy *should* exist before you backtest it** — precision (the chance a "significant" result is real) depends on the *prior odds*, so an economic story is not optional decoration but a direct input to the false-discovery rate (López de Prado, *ML for Asset Managers*, p.105–106; Grinold & Kahn's "intuition" guardrail, p.335–339).
2. **In-sample build / fit.** Fit optimal parameters on a training subset; choose sample breadth and length deliberately — more data = robustness but risk of explaining only the past (Narang, p.151–153). Optimise *in-sample only* (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.53). QEPM frames this as **three data segments**: *in-sample* (T0→T1), *out-of-sample* (T1→T2), optional *real-time/forward* (T2→T3); do **all** sequential factor-add/drop experimentation **only in-sample**, then lock the model — re-running on the same data after an out-of-sample failure *is* data mining, and out-of-sample testing preserves degrees of freedom so t-stats read normally (Chincarini & Kim, *QEPM*, Ch 16.3).
3. **Out-of-sample / walk-forward.** The real test is unseen data. Train on early data, deploy on later (Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algo*: AREX trained ≤2012 traded 2013–14; HMM trained ≤2004 used 2005–14, p.419, 465). Always do OOS testing — "testing is *more* than optimizing; an optimized backtest alone proves nothing" (Davey, p.30, 179). Davey ranks the **four ways results are produced**: plain historical optimization (*worthless* — by definition it looks great), single out-of-sample (10–20% held out, "a magnitude better"), **walk-forward (recommended default)**, real-time — and warns "the better a system tests historically, the less likely it performs that well live" (Davey2, ch. 5).
4. **Incubation / paper trade.** Paper trading is the ultimate out-of-sample test; the gap between a strategy's publication date and your test is genuine OOS (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.55). Always backtest *and* paper-trade before risking real money (Davey/Light, p.10). Incubation is a discrete pipeline gate in its own right (Davey2, ch. 8–14).
5. **Live, monitored, scaled.** No "set-and-forget" — monitor and adjust (Davey, p.10). Mitigate residual overfit with deflated Sharpe, staged paper-trading, and closely-monitored live execution *before* scaling (Jansen, p.1011–1012). **Schedule retraining/re-optimization in the methodology itself** so cadence is pre-committed rather than decided ad-hoc inside a drawdown (López de Prado emphasis; QuantAssetMgmt ch.14).

**Re-backtest everything yourself**, even published strategies with full results — it confirms you understood it, catches the author's errors, and lets you experiment (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.31).

**Iterate *less*, not more.** Fewer iterations → worse-looking backtest but *better* live results; more iterations → prettier backtest, worse future (Davey, p.21, 31). Optimal-stopping heuristic (secretary problem): examine ~1/e ≈ 37% of candidates, then take the first that beats them all (Jansen, p.358).

### From: Carver — be pessimistic about every backtest
Carver bans the "time machine": **never use in-sample fitting**; prefer **expanding (anchored) out-of-sample** windows, optionally rolling/walk-forward (e.g. a 5-yr window) once you have enough history (Carver, *Systematic Trading*, ch.3). Crucially, *do not select rules on performance* — instead pick a *few* ideas/variations on **behaviour** (turnover, correlation), drop any pair with >0.95 correlation or excessive cost, then let **forecast weights** down-weight (not exclude) the poor rules; this keeps the backtest honest because nothing was chosen by looking at its returns (Carver, *Systematic Trading*, ch.3, ch.7). Apply explicit **"pessimism factors"** — the fraction of a backtest's Sharpe you can realistically expect live (Carver, Table 14): single-period in-sample **25%**, single-period OOS **75%**, bootstrap in-sample 60%, bootstrap OOS 75%, handcraft in-sample 65–70% — assuming ~25% of past returns came from unrepeatable secular trends. Treat the *maximum* realistic SR as ≈ **1.0** for a diversified systematic system, **0.5** semi-automatic, **0.4** for an asset allocator (Carver, *Systematic Trading*, ch.4, Epilogue). Echoed by Ilmanen: reported returns are upward-biased (survivorship, backfill, voluntary reporting, overfit, understated costs), and falling discount rates inflated in-sample realized returns (e.g. the 1990s), so even multi-decade samples can be biased — mitigate with OOS/CV, economic priors, subperiod robustness, simple specs, and **real-time (non-hindsighted) coefficients** (Ilmanen, *Expected Returns*, Ch 4.6, 7.1–7.2, 8.6).

**Theory-first, low-parameter, backward-looking everything (Carver, *Advanced Futures Trading Strategies*).** Carver doubles down: build strategies from a *prior theory* of why an edge exists, keep the parameter count minimal, and estimate **everything on rolling backward-looking data** — forecast scalars, the instrument diversification multiplier (IDM), instrument/forecast weights, and volatility are all computed on data available *at the time* so nothing is fit in-sample; the fixed scalars/tables he publishes are stable enough to be used as live constants (*Advanced Futures*, p.183, p.124). Judge a strategy on the **alpha that survives a long-only-benchmark regression**, not raw return: regress the strategy's monthly returns on the long-only benchmark to strip the historical long-bias tailwind, and take the **intercept** as genuine added value (Strategy 6 alpha ≈ 1.13%/mo ≈ 13%/yr, t ≈ 9.6; S7 ≈ 1.17%/mo; *faster* trend beats slower on alpha) (*Advanced Futures*, p.176–177, p.199). **Positive skew is a feature of trend** (ride winners, cut losers) but only *appears* at a frequency matching the holding period — fast filters show daily/weekly positive skew, slow filters only at monthly/annual — and faster trends earn better skew at the cost of higher turnover (*Advanced Futures*, p.207–209). The **"trend-following diversification machine"**: long/short trend can make the *average instrument* look worse than long-only yet make the *aggregate portfolio* better, because long/short sub-strategies are less correlated (IDM rises 2.46 long-only → 2.89 long/short) — so judge the **portfolio**, not the median instrument (*Advanced Futures*, p.173–175). His net-of-cost, after-buffering Sharpe/turnover profile for the "Jumbo" aggregate sets realistic expectations (e.g. S4 vol-targeted buy-&-hold SR 0.85 / turnover 20.7; S6 slow long/short SR 1.01 / 31.6; S8 fast+forecast+buffer SR 1.06 / 60.5; S9 multi-speed trend SR 1.14 / 62.9 — yet median-instrument SRs are only ~0.18–0.32), and the very fast filters (EWMAC2/4) are **net money-losers on costly instruments**, so dropping EWMAC2 (keep EWMAC4+) live is defensible (*Advanced Futures*, p.156, 170, 173, 187, 197–198, 207–208, 226).

### From: Coqueret & Guida — the ML-factor backtesting protocol
Coqueret & Guida (*Machine Learning for Factor Investing*) treat the entire research pipeline as **one integrated, non-sequential problem**: data → features → model `f` → error structure `ε` → portfolio weights are all intertwined, and the *framing* (which predictors, which label horizon, which loss function) dominates the choice of algorithm — don't blindly minimize squared error, because alternative losses can be wiser (ch.2.2). On a **firm-characteristic panel** the validation crux is **persistence/horizon matching**: if feature autocorrelation and label autocorrelation are mismatched, forecasts come out biased and noisy, so the label horizon and the predictors' decay must be chosen together (ch.4.5.5, 4.6). A train/test split is the *minimum*; build **balanced categorical labels relative to the cross-sectional median (not zero)** so class balance survives across market regimes (ch.1.2, 4.5.1). The dedicated cross-validation, variance–bias, and hyperparameter-search machinery (their ch.10) and the data-snooping treatment (ch.12) are detailed in §3 and §4a below — the headline is that **plain k-fold CV is proscribed on a time-stamped panel** (it trains on the future and tests on the past), and you instead use either De Prado-style CV with *no informational overlap* (valid only if you assume the cross-section is time-stationary) or **walk-forward retraining at each rebalance** (ch.10.4). This reinforces that **ML enlarges the overfitting surface** (more knobs, more trials) rather than shrinking it — *cross-ref 05* for the model-risk / bias-variance side.

## 2. Backtest biases (and how to avoid each)

Halls-Moore frames four bias families that **inflate** apparent performance (*Successful Algo*, p.16–18); the fuller list:

**Look-ahead bias** — using data not available at decision time. Sources: off-by-one index bugs; computing regression coefficients / hedge ratios over the *full* sample (incl. future); using a period's High/Low/Close *during* that period (Halls-Moore, *Successful Algo*, p.16–17). Classic tells: "buy within 1% of the day's low"; fitting a cointegration hedge ratio on the same sample you trade (Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algo*, p.383). Chan stresses the same trap as his **first MR pitfall**: using same-day high/low (or any future info) for entry inflates mean-reversion backtests, and is eliminated *structurally* only if the backtest and the live system are the **same program fed different data** (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1). Aronson calls this "leakage of future information": rules built on **close-of-day** data must assume execution at the *next day's open*, and lagged/revised inputs (e.g. mutual-fund cash data) must be lagged to true availability — assuming a fill at a price not yet known when the signal formed is the canonical error (Aronson, p.29–30). Coqueret & Guida formalize the panel version as the **"red zone"**: with holding Δh, training size Δs, and label horizon Δl, the training window must span Δs+Δl and **stop at t−Δl** — *any* feature dated in (t−Δl, t] leaks the future, and this is "the most common backtest bug" (Coqueret & Guida, ch.12.1, 12.4.1). *Fixes:* lag all signal inputs to the prior period's close; a streaming handler must `stream_next(stream_date=…)` so it never peeks at future rows (Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algo*, p.451); **truncate the last N days and confirm positions over the overlap are identical** (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.51–52, 59).

**Survivorship bias** — testing only on assets that survived. Inflates "buy cheap"/value strategies badly: a toy example shows −42% real vs +388% survivor-only (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.24, 41–42; the same "buy the 10 cheapest, hold a year → 388%" backtest is the canonical trap in Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1). The directionality matters: missing delisted stocks is **most dangerous to long-only mean-reversion** (inflates it) but actually *deflates* short-only and momentum backtests (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1). Yahoo data is *not* survivorship-bias-free (Halls-Moore, p.17). A test using only stocks alive today biases results **upward**; include delisted/merged/bankrupt names (CRSP supplies estimated terminal returns) and apply universe criteria *as of each date* (Chincarini & Kim, *QEPM*, Ch 16.5). **Equity-universe handling (Clenow).** A stock is tradable on a given day **only if it was in the index that day**: test against the *historical* S&P 500 constituent list, and if a name leaves the index, sell it — and you **must include delisted/bankrupt stocks** (most had terrible performance), because omitting them gives "overly optimistic results" (Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*, ch.3). **Quantitative Value** does this with CRSP (which includes "dead"/delisted stocks) and *merges in delisting returns* via Beaver–McNichols–Price — omitting delisting returns "massively overstates" value-portfolio returns (Gray & Carlisle, *Quantitative Value*, ch.10). *Fixes:* use delisted-inclusive ("point-in-time") datasets; track the **historical index composition** (when names were added/removed), not just delisted prices (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.24); or start building your own bias-free set going forward (3–4 yrs). Intraday strategies tolerate biased data better (Chan, p.40; *151 Strategies* notes survivorship "not detrimental for intraday," p.125). Study the whole pre-selection group, not the survivors (Grimes, p.189).

**Data-snooping / multiple-testing / data-mining bias** — reusing one dataset repeatedly for selection (White 2000), or running large pair/strategy searches that inflate false positives (Hilpisch, p.111–113; Jansen, p.439–443). This is **the biggest statistical risk** (Jansen, p.357). Chan's framing: too many free parameters fit to noise — his antidotes are **simplicity/linearity** (a Taylor-linear approximation usually exists for any nonlinear model, so prefer the linear one), a **high Sharpe with short max-drawdown-*duration*** (which "auto-passes" cross-validation), cross-validation, and ultimately a **walk-forward test** (paper or small real money); expect **live Sharpe ≈ half of backtest** (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1). Coqueret & Guida put it starkly: iterating strategy/parameter/engine on **one** dataset *guarantees* you eventually find something that "works," so treat **p-hacking as the default failure mode** (Coqueret & Guida, ch.12.4.2). *Quantitative Value* makes the point with Leinweber's joke — Bangladeshi butter production "explained" 75% of the S&P 500 (then adding sheep + US cheese → 99%), a fully spurious fit — and warns you should **expect ~1 in 5 random strategies to look significant**, with the narrative fallacy making plausible spurious results hard to reject (Gray & Carlisle, *Quantitative Value*, ch.10). *Fixes:* report the **number of trials**; control familywise error; minimise the number of experiments (Jansen, p.556). "50 of 1,000 strategies profit by chance" — so 'statistically significant' often isn't (Kaufman, p.1859). *This bias now has its own full treatment — see §4a "The data-mining / multiple-testing problem" below, which is the single most important addition to this file.*

**Overfitting / curve-fitting** — fitting noise, not signal (Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algo*, p.275–281). The *cardinal sin* (Narang, p.162). Causes: too many factors, too many conditionalities, too-infrequent trading (few signals = no significance). Markets are extremely noisy (terabytes/day, tiny signal), so anything explaining the past *too well* is suspect. *Cure = parsimony* — Occam's razor, "as simple as possible but not simpler" (Narang, p.162–167). "More optimizing is almost always BAD"; auto-generating "strategy builders" usually produce junk that fails live; more parameters = more curve-fitting (Davey, p.19–20, 30, 131). Best historical results often come *from* overfitting and are a poor choice for live trading (Kaufman, p.542). **Two distinct kinds of overfitting** (López de Prado, *ML for Asset Managers*, p.6): *train-set* overfitting (the model fits noise) is detectable and curable with cross-validation + Monte Carlo, regularization (LASSO, early stopping, drop-out), and ensembles (more/shallower trees); *test-set* overfitting (selection bias from repeated trials) is what backtests **cannot** defend against, and is handled only with the deflated Sharpe ratio / FWER, thousands of CPCV splits, and Monte Carlo on synthetic data. **Polynomial analogy** (Tomasini, p.136–145): a degree-9 polynomial fits 10 points perfectly but predicts nothing; only the simplest (degree-0) generalises — every added rule adapts to *noise*, not signal. **Detectors:** daily-return **kurtosis > 7–8 ⇒ probably overfit** (too many same-size winners; >6 per Kaufman p.1859); spiky/jumpy parameter surfaces; in-sample CAGR high but OOS negative (Chan, *Machine Trading*: recurring across a 112-factor model, an all-leaves tree, a multi-neuron NN, p.1); a worked Tomasini case — a 6-parameter Bollinger system optimised on 2002–2006 made **$78k in-sample but only $379 OOS** (p.115–126). **Skip the backtest entirely for obviously over-fit shapes (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1):** high APR with low Sharpe and a long drawdown; a long-only system that "beats" buy-and-hold only on Sharpe but *not* on information ratio; and **100-node neural nets posting Sharpe 6** — these are textbook curve-fits not worth testing. **Overfitting defenses are model-specific (Coqueret & Guida, ch.10.2.4):** regressions — drop correlated features (flag >70% abs. correlation) and penalize (LASSO/ridge/elastic-net); trees — cap depth (≤6 ⇒ ≤2^d leaves), set a complexity parameter `cp`, set a minimum instances-per-leaf; random forests — ≤1000 trees, tune subsample and feature counts; boosted trees — **sync learning rate η with #trees** (both low ⇒ learns nothing, both high ⇒ overfit), penalize scores/leaves, add monotonicity constraints; NNs — η, weight/bias-norm penalties, dropout, sign constraints. The **variance–bias tradeoff** is the lens: avg squared error = model variance + squared bias + irreducible error, and ridge is the clean illustration (one λ slides the cursor: ↑λ ⇒ ↓variance, ↑bias) — empirically on their factor panel *simpler models often win* because the features carry little signal (Coqueret & Guida, ch.10.2.1–2). (*Cross-ref 05* — ML adds overfitting surface, not less of it.)

**Ignoring transaction costs & slippage** — the most common way a profitable-looking backtest dies. A Bollinger-band ES mean-reversion that is **Sharpe 3 gross flips to −3 after just 1 bp** of cost (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.23). Without costs, optimizers favour high-frequency junk; small-bar strategies die on costs; "anyone showing cost-free equity curves is misleading you" (Davey, p.28–29). Tomasini's LUXOR example shows **$30/round-turn slippage+commission cut the average trade from $35 to $5**, exposing that raw net profit is meaningless (p.43–47). *Fixes:* model commission + half-spread + market impact + slippage; **deduct costs only when weights change** (Chan, p.60–65). Slippage ≈ bid-ask (S&P ~1 tick; energy 20–30 bp ≈ $100). Do *not* pad costs "to be safe" — that kills profitable fast systems; instead require avg profit/trade to dominate costs (a $50/trade euro edge = 4 bp, too small) (Kaufman, p.1857). Aronson's nuance: **include costs when testing a stand-alone strategy** (frequent reversers incur more commission + slippage), but **omit them when the goal is to detect *raw predictive information***, since costs can mask the edge of a frequently-reversing rule (Aronson, p.31). Also assume realistic **short availability** — hard-to-borrow names make the best short picks un-executable → phantom alpha (Narang, p.169–170); both **hard-to-borrow constraints and the uptick / alternative-uptick circuit breaker** can fabricate "profitable" shorts that you could never have put on (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1). Chan is explicit that his **own book examples omit transaction costs and reuse data for fitting and testing**, and are therefore flagged "**prototype strategies**," not tradeable as-is — a useful caution that even a clean-looking published backtest may be a prototype (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1). Concrete institutional cost stacks for sims: QEPM used Abel Noser data (~43 bps/trade in Q4 2020, modeled 80% large-cap + 20% small-cap, plus **taxes** — what matters is after-tax return) (Ch 16.8); *Trend Following* (Covel) budgeted **~$20/contract round-trip + slippage, plus 1.5% mgmt + 0.5% fixed + 15% performance fee**; *Quantitative Momentum* (Gray & Vogel) used **1% mgmt fee + 0.20%/rebalance ×4 = 0.80% trading → 1.80%/yr**, arguing momentum "can easily survive trading costs" (capacity is finite). *Quantitative Value* models capacity directly — Graham's net-nets returned ~30%/yr but only on tiny illiquid stocks, so a **40th-percentile NYSE market-cap floor** keeps the backtest investable (Gray & Carlisle, *Quantitative Value*, ch.10). Coqueret & Guida give the ML-factor cost bar: a hit ratio is only meaningful **net of costs**, so benchmark it at **≥52% after costs** (ch.6.1.4).

**Point-in-time / data-quality bias** — sync restated financials & EPS to prices by *actual release date* (Jansen, p.353–356; QEPM: vendors often stamp EPS to the *quarter earned*, not the report date — shift every item to the month it became public, lagging fundamentals 3 months, CPI 1 month, etc.; also error-check for glaring ratio mistakes like an E/P of 0.000002, Ch 16.5). **Quantitative Value** quantifies the penalty: lag fundamentals **6 months**, and beware restated financials — *failing to use as-first-reported data overstated a P/E strategy by 28% (1987–2001)* (Gray & Carlisle, *Quantitative Value*, ch.10). Ensure split- *and* dividend-adjusted data (adjust by a multiplier so daily returns are preserved; un-adjusted data triggers false ex-date signals). **Use total-return data (Clenow):** adjust for splits *and cash dividends* (assume dividends reinvested) — price-only series make every ex-dividend look like a loss and push dividend-payers down a momentum ranking — and always benchmark against the **S&P 500 Total Return index**, not the price index (a price-index comparison is "misleading at best") (Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*, ch.3). Treat daily High/Low as far noisier than open/close — limit fills against them are unreliable and inflate returns. Error-check by flagging >4-SD daily returns with no matching news/index move (Chan, p.36–43); a single bad tick *inflates* mean-reversion backtests and triggers real losses, and **spreads are hyper-sensitive** because the error is a large % of the small spread — whereas the same errors *deflate* momentum backtests, so they are less dangerous there (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.3). **Closing-price trap:** consolidated SIP open/close come from a random tiny trade at any of 60+ venues; the *tradable* price is the **primary-exchange auction price** (MOO/LOO/MOC/LOC). An AAPL 8-bp uncorrelated white-noise difference manufactures *fictitious mean-reversion* excess return — use primary-exchange BBO/auction or midquotes (Chan, *Machine Trading*, Box 6.2, p.182–183; Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1: MOC/MOO fill on the primary exchange's *auction*, while the consolidated close/high/low includes off-exchange outliers, so the next-day "revert" from an unreachable outlier inflates MR backtests). **Futures data integrity:** verify vendor OHLC; pick a contract-stitching method (same-expiry / continuous / **perpetual** — perpetual is "not real," more extrapolated, further from reality); for long stock series beware mergers, splits, delistings, dividends (Tomasini, p.12–14). Chan adds the **back-adjustment dilemma**: with continuous futures you can make *either* P&L *or* return correct, **not both** — use **price back-adjustment for spread strategies** (with an additive constant to avoid negative prices), since calendar spreads are tiny relative to the leg price so a rollover error easily triggers false signals; use **settlement, not last-traded, prices** to keep the legs contemporaneous, and intermarket/future-vs-ETF spreads need *synchronous* (intraday/bid-ask) data (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1).

**Position-bias / no-skill-baseline confound (Aronson).** Observed backtest performance = (component from true predictive power) + (unwanted component from the rule's long/short **position bias** × the market's **net trend**) (Aronson, p.23). A long-biased rule in a rising market earns positive returns with **zero skill**; a genuinely skilled rule can show *losses* if its bias fights the trend (p.23–24). Position bias is often just an artifact of how loosely the long-vs-short condition is defined (p.25). **Two equivalent fixes:** (1) subtract the expected return of a no-skill rule with the *same* position bias, or (2) **detrend the market data** used for return calculation so its average daily change is zero — then any no-skill rule has expected return zero regardless of bias (detrended data is used *only* for computing returns, never for generating signals) (Aronson, p.27–28). Compute returns as **logs of daily price ratios**, not percentages, to avoid arithmetic distortions (p.28–29). This is the bias the **benchmark = a rule with no predictive power** (a randomly-generated signal, the placebo) is designed to expose — see §4a (Aronson, p.22–23).

**Regime / sample dependence** — the sample period must represent likely future regimes (volatility, volume); a benign window understates downside (Jansen, p.353–356; Narang p.153–160). **Price shocks corrupt tests:** a shock was unpredictable in real time, but a backtest credits the trend with its profit → the false premise that you can profit from shocks (Kaufman, p.303). Flag a shock as a day with True Range > 5× the 20-day-lagged ATR; profiting from >50% of shocks *overstates return and understates risk* — for trend systems, reverse those favourable shock gains to losses to see the real risk (Kaufman, p.1895–1905). **Regime shifts age backtests:** decimalization (Apr 2001) hurt stat-arb; removal of the short-sale plus-tick rule (Jun 2007) means pre-2007 short backtests overstate fills — demand good performance on **recent** data (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.24–25, 91–92). Chan reiterates that even a *clean, statistically significant* backtest will fail after a regime shift — **decimalization (2001), Reg NMS (2007), uptick-rule removal, and the 2008 volume collapse** each changed which *class* of strategy works (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1). **Small-sample / full-cycle caveat (Quantitative Value):** test across full market cycles (Gray & Carlisle ran **1964–2011**) and remember US equity returns 1921–96 were *globally exceptional* (Jorion–Goetzmann), so results may not transfer to the future or to other markets (Gray & Carlisle, *Quantitative Value*, ch.10). Davey's countermeasure: take the *limited*-test data slice at **random** from the full set, *not* from the 2008 crisis — testing on a crisis window breeds crisis-only systems (Davey2, ch. 12). Non-LLM baselines (Buy&Hold, MACD, SMA, Bollinger) are *regime-dependent and fail to generalize* — Buy&Hold was −8.59% ROI bearish vs +41.3% bullish (ATLAS, p.5–6). Always state the population a result is conditional on (*Autonomous Bidding*, p.113, 170). A long/neutral rule tested only in rising markets is **uninformative about declines** (Aronson, p.123).

## 3. Validation methods

**Train/test split.** Split first/second half (roughly equal; ≥1/3 test if data-limited). Rule of thumb for required data ≈ **252 × (# free parameters)**; cap total free parameters at ~5 (entry/exit thresholds, holding period, lookback) (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.52–55). In-sample/OOS budget that Kaufman recommends: **~60% in-sample, 20% validation, 20% final OOS**; once final OOS is used you cannot change anything, else you have overfit (p.1816–32). For ML pipelines: ordered **80/20 train/test (never random)** for all time-series models, with k-fold CV reserved only for hyperparameter tuning, and a confirmation of no overfitting on the out-of-time test set before trusting the model (MLDSBlueprints).

**Out-of-sample is single-use.** OOS testing can be done **only ONCE** — after that the data is contaminated and counts as in-sample (Grimes, p.181; QEPM Ch 16.3). Going back-and-forth *burns* the OOS into in-sample; a subtle channel is that the researcher already *knows* what happened in the OOS window ("burning data") (Narang, p.167–170). Davey makes the same point physically: treat data as fragile — "tap it lightly… and move away leaving hardly a mark"; **every retest burns the data toward a curve fit** (Davey2, ch. 12). *Mitigations:* separate research from selection; withhold/blind portions of data; randomize windows; or forgo OOS in favour of extreme in-sample vigilance + minimal parameter fitting.

### Walk-forward analysis (in depth) — Pardo / Tomasini / Davey

Static OOS (one in-sample block + one OOS block, OOS = **10–20%** of the data) is the *old* method; it is superseded by **Walk-Forward Analysis (WFA)** — a chain of successive OOS tests with **periodic re-optimisation**, which parallels how a system is actually run live (Pardo; Tomasini, p.21, p.147; Kaufman, p.1816–32; Davey uses walk-forward + real-time eval, p.30).

**Anchored vs rolling windows:**
- **Anchored WFA** — start point fixed, the in-sample window *grows*; best when the market keeps its "personality" (Tomasini, p.149–150, Fig 6.2).
- **Rolling (unanchored) WFA** — a fixed-length in-sample window *slides forward* each step; better for intraday / changing markets. Davey prefers **unanchored** (recent-data-only) so the model never relies on stale regimes (Davey2, ch. 13; Tomasini p.149–150).

**Window sizing & re-optimization cadence.** Define *every* parameter **before** running: in-period length (must contain **enough trades — Davey: ~30+ per parameter**), out-period length (**10–50% of the in-period**), the fitness function, and anchored-vs-unanchored (Davey2, ch. 13). Worked LUXOR rolling WFA: re-optimise only the two MAs on a **1-year in-sample window, shifted every 3 months** (19 runs over 2002–2008), applying each window's best params to the next 3 months of unseen data — OOS net profit came out slightly *higher* and drawdown *lower* than the static system, because the optimal params drifted only slowly (the market kept its structure) (Tomasini, p.150–154, Table 6.1). **Short in-sample windows create a short-term bias toward fast models** — fix with ≥5-yr windows (intraday is exempt but favours mean reversion).

**Walk-Forward Efficiency Ratio (WFE).** WFE = annualised **OOS** net profit ÷ annualised **in-sample** net profit. **>100% = efficient/robust**; many traders accept **≥50%** (i.e. expect roughly half the in-sample performance live). Run **≥10 WF tests** so a single lucky pass cannot fool you. Because an over-fit system fails WFA more readily than it fails a static test, *passing* WFA earns materially more trust (Tomasini, p.21–23, p.155).

**Stitching & the additivity trap.** Stitch the OOS windows into one continuous walk-forward equity curve via a **date-keyed parameter strategy**; a genuine edge produces a WF curve that stays *consistent* across time while an optimized curve *degrades* out-of-sample — that consistency is the entire point (Davey2, ch. 13). Beware **fitness-function pitfalls**: net profit (simple default, but ignores drawdown), equity-curve linearity (great for position sizing but can pick low-profit fragile iterations), return-on-account. **Many metrics are not additive** — net profit *adds* across periods, but **max drawdown and return/account do not**; unanchored WF software that assumes additivity returns flawed results (Davey2, ch. 13).

**Optimizing the in/out periods is *still* optimizing.** To tune the window lengths legitimately, **nest a second walk-forward inside the first**: tune the in/out periods on the early data, then validate **once** on ~3 untouched recent years (Davey2, ch. 13).

**Optimization profile / robust plateau (Pardo, via Tomasini).** Plot a metric (net profit, NP/DD) vs each input and look for a **broad plateau** where neighbouring parameters perform nearly as well — *not* a sharp profit spike: "If you don't like the neighbouring numbers, you've got a problem, because odds are you'll wind up with the neighbouring set's results" (Ruggiero, in Tomasini, p.23–26, p.49–50). Pick parameters from the **middle of the stable region**, not the single best (LUXOR chose slow MA 44 / fast 1, a time window centred at 9:30am even though 7:30am was the peak, stop 0.3%, trailing 0.8% — all middle-of-plateau) (Tomasini, p.53, 60, 71, 74). **Optimise one or two inputs at a time** with the rest fixed, and match the scan step to the scale (1→20 step 2; 20→200 step 20, so each step is a comparable % change) (Tomasini, p.23–26). **Average-of-results check:** if only ~1–5% of parameter combinations are profitable, the win was luck ("enough darts… a monkey at a piano"); a robust system is profitable *on the average* of tests — optionally subtract 1–2 std-dev from the mean net profit and confirm it is still positive (Tomasini, p.23).

**Degrees-of-freedom rules (Pardo, via Tomasini).** df = whole data sample − rules/conditions − **data consumed by rules/conditions**; keep **≥90% of df remaining**. Rule of thumb: *data used by a calculation × 10 ≤ window* (a 30-day MA needs ≥300 days). Data points used twice count once (Tomasini, p.16–18, citing Pardo). The **back-test window must be long enough to contain different market regimes and to produce ≥100 trades** (standard error ≈ √(n+1); 100 trades → ±~10% on every metric); LUXOR's ~2000–3000 trades give high confidence (Tomasini, p.18, p.46). **Sample size is not fixable by fractals:** 1 yr of 15-min bars ≈ 20 yr of daily ≈ 5000 bars, but that does *not* make the short sample equivalent — it still will not contain the once-in-20-years crash, and an inadequate sample gets "blindsided" by unseen patterns (Tomasini, p.155–158).

**Optimal complexity (Tomasini).** Optimising LUXOR's parameters one-by-one, OOS net profit *rose* for the first 3 (slow MA, fast MA, time filter) then *fell* from parameter 4 onward (the exits) — yet the exits still earn their keep on **risk control** (largest loss, % time in market) even when they add no OOS profit, so judge added features on the right axis (Tomasini, p.126–135). The cure for the Bollinger overfit above was simply **longer training data spanning many regimes** — re-training on 1986–2006 restored OOS profit (Tomasini, p.115–126).

**Test entries and exits separately first (Davey).** Test entries with a fixed stop+target, a fixed-bar exit, and a **random** exit — a real entry should win **>50%** (52–60% achievable) against a random exit; test exits against a generic entry and a random entry, judged on **profit/MAE/MFE, not win %**. Then test the combined core system: want **>70% of a gentle optimization's iterations profitable**; if **<30% profitable, try the *opposite* signals** (but add 2× slippage+commission — that cost is the reason most "opposite" systems fail) (Davey2, ch. 12).

**Cross-validation — but financial data is special.** Returns are **not IID** (serial correlation, heteroskedasticity); standard CV leaks future into past = data snooping (Jansen, p.280–281). Randomising serially-correlated data is invalid (Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algo*, p.278); k-folds *destroy* autocorrelation/non-stationarity (QuantAssetMgmt ch.14). Naive k-fold is "technically invalid on time-series" (Halls-Moore, *Successful Algo*, p.183–188) — yet k-fold (**k = 5 or 10**) is the variance/bias sweet spot vs a single validation-set or costly LOOCV (Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algo*, p.278–299). Use **time-aware CV** instead:
- **`TimeSeriesSplit` / `MultipleTimeSeriesCV`** — walk-forward with expanding (or fixed via `max_train_size`) train window; non-overlapping test periods; roll forward (Jansen, p.280, 510). Parameters: `train_length / test_length / lookahead gap / n_splits` (Jansen, p.528–533). Use **rolling window** (fixed train slides) or **expanding window** (train grows) (Kaabar, p.259–261).
- **Avoid early-stopping in CV** (biases validation metrics upward — uses future-outcome info) (Jansen, p.535, 575). Never use plain `train_test_split` or OOB on sequential data (Jansen, p.533).

**Coqueret & Guida's panel-CV recipe (ch.10.4).** Plain k-fold cross-validation is **proscribed** — it trains on the future and tests on the past. Two valid routes: **(a)** De Prado-style CV with *no informational overlap* (purge/embargo), **valid only if you assume the cross-section is time-stationary**; or **(b)** **walk-forward retraining at each rebalance**. A pragmatic shortcut they endorse: because hyperparameters are usually **stable across dates**, *tune them once on an early sub-sample* and then **reuse the tuned values while still retraining the model each period** — this keeps the expensive search out of the walk-forward loop without re-snooping. **Hyperparameter search itself (ch.10.3):** random search beats grid search (Bergstra & Bengio 2012); use two-stage grids (locate, then zoom); and **Bayesian optimization** models the objective `O(p)` as a Gaussian process and picks the next parameter set by maximizing **Expected Improvement** — efficient precisely *because each backtest is costly* — though in practice manual tuning + grid search still dominate the literature. Each tuning pass is another trial, so it feeds straight into the deflated-Sharpe / trial-count accounting in §4a.

### Financial cross-validation — purging, embargoing & CPCV (López de Prado)

Standard CV fails on financial labels because a label's *evaluation window* overlaps neighbouring samples, leaking information across the train/test boundary even when rows are nominally disjoint (López de Prado, *AFML*, Ch 7; *ML for Asset Managers*, p.6). Three constructs fix this:

- **Purging** — drop every *training* observation whose **label-evaluation period overlaps** the time interval of a *validation* (test) label. If a test label is built from prices over `[t0, t1]`, purge any training label whose own evaluation window intersects `[t0, t1]` (López de Prado; library `timeseriescv`; Jansen, p.281). Equivalent idea appears as inserting a **lag/embargo between features and the response** to block autocorrelation bleed, and in the **triple-barrier** labeling (profit/loss/time barriers → buy/sell/hold) whose post-breach return is what overlaps (López de Prado triple-barrier; QuantAssetMgmt ch.9).
- **Embargoing** — *additionally* drop training samples that fall **immediately *after*** each test period (a short embargo bar), because serial correlation makes the bars just after a test set partially predictable from it (López de Prado; Jansen, p.281).
- **Combinatorial Purged Cross-Validation (CPCV)** — instead of one walk-forward path, split the series into N groups and take **many train/test group combinations**, each purged and embargoed, producing **far more historical paths than a single walk-forward** and hence a *distribution* of Sharpe ratios rather than one point estimate (López de Prado, *AFML* Ch 12; Jansen, p.281). This is the recommended scenario-simulation engine, and the vehicle through which **thousands of CPCV splits** feed the deflated-Sharpe / FWER machinery (López de Prado, *ML for Asset Managers*, p.6). AFML positions **three backtesting paradigms to triangulate**, none sufficient alone: (1) **historical simulation = walk-forward** (Ch 11–12); (2) **scenario simulation = CV / CPCV** (Ch 12); (3) **simulation on synthetic data** (Ch 13) (AFML, Ch 14.1).

**Synthetic-data backtesting (AFML Ch 13).** To avoid fitting a strategy to a *single random historical path*, fit a stochastic process to prices — AFML fits a **discrete Ornstein-Uhlenbeck** process and grids profit-take × stop-loss into a 20×20 Sharpe heat-map. When the half-life τ is large the process approaches a random walk and **no** profit-take/stop-loss combination has consistently better Sharpe — calibrating a rule there just selects a fluke; so the **optimal trading rule (OTR) should be derived from the *process parameters*, not from historical simulation** (the OTR conjecture: a discrete O-U price has a unique PT/SL pair maximizing Sharpe) (AFML, Ch 13.6.1, 13.7). Generate synthetic paths with Cholesky (preserve correlations), copulas, regime-switching, or GANs (QuantAssetMgmt ch.14).

**Monte Carlo / resampling.** A single equity path suffers **sample bias** — use resampling/Monte Carlo to generate many paths and see the *distribution* of drawdowns and recovery times (Narang, p.153–160). When the parameter search space is too large, use **Monte Carlo sampling** of parameter combinations (e.g. 8,000 vs 100M exhaustive tests), using each value a "reasonable" number of times (Kaufman, p.1824–35). **Reduce evaluation variance with control variates** ("adjust for luck"): regress the score on observable exogenous factors and subtract the predicted luck component (cut variance ~22%; bias →0 as samples grow) — the trading analogue is neutralizing benchmark/factor moves before judging an alpha (*Autonomous Bidding*, p.176–178).

### Monte Carlo trade-order & start-date tests (Davey / Tomasini)

Beyond resampling parameters, two Monte-Carlo tests stress the *realized* track record:
- **Trade-order shuffle (Tomasini).** Shuffle the historical trade order ("selection without replacement") thousands of times (e.g. **5000 runs**) to estimate **worst-case drawdown by confidence level** — LUXOR's $10k drawdown became **$21k at 99% confidence**. Caveat: Monte Carlo **cannot detect over-fitting** and assumes Gaussian returns, which fails under fat tails (Tomasini, p.101–109). A complementary **timescale analysis** keeps all parameters and just changes the bar compression (5/10/30/120 min…) as a cheap pseudo-OOS — LUXOR stayed profitable across 5–35 min (most profit at 30 min), evidence the edge is *structural*, not curve-fit (Tomasini, p.90–100).
- **Davey's Monte Carlo + "is it real?" / monkey test (the centerpiece).** As a pipeline gate after walk-forward, run a Monte Carlo on the trade stream (and start-date) to get distributions of net profit and drawdown (Davey2, ch. 12). Then the **monkey test**: replace your **entry**, then your **exit**, then **both**, with *random* signals **tuned to match** your strategy's **trade count, long/short ratio, and average bars-in-trade**, and run **8,000 times**. A genuine edge **beats ~90% of the monkeys on both net profit *and* max drawdown**. Crucially, **re-run it live every 6–12 months: when the monkeys catch up, the edge is gone — stop trading** (a worked example fired this signal in 2012–2013, ahead of a real breakdown) (Davey2, ch. 12).
- **Clenow's "a random number generator can beat the index" (the baseline that must be subtracted).** Monthly-rebalanced portfolios of **50 *random* S&P 500 stocks**, ATR risk-parity sized, beat the S&P 500 *Total Return* in essentially every run of hundreds — because the cap-weighted index is itself a mediocre, top-heavy "trading system," and equal-ish *risk* weighting de-concentrates away from the mega-caps. The lesson is a no-skill baseline: momentum must add **selection** *on top of* this structural equal-risk edge, so don't credit the structural edge to your signal (Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*, ch.15).

**Parameter stability / sensitivity.** Vary parameters; **if only the optimum is acceptable, it's overfit** — a *smooth* performance surface = real effect, a jumpy surface = artefact (Halls-Moore, *Successful Algo*, p.16; Chan, p.60). Neighbours should behave similarly — pick a **robust plateau, not a lonely peak** (Narang, p.153–160; Leshik: vary key params ±5%, accept ~6/10 profitable, push to 7–8/10, p.33–34). Run **±variants to confirm the *concept* survives** and reject single "best" parameter sets — these are stability checks, *not* optimisation (Covel, *Trend Following*, ch.4, ch.8). **Clenow's momentum stability checks (not optimisation):** a regression window of **60 / 90 / 120 / 240 days all beat the index handily** (90 ≈ 120, 60 slightly worse, 240 slightly worse), so the concept is robust and the exact number is unimportant; even **simpler ranking metrics** (raw 90-day % return; slope *without* the R² adjustment) give *statistically indistinguishable* end results — yet he keeps the R²-adjusted slope anyway because it steers you into *comfortable, sensible* holdings you will actually keep buying (avoiding takeover/gap/choppy names); position-sizing at **5 / 10 / 50 bps** of risk all beat the index too (Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*, ch.3). Use **percentage/geometric spacing**, not equal increments (49→50d = 2% but 2→3d = 50%; equal steps over-weight long periods) (Kaufman, p.1820–24). For expected profit use the **average of neighbouring tests**; for risk use the **worst drawdown among neighbours**; then trade **3+ parameter sets** spaced across the surface, each ⅓ of funds, netting positions — you'll achieve the average (Kaufman, p.1853–57).

**A rule change is a *real* improvement only if** the average of all tests improves with σ unchanged, OR σ falls with the average unchanged (global vs single-case fix). Always prove an added feature (stop, profit-take, timing) improves a *simple* method that is already profitable (Kaufman, p.644, 1859).

**Parameter stability also drives re-optimization cadence (QEPM).** Pool monthly factor-premium regressions and test (F-test, 5% cutoff) whether premiums are *equal* across a quarter/half/year; high rejection rates (e.g. **55% for long-term asset growth over quarters**) ⇒ re-estimate *more often* — QEPM re-estimated **monthly**, using **rolling in-sample windows** (~60 months back, rolling forward each month) rather than a single static fit, while checking the re-estimates track real change, not noise (Chincarini & Kim, *QEPM*, Ch 16.7, Tenet 6).

**Overlapping vs non-overlapping portfolios (Gray & Vogel).** For holding periods > 1 month, **stagger entries to minimize seasonal/timing luck** (a 3-month hold deploys 1/3 of capital each month, each tranche held 3 months) — used throughout the momentum tests; note a *seasonality* test deliberately uses **non-overlapping** quarterly portfolios precisely *to capture* the seasonality it studies (Gray & Vogel, *Quantitative Momentum*, ch.5–7).

**Sanity-check the harness itself.** Run a known-answer test — e.g. random entry at 1:1 R:R should win ~50%; validate the harness before trusting conclusions (Davey, p.142). The **"can you lose on purpose?" test** (Mauboussin): if you can't deliberately trade your method to *lose* and get results clearly different from trying to win, your edge is just chance (Grimes, p.108).

## 4. Performance metrics & statistical significance

**Returns.** Annualized return = avg daily return × 252; CAGR is the compounded figure (*151 Strategies*, p.84, 129). Convert prices to a **return series** (% or log) before analysis (Grimes, p.184); log returns are additive, so gross perf = `exp(Σ log-returns)` (Hilpisch, p.92). Avoid quoting *average* returns — the denominator is ambiguous (one/both sides, levered/unlevered) (Chan, *Quantitative Trading*, p.43). Be explicit about geometric `g_P` vs log `ζ_P` vs arithmetic `a_P` (`ζ_P ≤ g_P ≤ a_P`) and stay consistent: compound returns measure ending wealth, arithmetic returns are linear across periods (needed when attributing/cumulating sources — handle the cross-products) (Grinold & Kahn, Eq. 17.2–17.6, p.483–484).

**Sharpe ratio** = mean(excess return) / SD(excess return).
- Annualize: × **√N_T**, where N_T = periods/yr. Daily → ×√252; an **hourly** NYSE-hours strategy → N_T = 252 × 6.5 = 1638 (common error: using 252×24) (Chan, p.44–45).
- For a **dollar-neutral / self-financing** strategy do **not** subtract the risk-free rate (credit interest ≈ r_F cancels); subtract r_F only if the strategy incurs financing cost (Chan, p.43–44). The HFT version likewise drops the risk-free term (no overnight carry) and is **invariant to leverage** (Aldridge, Ch 6).
- Rules of thumb: Sharpe < 1 not viable stand-alone; profitable-most-months ⇒ Sharpe > 2; profitable-most-days ⇒ Sharpe > 3 (Chan, p.21).
- **Sharpe vs Sortino for skewed strategies:** Sharpe penalizes *upside* volatility, which unfairly hurts positively-skewed trend-following (2008's huge gains *hurt* the Sharpe) — prefer **Sortino** there (Covel, *Trend Following*).

**Sortino** (downside-deviation denominator), **Calmar** = return / max-drawdown, **Sterling**, **Omega**, and the **information ratio** (Sharpe minus risk-free) round out the risk-adjusted suite (Narang, p.153–160; Kaabar, p.187). All of these drawdown-based (Calmar/Sterling/Burke), downside (Sortino/Omega/Kappa), and VaR-based (CVaR/MVaR) ratios tend to **rank strategies similarly** to each other (Aldridge, Ch 6).

**Drawdown.** drawdown = current equity vs the prior high-watermark; **max drawdown = peak-to-trough** (trough must *follow* the peak); **max-drawdown duration** = longest recovery time. The two maxima usually don't coincide. Set personal tolerance (e.g. 20% / 3 months) and compare to the backtest *before* committing (Chan, p.21–22). Formula form: `(trough − peak)/peak` (Kaabar, p.186). Prefer the **% "underwater equity"** (DD ÷ prior equity high) for comparability; compute the **average DD and its std-dev** — a largest DD within 1–2 std-dev of the mean is "normal," beyond 2 std-dev means rethink the logic (unless a freak event); pro tolerance ~20–30%, 10% is great, 40–50% unbearable (Tomasini, p.30–31). Evaluate **depth *and* recovery time** — use **drawdown ÷ annual return** ("years of return given up"): a trend core ≈ 1.1–1.7 yrs vs MSCI World's **11.4 yrs** (Covel). Cognitively, expect the backtest's drawdown *live* and be ready — people abandon good strategies mid-drawdown (Halls-Moore, *Successful Algo*, p.17–18). Realistic profile to expect: **drawdowns of 20–30% lasting a year-plus are normal**, with higher volatility *while in* drawdown (Covel).

**Hit rate / accuracy** = correct directional predictions / total ×100 (Kaabar, p.184). Even world-class intraday traders win barely >50% (often 40–60%); small frequent losses are normal — measure in **percentages, not dollars/shares** (Bellafiore, p.95, 157, 348). For targets that don't hover around zero (ISM PMI, COT levels) use `calculate_directional_accuracy()` (Kaabar, p.263). **Expectancy** E(X) = Σ pᵢxᵢ; a true edge means Σ(winner P&L) > Σ(loser P&L) over a *large* sample (Grimes, p.21–24).

**Profit factor** = gross profit / gross loss (>1 profitable) (Kaabar, p.186; Tomasini: ~2 healthy, good systems 1.5–3, **>3 is suspicious — re-examine for over-fit**, p.30). **Turnover** and **capacity** matter because alpha is eaten by costs at scale — "the third side of performance is trade count": without costs momentum ≫ SMA, but with fixed+proportional costs (e.g. $10 + 1%) the *low-turnover* SMA wins (Hilpisch, p.184).

### From: Tomasini — the metric panel he actually uses
- **Reject net profit alone** — it ignores risk and *when* the profit was made; always compare return to risk and cut stats **year-by-year** (they should look alike) (p.27–28).
- **Average trade** = net profit / #trades; it *must* cover slippage+commission with profit left over (track its % over time) (p.28).
- **% profitable:** ~50% is healthy; **>60–70% is a warning** unless using profit targets (then avg-win/avg-loss falls under 2); trend systems can be fine at ~35% (p.28–29).
- **Avg win / avg loss ratio:** ~2 is healthy (inverse relationship with % profitable) (p.28–29).
- **RINA Index** = (select net profit, excluding ±3σ outlier trades) ÷ average drawdown ÷ % time-in-market = reward/risk per unit time; **should be >30, higher is better** (p.32).
- **Coefficient of variation** (std-dev/mean) of the metrics **should be <250%** (p.32).
- Visual triage the authors actually run: a **smoothly rising equity line** with shallow drawdowns and welcome **flat periods** (the system sits out when there's no edge), then check avg trade, profit factor, % profitable, avg-win/avg-loss, and the monthly-return distribution (p.32–33).

### From: AFML — what a backtest report must contain
Report statistics in groups (López de Prado, *AFML*, Ch 14): **general characteristics** — time range covering *many regimes*, AUM, **capacity** (= max AUM that still holds a target risk-adjusted return), leverage (avg position / avg AUM), ratio of longs (≈ 0.5 for market-neutral), **frequency of *bets* not trades**, holding period, **turnover**, correlation-to-underlying; **performance** — PnL, hit ratio, avg return on hits vs misses; **runs/drawdowns**; implementation shortfall; efficiency; classification scores; attribution.
- **Bets ≠ trades.** A *bet* is a sequence of same-side positions until flattened/flipped; counting trades **overstates the number of independent opportunities** — derive bet timing from the target-position series (AFML, Ch 14.3, Snippet 14.1). (Carver/Aldridge echo: a *declining win ratio* is a sign you are nearing capacity, Aldridge Ch 6.)
- **Returns concentration (HHI).** Compute HHI-style indices on positive returns (`h⁺`), negative returns (`h⁻`), and bets-per-month (`h[t]`); prefer strategies with high SR, many bets/yr, high hit ratio, and **low h⁺** (no right fat-tail), **low h⁻** (no left fat-tail), **low h[t]** (bets not concentrated in time). Also report the **95-percentile drawdown** and **time-under-water** (AFML, Ch 14.5).
- **Classification scores for meta-labeling.** Judge a secondary/overlay model with **precision, recall and F1** (harmonic mean), *not* accuracy — accuracy is misleading under class imbalance (predict-all-negative scores high accuracy with recall 0); **negative log-loss (cross-entropy)** is preferred because it weights predictions by confidence, not just correctness (AFML, Ch 14.8).

### From: Coqueret & Guida — ML learning metrics that map to investment value
- **Regression metrics (ch.10.1.1).** MSE/RMSE are standard but **suboptimal for allocation**. Decompose `MSE = realized² + predicted² − 2·(realized·predicted)`; only the **cross-term** matters to an allocator, because it rewards *correct direction* of bets — so a custom loss maximizing `Σ yᵢỹᵢ` (a correlation-like objective, which NNs permit) can beat vanilla L2. Judge fit with **out-of-sample R² against a *zero*-prediction benchmark** (Gu et al. 2020 form, *no mean in the denominator*): beating a constant-zero forecast is the real bar (consistent with Narang's "a great OOS R² is ~0.04–0.05").
- **Classification metrics (ch.10.1.2).** Read the **confusion matrix**, and note the asymmetry: a **Type-I error (predict up, get down → the name *enters* the portfolio and loses) is far more damaging than a Type-II** (a missed opportunity). Track **precision** (probability that a chosen investment is good), **recall/TPR**, **F1**, and **AUC/ROC**. Critical caveat that mirrors the no-skill-baseline theme: **do *not* benchmark the hit ratio against 0.5 — benchmark against the *unconditional* share of positive returns** (≈0.556 in their sample), otherwise a long-biased model looks skilled for free.

**Error metrics for ML models:** MAE (`Σ|ŷ−y|/n`, outlier-robust), MSE (`Σ(ŷ−y)²/n`, penalizes large errors), RMSE (`√MSE`, target-unit scaled) — lower is better, compare train vs test (Kaabar, p.184–185). **R² / fit:** R² = 1 − SS_resid/SS_total (*151 Strategies*, p.318). A *great* **out-of-sample** R² is only ~0.04–0.05; >0.15 "and you'll be arrested for insider trading" (Narang, p.153–160). **Strategy backtest pattern (ML):** `Strategy Returns = market_pct_change × predicted_signal.shift(1)`; compare cumulative strategy vs actual, and assess with **Sharpe + max drawdown, not just accuracy**; for portfolios the **in-sample vs out-of-sample Sharpe is the decisive metric** (MLDSBlueprints; QuantAssetMgmt: condense a strategy into a single smooth statistic — Sharpe / rolling return — for response design).

**Statistical significance.**
- **Chan's three nulls for the test statistic (e.g. mean daily return = Sharpe·√N) (*Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1).** Hypothesis-test the statistic against a null, but **which null matters**: **(1) Gaussian** — `mean(ret)/std(ret)·√N`, critical values 1.645 (p=0.05) / 2.326 (p=0.01); **(2) Monte Carlo on *simulated* price series** sharing the same first 3–4 moments (e.g. `pearsrnd`) — what fraction of simulated runs beat the backtest?; **(3) Lo–Mamaysky–Wang** — randomize trade *entry dates* while preserving #long/#short and holding period. The three can disagree: a "TU" momentum strategy **passed Gaussian (stat 2.93) but reached only the 88th percentile under simulated returns**, revealing that **high kurtosis alone favours momentum** — and more round-trip trades raise significance under every null.
- **t-stat of the signal:** df = n−1; 5% significant, 1% highly significant; more rules/variables ⇒ more data needed. Apply a 2-sample t-test to **trade returns** to check consistency (not to discard "inconsistent" price data) (Kaufman, p.155–157). The null hypothesis can only be *rejected*, never proved (Grimes, p.180, 187). **Hypothesis-testing logic (Aronson):** start by assuming the **null** — the rule has *no* predictive power and any backtest profit was luck (a chance correspondence of signals with trends); the **alternative** is that it has predictive power. Build the **sampling distribution** of returns a *useless* rule could produce by chance (centered at zero on **detrended** data) and ask whether the observed return is too far into the right tail to be luck (Aronson, p.166–169).
- **The p-value subtlety (Aronson).** The tail area is the probability of seeing performance *this good if the null were true* — it speaks to the likelihood of the **evidence**, **NOT** the probability that either hypothesis is true. Evidence highly improbable under the null lets you infer the null is false (proof by contradiction); a *large* tail area gives **no** justification to reject the null. Asserting a rule works merely because the backtest was profitable commits the **fallacy of affirming the consequent** (Aronson, p.118, p.169). Strength of an inductive (backtest) conclusion grows with the **quantity and quality of evidence**; judging a rule on a handful of signals is a "hasty generalization" (Aronson, p.123).
- **Statistical ≠ economic significance:** a pattern can be statistically significant yet *not economically* significant if the edge is smaller than transaction costs — significance is firm/trader-specific (Grimes, p.171).
- **Standard error ≈ 1/√N:** 1 trade = 100% error, 4 = 50%, **400 trades = 5%**. Slow systems (2–3 trades/yr) must be tested across many markets to accumulate trades (Kaufman, p.125–126). Learn from 20–30 trades not 1; change rules on ~50 not 5 (Grimes, p.109–110); be wary below ~50 trades — aggressive filters can cut a 1,153-trade strategy to 18 (Davey, p.101, 114). **Tomasini's ≥100-trade rule:** the back-test window must produce **≥100 trades** so the standard error (≈ √(n+1)) keeps every metric to ±~10% (Tomasini, p.18).
- **Data requirements to *confirm* a Sharpe (Carver, T-test, 2-sigma):** years needed — SR 0.3 → **~37 yrs**; SR 0.5 → ~20; SR 1.0 → ~6; SR 2.0 → ~1.4 (Carver, Table 5). To distinguish *two* rules you usually need ~30 yrs unless they are both highly correlated *and* far apart in SR (Carver, Table 6). **Evaluation length is set by the Sharpe ratio** (Aldridge, Ch 6): higher claimed SR ⇒ shorter verification window (SR=2 ⇒ ~8 months of daily data; SR=6 ⇒ <1 month). *Mitigation for short history:* **pool data across instruments** — diversification raises a rule's SR and makes rules distinguishable; only fit instruments separately if there is a *statistically significant* difference, which is rarely true (Carver, ch.3).
- **Autocorrelation:** Durbin-Watson d ∈ [0,4]; d≈2 none, d<2 positive (trend persistence), d>2 negative (Kaufman, p.158–159).
- **Distributional checks:** Kolmogorov–Smirnov test (`scipy.stats.kstest(data,'uniform')` → statistic + p-value; small p ⇒ reject the assumed CDF) validates any distributional assumption behind VaR/simulation (Lachowicz, p.110, 187). Asset returns are rarely normal (fat tails) and stationarity may not hold — don't assume either (Grimes, p.180, 188).
- **Model bias** = #bullish / #bearish signals (ideal ≈ 1.0) detects directionally skewed systems (Kaabar, p.186; same as AFML's "ratio of longs ≈ 0.5" for market-neutral).

**Tradability tests (use *bars*, not trades — far larger sample):** **ADF** for mean reversion (regress Δyₜ on yₜ₋₁, test γ=0; `statsmodels.tsa.adfuller`); **Hurst exponent** (H<0.5 mean-reverting, =0.5 GBM, >0.5 trending); **cointegration / CADF** for pairs (OLS hedge ratio β, then ADF the residuals — AREX–WLL stat −2.96 < −2.87 crit ⇒ cointegrated) (Halls-Moore, *Successful Algo*, p.88–95). **Chan: run the stationarity pre-test *before* the strategy backtest (ch.2).** ADF / Variance-Ratio / half-life are **strategy-agnostic** and use *every bar*, so they carry far more statistical significance than the handful of round-trips a strategy generates — and **a short half-life guarantees that *some* profitable mean-reversion strategy exists**, so the pre-test both screens candidates and tells you the edge is structural rather than fit (Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.2).

## 4a. The data-mining / multiple-testing problem (the deepest validation issue)

This is the single most important addition to this file: it elevates "data-snooping" from a bias bullet to the central statistical problem of backtesting. **Most discoveries in finance are false** ("Marcos' Third Law of Backtesting"). Selection across many trials inflates the apparent Sharpe ratio *even when true skill is exactly zero*, so **every backtest must be reported together with all the trials run to produce it** — without the trial count you cannot estimate the result's false-discovery probability (López de Prado, *AFML*, Ch 14.7.3; *ML for Asset Managers*, p.105).

**It is alarmingly easy to manufacture significance by coincidence.**
- **20 informationless strategies give a ~64% chance** that at least one shows |t| > 2 (Grinold & Kahn, p.335–339).
- Testing **100 zero-edge rules** at an SR cutoff of 1.0 yields **~16 false positives**; to keep <5% chance of accepting one truly-bad rule among 100 candidates you need an SR cutoff of **~1.5** (with 10 yrs of data) — far above a realistic rule SR of ~0.3 (Carver, Tables 3–4).
- Aronson's empirical capstone: a study of **6,402 TA rules** on the S&P 500 found **no rule with statistically significant predictive power** after correcting for data-mining bias — the apparent winners were chance (Aronson, *Evidence-Based Technical Analysis*). (Cf. Kaufman's "50 of 1,000 strategies profit by chance," p.1859, and López de Prado's worked **6,385 backtests collapsing to 4 effectively-independent strategies**, p.114.)
- *Quantitative Value*'s rule of thumb is the same order of magnitude from the practitioner side: **expect ~1 in 5 random strategies to look "significant,"** and the *narrative fallacy* makes a plausible spurious result especially hard to discard (Gray & Carlisle, *Quantitative Value*, ch.10).

**Precision depends on the prior odds (López de Prado, *ML for Asset Managers*, p.106).** With true:false odds θ, precision = (1−β)·θ / [(1−β)·θ + α]. A strategy is **more likely false than true when (1−β)·θ < α**. Example: if only **1 in 100** strategies is real (θ = 1/99), then at α = 0.05, β = 0.2 a p-value of 0.05 implies an **86% false-discovery rate**. This is *why* you must gather theoretical evidence a strategy should exist *before* backtesting — it raises θ. Under K independent trials the family-wise error compounds: **α_K = 1 − (1−α)^K**, β_K = β^K, and precision must use α_K, β_K.

**The "False Strategy" theorem / expected maximum Sharpe under the null (López de Prado).** For K trials of a *true-zero-Sharpe* strategy with cross-trial variance `V[{SR̂ₖ}]`, the expected **maximum** Sharpe is

  **`E[max_k SR̂ₖ] ≈ √V · [ (1−γ)·Z⁻¹(1−1/K) + γ·Z⁻¹(1−1/(K·e)) ]`**

where γ ≈ 0.5772 is the Euler–Mascheroni constant and Z⁻¹ is the inverse standard-Normal CDF (*AFML*, Ch 14.7.3; *ML for Asset Managers*, p.108). This hurdle grows quickly with K and with the dispersion of trial-Sharpes: **1,000 trials at V=1 ⇒ expected max Sharpe = 3.26 even when the true Sharpe is zero.** Unless your discovered Sharpe **≫** this hurdle, it is a false positive (Monte Carlo confirms the theorem is asymptotically unbiased).

**Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio (PSR)** corrects an *observed* SR for short, skewed, fat-tailed track records:

  **`PSR = Z[ (SR_obs − SR*)·√(T−1) / √(1 − γ₃·SR_obs + ((γ₄−1)/4)·SR_obs²) ]`**

where T = #returns, γ₃ = skew, γ₄ = kurtosis (3 for Gaussian), and SR* = benchmark Sharpe (often 0). PSR **rises** with more observations and positive skew, **falls** with fat tails; require **PSR > 0.95** (5% significance) (*AFML*, Ch 14.7.2). The underpinning: SR̂ is asymptotically Normal even for non-Normal returns, but its variance depends on skewness γ₃ and kurtosis γ₄ (Mertens) — **wrongly assuming IID-Normal grossly underestimates the false-positive probability**, which is critical because hedge-fund returns are negatively skewed and fat-tailed (*ML for Asset Managers*, p.107).

**Deflated Sharpe Ratio (DSR)** is PSR with **SR\* set to the data-mined expected maximum** `E[max_k SR̂ₖ]` rather than a user-chosen benchmark — i.e. the rejection threshold is *raised to reflect the multiplicity of trials*:

  **`DSR = Z[ (SR̂ − E[max_k SR̂ₖ])·√(T−1) / √(1 − γ̂₃·SR̂ + ((γ̂₄−1)/4)·SR̂²) ]`**

It is the probability the *true* Sharpe > 0 after adjusting for **skewness, kurtosis, sample length, AND multiple testing** simultaneously; require **DSR > 0.95** (*AFML*, Ch 14.7.3; *ML for Asset Managers*, p.113). This is the headline defense against backtest overfitting; the conceptual reading is a **haircut** — the more trials you ran, the larger the deduction from the observed Sharpe before you believe it (and Jansen's minimum-backtest-length corollary: **2 yrs of daily data supports ≈ 7 strategies; 5 yrs ≈ 45**, p.358).

**Estimating the *effective* number of trials (López de Prado, *ML for Asset Managers*, p.114).** Real trials are *correlated*, not independent, so a naïve K overstates the penalty. **Cluster the trials' correlation matrix with ONC** to count effectively-independent strategies E[K] (a conservative lower bound) — e.g. **6,385 backtests collapse to 4 strategies**; form min-variance cluster-aggregate return series, annualize each cluster Sharpe, and estimate `V[{SR̂ₖ}]` from them.

**FWER alternative (López de Prado, *ML for Asset Managers*, p.116–118).** Instead of (or alongside) DSR, apply **Šidák's correction** `α = 1 − (1−α_K)^{1/K}` (Bonferroni ≈ α_K/K) using E[K] from ONC. Worked example: a strategy with non-annualized Sharpe 0.0791 (skew −3, kurt 10, 1,250 obs) gives single-trial α ≈ 0.0062 (annualized Sharpe ≈ 1.25 — *looks great*), but correcting for **E[K] = 10 effective trials** raises FWER to **α_K ≈ 0.0608 — likely a false discovery** (assuming Normal returns would have wrongly reported ≈ 0.0261). **SBuMT** (Selection Bias under Multiple Testing) is compounded by "hyperfitting": the researcher selects the best, then the firm selects the best across researchers (*ML for Asset Managers*, p.105).

**Coqueret & Guida operationalize the DSR for ML-factor research (ch.12.4.2).** They treat backtest overfitting as the **core warning** of the whole book: iterating strategy/parameter/engine on one dataset *guarantees* an apparent winner, and **live Sharpe ≈ backtest Sharpe / 2 at best** (Harvey & Liu 2015). Their remedy is the **deflated Sharpe ratio** (Bailey & de Prado 2014, their eq. 12.5), which requires you to **store the metrics of *every* strategy tried** — the number of trials N, plus skew and kurtosis — and then compares the best observed SR to the theoretical **max SR\*** expected from N random trials (exactly the §4a hurdle). **Simple safeguard heuristic (ch.12.4.3):** take the benchmark to be an **equal-weighted portfolio of the *entire* universe**; if a long-only strategy's transaction-cost-adjusted Sharpe exceeds **2× the benchmark's**, suspect a bug. Realistically, beating that benchmark by **~150 bps/yr at the same volatility is already excellent, and >5% excess is dubious** — a concrete sniff-test that complements the formal DSR.

**Quantitative Value's anti-data-mining discipline (ch.10).** Gray & Carlisle rank *past results last* in their **Sustainable Alpha pyramid**: **Robust Idea Generation** (a real economic edge — *who is on the other side of the trade?*) → **Risk Management** → **Operations** → and only then **Past Results**. The defenses they actually apply: **start from established value theory and published research** (don't mine the idea out of the data), then run **out-of-sample tests through 2011** and **discard any variable that vanishes out-of-sample** — the direct antidote to data-mining accounting/value signals. On **benchmarking honesty**, compare **market-cap-weighted** strategy returns to the **S&P 500 total return** (equal-weighting flatters by overweighting small names), and use **factor models to strip the "indexable" opportunity cost** (size, value, momentum, liquidity) so what remains is *true* alpha rather than a repackaged known premium (Gray & Carlisle, *Quantitative Value*, ch.10).

**The classic statistician's guardrails (Grinold & Kahn, p.335–339), still the practical checklist:** **intuition** (an economic story *before* the backtest), **restraint** (pre-map the variations; do not fish), **sensibleness** (only ~10% of real IRs exceed 1, so disbelieve IR > 2 for public data like book-to-price), and **out-of-sample testing**. Deflated/Probabilistic Sharpe is also referenced in Jansen (p.357–359) as the multiple-testing/short-sample/non-normal correction to apply before believing a Sharpe.

### Information analysis — measuring a signal's *unfettered* value (Grinold & Kahn)
Before full backtesting (which adds turnover/cost/tradability), **information analysis** measures a signal's raw value in two steps (Ch 12): (1) **turn predictions into portfolios** — six ways, of which the recommended **controlled factor portfolios** (long–short, equal value & beta, unit signal bet, minimum tracking, plus extra factor controls) isolate the signal like a designed experiment; (2) **evaluate** via `r_P = α + β·r_B + ε` → realized α, β, with **t-stat = α/SE(α)** (|t| > 2 ⇒ <5% chance of luck) and **IR ≈ t-stat/√(years)** (Eq. 12.2) — the same |t| over *more* years is *more significant* but the *same value added* (p.315–328). The **fundamental law of active management** ties it together: **`IR = IC·√BR`** where IC = corr(signal, realized alpha) and BR = breadth = independent bets/yr (e.g. book-to-price US: IR 0.27, IC 0.01 ⇒ BR ≈ 700 from 6,000 raw signal-items/yr). For episodic signals use **event studies** with **residual (asset-specific) returns** to strip calendar/market effects; forecast decays as `IC(j) = IC(1)·γ^{j−1}` (half-life `log0.5/logγ`), and rare events have small effective breadth `N* = p·N/[1 − γ²(1−p)]` (Eq. 12.12) (p.329–337).

### Performance attribution — separating skill from luck (Grinold & Kahn / QEPM)
Cross-sectional return tables **cannot** separate skill from luck (survivorship, one-man-one-vote, no risk adjustment); **returns-based** time series can, and **holdings-based (portfolio-based)** attribution further says *where* the skill is (Grinold & Kahn, p.477–487). Key statistic: **`SE{IR} ≈ 1/√(years)`** (Eq. 17.1) ⇒ confirming a top-quartile (IR = 0.5) manager at 95% needs **~16 years**; even a *true* IR = 0.5 manager has only a 56% chance of a positive month and 87% over 5 yrs (so **13% of skilled managers post a negative 5-yr alpha**) (p.480–482). Use **ex-ante (a-priori) betas** in the Jensen regression `r_P = α + β·r_B + ε`, t ≈ (α/ω)·√T (Eq. 17.7–17.8): switching realized→forecast beta flipped one example's alpha from +3 to −4 bp/month (p.493). Refinements: **benchmark-timing** `+ γ·Max{0,r_B}` (Henriksson–Merton, γ>0 = timing skill), Bayesian (Vasicek) shrinkage, **style analysis** (Sharpe 1992 constrained QP — fine for large studies, unreliable for identifying a single manager's style), and **Fama–French SMB/HML** (Carhart adds momentum) (p.487–497). **Holdings-based** decomposition `r_P = Σ x_Pj·b_j + u_P` isolates the **specific return = stock-picking after factor control**, groupable by analyst/sector — but attribution is **not unique** (you choose the factors) (p.497–506). **QEPM's Brinson attribution** splits excess return exactly into **allocation effect** (sector bets) + **security-selection effect** (within-sector picking): `r_P − r_B = AE + SSE` — a Dec-2020 example had excess 2.726% with **AE = −0.284% (poor sector calls) but SSE = +3.010% (strong picks)**, diagnosing a model that picks stocks well but sectors poorly and arguing for a sector-neutral constraint (Chincarini & Kim, *QEPM*, Ch 15.5, 17.2). Attribute against **your own α factors**, not just the commercial risk model, when that is what you actually manage (QEPM, Ch 15.5.2).

### Microstructure validation targets (Hasbrouck)
For execution/impact validation, the **random-walk variance `σ_w²`** is the key permanent-impact statistic: decompose price into a random-walk (permanent) plus a stationary (transitory pricing-error `s_t`) component; from the autocovariance generating function **`σ_w² = θ(1)²·σ_e²`** with `θ(1) = Σθ_j`, and in the generalized Roll model `σ_w² = λ² + σ_u² = γ_0 + 2γ_1` — remarkably **identifiable without further restrictions** even when the structural parameters separately are not, and **invariant to the VAR variable set**, which makes it a *stable validation target* across stocks and samples (Hasbrouck, *Empirical Market Microstructure*, p.46, 55, 100; time-scales as `Var(m_t − m_{t−k}) = k·σ_w²`). The **pricing-error variance `σ_s²`** (`s_t = p_t − m_t`) measures how far price strays from efficient value — only a *lower bound* is identified — and is a market-/execution-quality metric for transient microstructure noise (p.60).

## 5. Vectorized vs event-driven engines (pitfalls — see file 11)

**Two paradigms, deliberately contrasted** (Hilpisch, p.81, 175; Halls-Moore, *Successful Algo*, p.22–23):
- **Vectorized** (whole-dataset NumPy/pandas array ops) — concise, fast, ideal for a quick first test. Core idiom: `data['strategy'] = data['position'].shift(1) * data['returns']`, then `.cumsum().apply(np.exp)`; the **`.shift(1)` is what avoids look-ahead** on the signal (Hilpisch, p.92).
- **Event-driven** (explicit loop, one bar = one "event", OOP state) — realistic, **reuses code for live trading**, avoids look-ahead by *drip-feeding* data; slower and more bug-prone (Halls-Moore, p.130).

**Vectorized limitations (why you also need event-driven):** (1) look-ahead risk because it operates on the full set; (2) can't model fixed per-trade costs or share indivisibility (it's relative-return-based); (3) non-recursive — can't track path-dependent state (running P&L, stop-loss, max-so-far) (Hilpisch, p.175). **Event-driven > vectorized** precisely because vectorized code "ignores transaction costs and the nitty-gritty" → live underperformance (Halls-Moore, *Advanced Algo*, p.351–352). **Always research-test first, then promote to event-driven.** Use the **same program/model for backtest and live** to guarantee identical logic (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.1). The decisive practical argument: event-driven code can be **"lifted out" for live execution**, minimizing translation error — design as cooperating agents (point-in-time/unadjusted data-feed; back-office for tax lots, hurdles, high-water marks, time-varying borrow; PM; and a **broker module** that models market impact / partial fills and *flips a switch* to go live) (QuantAssetMgmt ch.14). HFT confirms the pattern: the back-test and paper engines **share core code and differ only in the quote-source module** (Aldridge, Ch 7, 16).

**Use Look-Inside-Bar / Bar-Magnifier** whenever a stop *and* a target could both be hit within one bar — otherwise results are simply wrong (Davey, p.140, 181). Build BBO from the ITCH stream for true limit-fill simulation; market-order intraday strategies need ≥ level-2 (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.178–181). **Simulate execution conservatively:** market orders fill at the best quote *minus estimated market impact*; limit orders count as filled **only when price strictly *crosses* (not merely touches)** — naive best-quote fills overstate performance (Aldridge, Ch 16; HFTAldridge **capacity** = market impact × probability-of-execution × explicit costs, and **alpha decay** = erosion from non-execution of limit orders or latency on market orders). *See **11 — Backtesting engines** for full engine architecture, order-book reconstruction, and data-feed requirements.*

## 6. Practical checklist before trusting a backtest

Kaufman's robustness checklist (p.1862, 1906): (1) sound premise; (2) **self-adapting** (volatility-scaled stops/sizing, no fixed $); (3) **fewest rules** — best systems have **≤4 variables** (Futures Truth); (4) tested on the **most data and markets**. Add:

- [ ] **Sound economic premise stated *first*** so the prior odds θ are high — precision depends on θ, and a 1-in-100 prior makes a p=0.05 "winner" 86% likely false (López de Prado *MLAM* p.106; Grinold & Kahn "intuition" p.335).
- [ ] **Number of trials reported**, and the observed Sharpe compared against the **expected-maximum-Sharpe-under-the-null hurdle** `E[max_k SR̂ₖ]`; apply the **Deflated Sharpe Ratio (require DSR > 0.95)** or a Šidák FWER using the **ONC-clustered effective trial count** (López de Prado *AFML* Ch 14.7 / *MLAM* p.108–118; Jansen p.357).
- [ ] **Costs & slippage modelled** realistically (commission + half-spread + impact), deducted only on weight changes — and the strategy still survives 1 bp stress; do **not** pad costs "to be safe" (Chan, p.23, 60–65; Kaufman p.1857). Include **taxes / after-tax return** for taxable accounts (QEPM Ch 16.8).
- [ ] **Survivorship-bias-free** universe incl. delisted names *and* historical index membership, criteria applied as-of-date — for equities, hold a name only if it was *in the index that day* and merge in **delisting returns** (Chan, *Machine Trading*, p.24; Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*, ch.3; Gray & Carlisle, *Quantitative Value*, ch.10; QEPM Ch 16.5).
- [ ] **Point-in-time data**, split- *and* dividend-adjusted; signals at the close, trades at the **next open**; auction (not SIP) prices for fills; fundamentals lagged to the **report date, not the quarter earned** (~6-mo lag for fundamentals; as-first-reported data — restated financials once overstated a P/E strategy by 28%); benchmark momentum against the **Total Return** index, not the price index (Jansen p.356–358; Chan p.182–183; Gray & Carlisle ch.10; Clenow ch.3; QEPM Ch 16.5).
- [ ] **For ML pipelines, respect the "red zone":** the training window spans Δs+Δl and **stops at t−Δl** — no feature dated in (t−Δl, t]; plain k-fold is proscribed, so use purged-CV (only if the cross-section is time-stationary) or walk-forward retraining, tuning hyperparameters once on an early sub-sample (Coqueret & Guida, ch.10.4, 12.1, 12.4.1).
- [ ] **No-skill baseline removed:** detrend returns (or subtract a same-position-bias placebo rule) so position bias × market trend isn't mistaken for skill; compare to a **random-signal benchmark** beaten by a margin too large to be chance (Aronson p.22–28).
- [ ] **Look-ahead audit:** truncate last N days → positions over the overlap unchanged (Chan, p.59).
- [ ] **Final OOS untouched** and used **once**; if you tune window lengths, **nest a second walk-forward** and validate once on untouched recent years (Grimes p.181; Davey2 ch.13).
- [ ] **Walk-forward passes:** anchored/rolling per market, ≥10 WF runs, **WFE ≥ 50% (ideally > 100%)**, a *consistent* stitched OOS equity curve, ≥30 trades/parameter in-period, and remember **max-drawdown is not additive** across windows (Tomasini p.21–23, 150–155; Davey2 ch.13; Pardo).
- [ ] **Degrees of freedom ≥ 90% remaining** (data-used-by-a-rule ×10 ≤ window) (Tomasini/Pardo p.16–18).
- [ ] **Sensitivity passes:** smooth surface, **robust plateau** with the chosen params in the *middle* of the stable region, profitable on the *average* of neighbouring tests, worst-neighbour drawdown for risk (Kaufman p.1853–57; Tomasini p.23–26, 49–53; Covel ±variants).
- [ ] **Monte Carlo passes:** trade-order shuffle for worst-case DD by confidence level (Tomasini p.101–109), **and** Davey's monkey test — beat **~90% of 8,000 trade-count-matched random systems on net profit *and* max drawdown**, with a plan to **re-run it live every 6–12 months and stop when the monkeys catch up** (Davey2 ch.12).
- [ ] **Enough trades** — Tomasini's **≥100-trade** rule (±~10% on metrics), target ≳400 for ~5% error, never trust <~50 (Tomasini p.18; Kaufman p.125; Davey p.114). For short history, **pool across instruments** rather than fitting each separately (Carver ch.3).
- [ ] **Kurtosis check** (>6–8 ⇒ overfit) and **price-shock audit** (>5×-ATR days not driving the P&L) (Kaufman p.150, 1895).
- [ ] **Works on sibling instruments** (an ES edge should hold on NQ/YM/DAX — built-in OOS) and on **recent** data (Kaufman p.1859; Chan p.91).
- [ ] **Theory-first, low-parameter:** the strategy rests on a *prior* economic premise and minimal parameters, with scalars/weights/vol estimated on **backward-looking** windows (no in-sample constants), and judged on the **alpha that survives a long-only-benchmark regression**, not raw return; expect positive **skew** to appear only at the holding-period frequency (Carver, *Advanced Futures*, p.124, 176–177, 183, 207–209).
- [ ] **EW-universe sniff test:** benchmark a long-only strategy against an equal-weighted portfolio of the whole universe — a TC-adjusted Sharpe **> 2× the benchmark suggests a bug**, and **> ~5% excess/yr (same vol) is dubious** (~150 bps is already excellent) (Coqueret & Guida, ch.12.4.3).
- [ ] **No-skill *structural* baseline subtracted:** confirm the edge survives beyond what equal-*risk* weighting alone delivers (a random 50-stock risk-parity portfolio already beats the cap-weighted index), and benchmark the hit ratio against the **unconditional positive-return share**, not 0.5 (Clenow, *Stocks on the Move*, ch.15; Coqueret & Guida, ch.10.1.2).
- [ ] **Apply a pessimism factor** to the surviving Sharpe (single-period OOS ≈ 75%; max realistic SR ≈ 1.0 diversified; live Sharpe ≈ ½ backtest) and **expect Sortino > Sharpe** to better judge positively-skewed systems (Carver Table 14; Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*, ch.1; Coqueret & Guida ch.12.4.2; Covel).
- [ ] **Paper-trade / incubate** before live (reconcile daily against the backtest — they should match), then start **tiny**; expect the backtest's drawdown and pre-commit to tolerance (Chan p.55; Aldridge Ch 16; Davey2; Halls-Moore p.17–18).

## To validate empirically / Common failure modes
- **The single most expensive bug is silent look-ahead** (full-sample regression coefficients, period High/Low used intra-period, fundamentals stamped to the quarter earned). Build the truncate-and-compare test into CI for every strategy.
- **Backtest overfitting is *the* problem, and it has no clean fix** in low-signal financial data — it's worse than web-scale ML, and *test-set* overfitting (selection across trials) is precisely what a backtest cannot defend against. Treat **"most discoveries in finance are false"** as the prior: report the number of trials, apply a **Deflated/Probabilistic Sharpe (>0.95)** against the expected-max-Sharpe-under-the-null hurdle, cluster trials with ONC for the *effective* count, and treat staged paper/live as the real test. The arithmetic is brutal — 1,000 zero-edge trials at V=1 manufacture an expected max Sharpe of **3.26**, 20 informationless rules give a **64%** chance of a |t|>2, and **6,402 TA rules** showed *no* real edge after correction (López de Prado; Grinold & Kahn; Aronson). Practitioner sniff-tests agree: **live Sharpe ≈ backtest Sharpe / 2** and a long-only TC-adjusted Sharpe **> 2× an equal-weighted-universe benchmark is probably a bug** (Coqueret & Guida; Chan, *Algorithmic Trading*); **expect ~1 in 5 random strategies to look significant** (Gray & Carlisle).
- **ML *enlarges* the overfitting surface, it does not shrink it (cross-ref 05).** Every extra model knob and every hyperparameter-search pass is another trial that must enter the DSR trial count; **plain k-fold CV is proscribed** on a time-stamped factor panel (trains on the future, tests on the past); the **"red zone"** training-window rule (stop at t−Δl) is the most common ML backtest bug; and metrics must map to *investment value* — judge OOS R² **against a zero forecast**, hit ratio **against the unconditional positive-return share** (not 0.5), and prefer the MSE **cross-term** / precision-recall-F1 over raw MSE or accuracy (Coqueret & Guida ch.10, 12).
- **Costs are not a footnote** — a gross-Sharpe-3 strategy can be net-negative after 1 bp, and $30/round-turn cut a real example's avg trade from $35 to $5. Never trust a cost-free equity curve, and never pad costs to "be safe" (it kills real fast edges).
- **No-skill baselines masquerade as alpha:** a long-biased rule in a rising market earns positive returns with zero skill (detrend returns or subtract a same-bias placebo), and a *profitable backtest alone proves nothing* — believing it does is affirming the consequent (Aronson).
- **Regime dependence masquerades as alpha:** a benign sample understates downside, price shocks fake trend profits, and falling discount rates inflated past returns. Demand performance on recent data and across regimes; audit ≥5×-ATR days; take limited-test slices at random rather than from a crisis window.
- **Fewer trades than ~50 (Tomasini wants ≥100), kurtosis above ~7, a spiky parameter surface, a profit factor >3, or a walk-forward efficiency below ~50%** are each standalone red flags — any one should stop you trading the configuration.
- **Walk-forward / sensitivity discipline:** pick the *middle* of a robust plateau (not the lonely peak), keep ≥90% degrees of freedom, run ≥10 WF passes, and remember max-drawdown is **not additive** across stitched windows.
- **Monte Carlo is necessary but not sufficient:** the trade-order shuffle gives worst-case drawdown by confidence level and Davey's monkey test guards against fake edges, but **neither can detect overfitting** and both assume Gaussian-ish returns — pair them with DSR and OOS.
- **Empirically confirm** that walk-forward / purged-CV (ideally CPCV) OOS results, paper-trading (reconciled daily, should match exactly), and the first live block agree within the error bars implied by 1/√N — and within the relevant **pessimism factor** — before scaling capital.
